What kind of firm Pictet is@@p1-what@@

Pictet is a Geneva-based group that does four related jobs for other people's money: it advises wealthy families, it runs investment funds, it invests in private assets, and it holds and administers assets for other financial firms [S491] CONFIRMED. Key terms
  • Private bank: looks after the whole financial life of a wealthy family: advice, portfolios, safekeeping, succession. Pictet Wealth Management is one [S491] CONFIRMED.
  • Asset manager: paid a fee to invest clients' money through pooled funds (many investors, one portfolio) or mandates (one client, one portfolio). Pictet Asset Management, where you interned, is one [S491] CONFIRMED.
  • Custody: safekeeping and administering assets that belong to someone else (settling trades, collecting dividends, keeping records). Pictet Asset Services does this [S491] CONFIRMED.

Traditional versus alternative: where Pictet sits and where KKR sits

Investment firms split roughly into two families. Traditional managers buy shares and bonds that trade on public markets, so their funds can be priced and dealt every day. Alternative managers buy things that do not trade on an exchange: whole private companies, infrastructure, property, private loans. Their funds lock money up for years because the assets cannot be sold quickly INFERRED. KKR says its founders launched "what we now know as the alternatives industry" when they started the firm on 1 May 1976 [S13] CONFIRMED. KKR managed $796.5bn at 30 June 2026 [S1] CONFIRMED, and 93% of that was perpetual capital or capital committed for 8+ years at inception [S1] CONFIRMED.

Pictet is mostly traditional, with a growing alternatives arm. Its group total of CHF 810bn (assets under management or custody, 30 June 2026) converts to about USD 1,004bn on Pictet's own site [S493] REPORTED. That looks bigger than KKR, but it is not like for like: Pictet's number includes custody assets it only holds, and listed assets it can sell daily INFERRED.

The partnership model: who owns Pictet and how it decides@@p1-partners@@

Most large financial firms are companies owned by shareholders who can buy and sell their shares on a stock exchange. Pictet is not. Its legal form is a "société en commandite par actions, a partnership limited by shares" [S491] CONFIRMED, abbreviated SCA. The top holding entity is Pictet & Cie Group SCA, and its "significant equity owners are the Managing Partners" [S490] CONFIRMED.

Key term

SCA (partnership limited by shares): a company form with two kinds of owner. Managing partners run the firm and carry the ultimate responsibility; other shareholders own shares but do not manage. Pictet chose it to keep partner control while ending unlimited personal liability [S497] CONFIRMED. Unlimited liability means a partner's own house and savings could be taken to pay the firm's debts. Until 2014 that was true for Pictet's partners [S497] CONFIRMED.

The change was announced on 6 February 2013, the same day as fellow Geneva bank Lombard Odier, and took effect on 1 January 2014. The Swiss bank, Banque Pictet & Cie SA, became a limited company and the group moved under an SCA. The new structure required a consolidated annual report and an independent supervisory board [S497] CONFIRMED. In plain words: Pictet kept partner ownership but started publishing group accounts and accepting outside oversight INFERRED.

Key term

UCITS: the EU rulebook for funds sold to the general public. 2. Client onboarding, start to finish@@p2-onboard@@

Onboarding is everything that happens between "this client wants to invest" and "this client's first order is accepted". Step 1. Client categorisation: retail, professional or eligible counterparty

UK rules sort every client into one of three boxes. The rulebook is the FCA Handbook's Conduct of Business Sourcebook, chapter 3 (COBS 3), which copies the EU's MiFID II categories [S543] CONFIRMED. The box decides how much protection the client gets, which share classes and documents they can access, and which rules the firm must follow when dealing with them INFERRED.

  • Retail client: the default. Everyone who is not shown to be something else. Maximum protection: full disclosure documents, suitability or appropriateness checks, Consumer Duty INFERRED.
  • Professional client, "per se": automatically professional because of what they are. COBS 3.5 lists credit institutions, investment firms, insurers, collective investment schemes, pension funds and large undertakings among others [S543] CONFIRMED.
  • Professional client, "elective": a retail client who asks to be treated as professional and passes the tests. The firm must run a qualitative test (does the client have the expertise, experience and knowledge?) and, for MiFID business, the client must meet two of three quantitative tests: about 10 significant trades per quarter over the previous four quarters; a portfolio over EUR 500,000; or at least one year working in the financial sector in a relevant role [S543] CONFIRMED.
  • Eligible counterparty (ECP): the top tier, for certain financial institutions in certain kinds of business such as order execution. Fewest protections, because both sides are professionals (from the COBS structure in S543) INFERRED.
Why categorisation comes first

Everything downstream depends on the box. A retail client must get a retail disclosure document and can only buy share classes open to retail. 3. Client servicing: dealing, reporting, queries and fund events@@p2-service@@

Once a client is onboarded, the client group's job becomes keeping them informed, keeping their transactions clean, and answering whatever they ask. This section explains the machinery underneath: how a UCITS deals every day, why nobody knows the price when they place an order, what goes in a factsheet, how a question travels through the firm, and the two UK value tests that shape what clients ask.

How a daily-dealing fund works

A UCITS like Pictet-Water calculates a NAV on each Valuation Day (normally each business day). Investors can subscribe (buy new shares, so the fund grows) or redeem (sell shares back to the fund, so it shrinks). There is no stock exchange in the middle: the fund itself creates and cancels shares at NAV (standard mechanism) INFERRED.

Three rules govern every order:

  1. Cut-off time. Orders must reach the transfer agent before a fixed time. A July 2018 Pictet notice to shareholders moved many compartments to a 1:00 pm Luxembourg time cut-off on the Valuation Day, and Pictet - Global Megatrend Selection to 11:00 am. Hong Kong distributors had a separate 5:00 pm Hong Kong time deadline through Bank Pictet & Cie (Asia) [S527] (dated 2018) CONFIRMED. Current cut-offs are in today's prospectus, which was not read [Gap].
  2. Forward pricing. An order placed before the cut-off is dealt at the NAV calculated after the cut-off, using that day's closing prices. You never know the exact price when you place the order [S527] (the mechanism is INFERRED). This stops anyone trading on yesterday's stale price after news breaks INFERRED.
  3. Settlement. The cash moves a set number of days later. The 2018 notice moved settlement for some compartments to "within 2 week days after the Valuation Day" [S527] (dated 2018) CONFIRMED, usually written T+2.

Consumer Duty fair value and the Assessment of Value

Two UK tests decide whether a fund is "worth the money". Clients and distributors ask about both, so the client group needs to know which applies.

Consumer Duty. The FCA's consumer principle reads: "A firm must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers" [S542] CONFIRMED. Assessment of Value (AoV). The FCA found in August 2023 that reports had "significantly improved", but that some independent directors "did not provide sufficient challenge" [S541] CONFIRMED. 2. The product bridge: three clocks for the same money@@p3-product@@

A product, technically, is a set of rules about cash: when it goes in, at what price, what it costs each year, and when and how it comes out. Every client conversation you had at Pictet sat on top of one set of rules, the UCITS rules. KCS sits on top of two other sets: the drawdown rules and the evergreen rules. The next figure shows the three as clocks that tick at different speeds.

Key terms

UCITS: the EU's regulated open-ended fund format for the general public; Pictet's Luxembourg funds are UCITS [S524] CONFIRMED. NAV (net asset value): the fund's assets minus its debts, divided by the number of shares; the price you deal at. Forward pricing: you place an order now and deal at the next NAV, which nobody knows yet [S527] INFERRED. Drawdown fund: investors promise ("commit") money now; the manager asks for it ("calls" it) over several years as it finds deals INFERRED. Evergreen fund: no end date; investors subscribe periodically at NAV and can ask for limited repurchases [S70] CONFIRMED.

Forward pricing at Pictet, and its cousin at K-PEC

At Pictet you learned that a client never buys "2,500 shares at today's price". The client sends an amount of money before the cut-off, and the transfer agent (the firm that keeps the fund's register of shareholders) works out the share count after the next NAV is struck. Miss the cut-off by five minutes and the order rolls to the next Valuation Day [S527] CONFIRMED (cut-off rule; the mechanics are INFERRED). K-PEC works on the same principle with a monthly clock: "An investor subscribes a dollar amount and only learns the share count afterwards", and a subscription accepted on 1 September is priced at the 31 August Transactional NAV [S420] CONFIRMED. The figure below puts the two side by side.

Liquidity tools: from suspension to the 5% cap

A UCITS promises daily dealing, so its main emergency tool is a suspension. Pictet's board suspended NAV calculation and dealing in Pictet - Russian Equities in February 2022 [S527] REPORTED. K-PEC builds the brake into normal life. Repurchases are quarterly and capped at "no more than 5.0% of our aggregate NAV ... per calendar quarter"; the board can buy back less or nothing; shares redeemed within 24 months pay a 5.0% Early Repurchase Fee, kept by the fund for the remaining holders; if requests exceed 5%, the board may tender for the excess "at or about a 10% discount to the most recent NAV" [S420] CONFIRMED. The numbers, step by step

K-PEC Class I, EUR 1m (illustrative)

Management fee. 1.25% x EUR 1,000,000 = EUR 12,500 a year if NAV stays flat (charged monthly on month-end NAV) [S420].

Performance allocation, good year. Total return 10% = EUR 100,000. The investor first gets the 5% hurdle (EUR 50,000). Then KKR takes 100% ("catch-up") until it holds 15% of everything paid so far, then 15% of the rest. Because 10% clears the hurdle comfortably, KKR ends with 15% of the whole return: EUR 15,000; the investor keeps EUR 85,000.

Weak year. Return 5.5% = EUR 55,000. The investor gets 50,000; the next 5,000 all goes to KKR as catch-up, which is not complete (15% of 55,000 would be 8,250). So KKR gets EUR 5,000 INFERRED (arithmetic; high-water mark and loss carryforward ignored).

Early exit at month 18. 5% x 1,000,000 = EUR 50,000 kept by the fund [S420].

Same order in Class S. Up to 3.50% upfront (up to EUR 35,000) plus 0.85% a year (EUR 8,500) on top of the 1.25% [S420].

GII V-style commitment, EUR 1m (illustrative)

Calls. Year 1: 200k; Year 2: 250k; Year 3: 200k; Year 4: 150k; Year 5: 100k. Total called: 900k (90%); 100k never called.

Distributions. Years 4 to 11: 50k, 100k, 150k, 200k, 250k, 250k, 200k, 150k. Total: 1,350k, so 1.5x the cash called.

Net cash out (calls minus distributions, cumulative): 200k, 450k, 650k, 750k, 750k, 600k, 400k, 150k, then the client is net positive from Year 9.

The lesson. The client never has the full EUR 1m out at once: peak net exposure here is EUR 750k. That is why institutions "over-commit" and run pacing models [S454, S455] REPORTED, and why the KKR TPA paper notes private market distributions have historically averaged about "10-30% of NAV" a year [S436] CONFIRMED.

Fee. At an illustrative 1.0% (inside the 10-K's 0.75% to 1.50% range [S2]) on commitment: EUR 10,000 a year during the investment period.

How to use the glossary@@g-how@@

Terms are grouped by theme so you can learn them in clusters, not alphabetically. Bold words inside a definition are themselves defined somewhere on these pages. KKR-specific names carry a source marker; general finance definitions are standard usage.

1. The basic cast

TermPlain-English meaning
Alternative asset managerA firm that invests other people's money in things outside listed shares and bonds: private companies, loans, buildings, infrastructure. KKR, Blackstone and Apollo are the largest.
Traditional asset managerA firm that mainly buys listed shares and bonds, often cheaply in index funds (BlackRock, Vanguard). Lower fees, daily liquidity.
General partner (GP)The manager that runs a private fund, picks the investments and earns fees and carried interest. KKR is the GP of its funds.
Limited partner (LP)An investor in a private fund: a pension, insurer, sovereign wealth fund, endowment, family office or wealthy individual. "Limited" because they can lose only what they put in and do not run the fund.
Client / investorKKR's own word for LPs and other buyers of its products. KCS calls them clients.
Portfolio companyA company a private equity fund owns.
SponsorA private equity firm in its role as owner and backer of a deal. "Sponsor-backed" means owned by a PE firm.
Buy side / sell sideBuy side invests money (KKR, pensions). Sell side sells services to investors (investment banks, brokers).
Family officeA private company that manages one wealthy family's money. KKR calls this channel Family Capital [S84].
Sovereign wealth fund (SWF)A state-owned investment fund, for example in Norway, Abu Dhabi, Saudi Arabia or Singapore.
EndowmentThe investment pot of a university or charity, spent slowly over very long horizons.
Defined benefit (DB) pensionA pension that promises a set income in retirement. The scheme carries the investment risk, so it invests to meet future payments (liabilities).
Defined contribution (DC) pensionA pension where each saver has a pot and carries the investment risk themselves. Workplace DC pots are the target of the UK Mansion House reforms.
Private wealthWealthy individuals investing through private banks, wealth managers and financial advisers. Smaller cheques, many more investors, stricter consumer rules.
WirehouseA large US brokerage with thousands of financial advisers (for example Morgan Stanley, Merrill). A key gateway to private wealth.
Insurer / annuityAn insurer collects premiums and invests them to pay future claims. An annuity pays a retiree a fixed income for life. Long-dated promises suit long-dated private assets.

2. How a private fund works

TermPlain-English meaning
Closed-end / drawdown fundA fund with a fixed size and life (often 10-12 years). Investors promise money up front and hand it over only when the GP asks.
CommitmentThe amount an LP promises to a drawdown fund. Not paid on day one.
Capital call (drawdown)The GP's request for part of the commitment, usually with 10 business days' notice, when it needs cash for a deal.
Dry powderMoney committed to funds but not yet invested.
First close / final closeThe first date the fund can start investing with commitments so far, and the last date new investors can join.
Hard capThe maximum size the GP has promised the fund will reach.
Investment periodUsually the first 4-6 years, when the fund buys new assets.
Harvest periodThe later years, when the fund sells assets and returns cash.
DistributionCash paid back to LPs after a sale or from income.
ExitSelling an investment: to another company (trade sale), to another PE firm (secondary buyout), or by listing it (IPO).
Flagship fundA firm's main, numbered fund in a strategy, e.g. KKR North America Fund XIV, European Fund VI, Global Infrastructure Investors V [S2].
Vintage yearThe year a fund starts investing. Used to compare funds fairly.
J-curveThe shape of an LP's returns over time: negative early (fees, no sales yet), positive later as investments are sold.
Re-upAn existing LP committing again to the next fund in the series. A core KCS success measure.
Evergreen / semi-liquid fundA fund with no end date that takes new money continuously and lets investors redeem, usually monthly or quarterly, up to a limit. The main product for private wealth. KKR's are the K-Series.
NAV (net asset value)The value of a fund's assets minus its debts. Evergreen funds issue and redeem units at NAV.
Redemption gateA cap on how much investors can withdraw in a period (often 5% of NAV a quarter). Protects remaining investors from fire sales.
Interval fundA US registered fund that offers to buy back a set percentage of shares at set intervals. The Capital Group KKR funds use this structure [S92].
BDC (business development company)A US vehicle that lends to mid-sized companies and can be sold to retail investors. K-FIT is one [S113].
Non-traded REITA real estate investment trust that is not listed on an exchange. Sold to wealth clients; KREST is KKR's [S114].
Feeder fundA vehicle that pools many small investors and invests as one LP into the main fund. Platforms like iCapital run them.
Separately managed account (SMA)A portfolio run for one large client under its own rules, instead of a pooled fund. One meaning of "customised products".
Co-investmentAn LP investing directly in a specific deal alongside the fund, usually with low or no fees. Highly valued by LPs; at KKR, syndicated with KCM [S70].
SyndicationSelling pieces of a deal or a loan the firm has committed to, to other investors.
SecondariesBuying or selling existing fund stakes or assets from other investors, often at a discount, to give early liquidity.
Continuation vehicle (CV)A new fund a GP sets up to buy one or more assets from its own older fund, so existing LPs can cash out or roll over.
Strategic partnershipA large multi-strategy mandate with one client across several KKR products, often with fee terms and co-investment rights.

3. Fees and returns

TermPlain-English meaning
Management feeAn annual fee on committed or invested capital (traditionally about 1.5-2% for buyout funds). Pays for the team whatever happens.
Carried interest (carry)The GP's share of profits (traditionally 20%) once LPs have their money back plus the hurdle.
Hurdle / preferred returnThe minimum annual return LPs get before carry starts, often 8%.
Catch-upAfter the hurdle, the GP takes a larger share for a while until it has "caught up" to its full 20%.
WaterfallThe order in which cash is split between LPs and GP: capital back, hurdle, catch-up, then 80/20.
ClawbackThe GP returns carry it was paid early if the fund later underperforms. KKR repaid $344m on Asian Fund II in Q4 2025 [S2].
GP commitmentThe GP's own money invested in its fund. "Skin in the game".
Side letterA private agreement giving one LP special terms (fees, reporting, co-invest rights).
MFN (most favoured nation)A clause letting an LP claim terms given to other similar-sized LPs.
Basis point (bp)One hundredth of a percent. 84bp = 0.84%.
IRR (internal rate of return)The annualised return that accounts for the timing of cash in and out. Sensitive to timing; can flatter quick wins.
MOIC / TVPIMultiple on invested capital / total value to paid-in: total value (cash returned plus value still held) divided by money put in. 2.0x = doubled.
DPI (distributions to paid-in)Cash actually returned divided by money paid in. The "real money" metric; the word of 2025-26 because exits have been slow.
RVPIResidual value to paid-in: the unsold part. TVPI = DPI + RVPI.
PME (public market equivalent)Compares a fund's return with what the same cash flows would have earned in a stock index.
Sharpe ratioReturn per unit of volatility. Higher means a smoother ride for the return earned.
Denominator effectWhen public markets fall, private holdings become a bigger share of an LP's portfolio, pushing it over its target and making it pause new commitments.

6. Asset classes and deal words

TermPlain-English meaning
Private equity (PE)Buying stakes in private companies, usually control, improving them and selling within years.
Leveraged buyout (LBO)Buying a company using a lot of borrowed money secured on the company itself. KKR's founding idea.
Growth equityMinority stakes in fast-growing companies that need capital to expand.
Impact investingInvesting for a financial return and a measurable social or environmental result. KKR Global Impact focuses on climate, learning, sustainable living and inclusive growth.
Private creditLoans made by non-bank funds directly to companies or backed by assets. The fastest-growing private asset class.
Direct lendingPrivate credit lent straight to mid-sized companies, mostly floating rate.
Asset-based finance (ABF)Lending secured on pools of assets (loans, equipment, receivables) rather than a company's cash flow.
InfrastructureLong-life physical assets providing essential services: power, data centres, fibre, transport, utilities.
Core / core-plus / value-add / opportunisticThe risk ladder for real assets, from stable income (core) to heavy repositioning (opportunistic).
Real estateBuildings and land: logistics, housing, offices, data centres. Earns rent and value growth.
ECM / DCMEquity capital markets (share issues, IPOs) and debt capital markets (bonds, loans).
Due diligenceThe deep investigation before an investment: financial, commercial, legal, operational.
Value creationHow an owner makes a company worth more: growth, margins, better capital structure, bolt-on acquisitions.
Investment theme / thesisA view about a long trend (say, power demand from data centres) used to find deals.

FCAFinancial Conduct Authority, the UK conduct regulator. KKR's UK LLP is FCA-authorised (FRN 593180) [S23].
