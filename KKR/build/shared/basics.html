1. Investing, and what an "alternative" asset manager is

Investing means handing over money today to get more back later. The person or institution with the money (a pension fund, an insurer, a wealthy family) usually does not pick the investments itself. It hires an asset manager and pays it a fee. The asset manager's product is a fund: a pot of money from many investors, run under one set of rules.

Key term

Traditional investments are listed shares (stocks), government and company bonds, and cash. You can buy and sell them every trading day at a public price [Inferred]. Alternative investments are everything else that big investors buy: private equity, private credit, infrastructure, real estate, hedge funds and natural resources [S269] [Reported]. Their defining feature is that they are illiquid: you cannot sell them quickly at a known price. In return investors hope for an illiquidity premium, a higher return than public markets [Inferred].

Five kinds of firm get confused all the time. Here is the difference in one line each [Inferred, standard definitions]:

  • Traditional asset manager (BlackRock, Vanguard): mostly index funds and listed share and bond funds. Very low fees, huge scale, daily liquidity.
  • Hedge fund: trades mostly liquid securities, often with monthly or quarterly liquidity, and aims for returns that do not move with the market.
  • Investment bank: advises on deals (mergers, listings) and underwrites securities. Paid per transaction, not a long-term management fee. Banks are counterparties and lenders to the firms above.
  • Private equity firm: buys whole companies that are not on the stock market, improves them and sells them, using funds that last about ten years.
  • Alternative asset manager (KKR, Blackstone, Apollo, Carlyle, Ares, Brookfield, EQT): a private equity firm that grew into many strategies. Runs long-lived private funds, earns management fees plus a share of profits, and increasingly owns insurance companies and runs products for individuals.

KKR is the clearest example of that growth. At 31 December 2010 traditional private equity was over 70% of KKR's assets under management. At 30 June 2026 it was "less than 25%" [S332] [Confirmed]. Today KKR's $796bn splits into Credit and Liquid Strategies $331bn, Real Assets $211bn and Private Equity $255bn [S332] [Confirmed].

Say this in the interview

"KKR started as a buyout firm but is now mostly a credit, infrastructure and insurance business. Private equity is less than a quarter of its assets. So KCS is not selling one product, it is selling a platform."

2. The GP / LP structure: who gives what, who gets what

A private fund is usually set up as a limited partnership, a legal form with two kinds of partner [Inferred].

Key term

General partner (GP): the manager. KKR, Blackstone, Apollo and Carlyle act as GPs. The GP picks the investments, runs them and decides when to sell [Inferred]. It also puts in some of its own money, the GP commitment, so it has "skin in the game" [Inferred].

Key term

Limited partners (LPs): the investors who supply most of the money. "Limited" because they cannot lose more than they put in and they do not run the fund [Inferred]. Pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurers, endowments, family offices and wealthy individuals.

The contract between them is the limited partnership agreement (LPA). Individual LPs often negotiate extra terms in a side letter, for example a fee discount or extra reporting [Inferred]. The LPs' trade body, ILPA (Institutional Limited Partners Association), lists three guiding principles: alignment of interest, governance and transparency [S317] [Confirmed]. Keep those three words in your head: they are what every LP is checking when it meets KCS.

3. The life of a closed-end (drawdown) fund

Most KKR flagship funds are closed-end: investors join only during fundraising, then the door shuts and the fund runs for a fixed life. They are also called drawdown funds, because the GP "draws down" committed money in stages. Think of it as a ten-year journey in four stages [Inferred, standard model]:

  1. Fundraising and commitment. The GP markets the fund. LPs sign a commitment to give, say, $100m when asked. No cash moves yet. The fund holds a first close (the first date it accepts commitments and can start investing) and later a final close (the last day it accepts new investors) [Inferred].
  2. Investment period (roughly years 1 to 5). When the GP finds a deal it sends a capital call (also called a drawdown) asking each LP for its share. LPs must pay within days. Money promised but not yet called is dry powder or unfunded commitments [Inferred].
  3. Value creation and harvest (roughly years 4 to 10). The GP improves the companies, then sells them in an exit: a sale to another company, a sale to another fund, or a stock market listing (IPO) [Inferred].
  4. Distribution. Cash from exits goes back to LPs. The fund usually has a 10-year life, often with 1 to 2 years of extensions [Inferred].

A real example: in April 2026 KKR held the final close of North America Fund XIV at about $23bn, which KKR calls the largest private equity fund focused only on North America [S335] [Confirmed]. Its LPs included public and private pension plans, sovereign wealth funds, insurers, endowments and foundations, private wealth platforms, family offices and wealthy individuals [S335] [Reported]. That list is the KCS client map.

The J-curve: why the early years look bad

Plot an LP's cumulative net cash flow (all cash received minus all cash paid in) over the fund's life. In the early years the LP pays capital calls and fees but gets little back, so the line falls. Once exits start, it climbs. The shape looks like a letter J [Inferred]. The bottom of the J is where LPs feel the most strain, and it is why slow exits in 2022 to 2026 hurt so much.

Why this matters

Exits have been slow, so the J has been stuck near the bottom for many funds. PitchBook counts almost $5tn sitting in funds that are seven years old or older, 39% of total NAV, as of December 2025 [S260] [Reported]. Bain counts about 32,000 unsold portfolio companies worth about $3.8tn [S262, S326] [Confirmed]. LPs with no cash coming back have less to commit to the next fund. That is the KCS problem in one sentence.

4. Fees and the waterfall

The industry shorthand is "2 and 20". The real terms vary by fund, and large LPs often negotiate lower ones [Inferred, standard definitions].

Key terms

Management fee (the "2"): about 1.5% to 2% a year of committed capital during the investment period, often stepping down later to invested capital. Pays salaries and running costs [Inferred].

Carried interest or "carry" (the "20"): the GP's share of profits, typically 20% [Inferred].

Hurdle rate or preferred return: the minimum return LPs must earn before the GP gets any carry, commonly 8% a year [Inferred].

Catch-up: once the hurdle is cleared, the GP takes a large share of the next profits until it has "caught up" to 20% of total profit [Inferred].

Waterfall: the order in which cash is paid out. A European (whole-of-fund) waterfall pays carry only after LPs get all their money back plus the hurdle across the whole fund. An American (deal-by-deal) waterfall can pay carry deal by deal, which is better for the GP [Inferred]. A clawback makes the GP repay carry if later losses mean it was overpaid; ILPA's Principles 2.0 (2011) added a clawback appendix [S317] [Confirmed].

Worked example you can do on paper

All numbers are illustrative. A fund has $100m paid in by LPs. After exactly five years every investment has been sold for $200m in total. The terms: 8% preferred return (simple, not compounded, to keep the maths easy; real funds usually compound), 100% GP catch-up, 20% carry, European waterfall. Ignore fees inside the example.

Now add the management fee. At 2% a year on $100m of commitments for a five-year investment period, the GP would collect $10m of fees before any carry (illustrative). The fee is paid whether or not the fund makes money. Carry is paid only if it does. That is why LPs like a large GP commitment: if the GP has its own money in the fund, it suffers losses alongside them.

Don't say this

"KKR makes its money from carried interest." Carry matters, but the steady engine is fees. KKR's Fee Related Earnings were $1.2bn in Q2 2026 alone [S330] [Confirmed], and its fee-paying AUM was $638bn [S330] [Confirmed]. Say: "Fees pay for the platform; carry rewards performance."

5. Performance metrics: IRR, MOIC, TVPI, DPI, RVPI, PME

LPs judge a GP by its numbers. Six abbreviations do most of the work [Inferred, standard definitions]:

The one equation to remember is TVPI = DPI + RVPI. NAV (net asset value) is the GP's own estimate of what the unsold holdings are worth, updated quarterly.

Mini-example (illustrative numbers)

An LP has paid in $50m. By year 6 it has received $30m in cash and the holdings left are valued at $55m. DPI = 30 / 50 = 0.6x. RVPI = 55 / 50 = 1.1x. TVPI = 0.6 + 1.1 = 1.7x. The fund looks good on paper, but only 0.6x is cash in hand.

Why IRR is not enough. Fund A doubles money (2.0x) in 3 years: IRR is about 26% a year, since 2 to the power of one-third is about 1.26. Fund B doubles money in 8 years: IRR is about 9%, since 2 to the power of one-eighth is about 1.09. Same multiple, very different experience. Always quote a multiple and an IRR together.

Why DPI is the word of 2025-26

If exits are slow, a fund can show a fine TVPI while sending back little cash. LPs need cash to pay pensions and to fund new commitments, so they now ask about DPI first [Inferred]. The evidence:

  • Bain: distributions as a share of NAV were about 14% in 2025, and below 15% for four years running [S262] [Confirmed].
  • McKinsey's 2026 report (via a Dakota summary): distributions fell to 6% of AUM against a 2015 to 2019 average of 16% [S261] [Reported].
  • Bain cites an ILPA survey in which 53% of LPs said undrawn commitments limit new private equity commitments, up 15 percentage points [S262] [Confirmed].
Source uncertain

Bain's 2025 report described 2024 distributions as the lowest rate in over a decade (one reprint says 11%), which does not square neatly with "essentially flat at 14%" in the 2026 report [S271] [Reported]. Quote the provider with the number, and say "about".

KKR leans into this. Its co-CEOs called Q2 2026 KKR's "strongest monetization quarter ever" (monetisation means selling or part-selling investments and returning cash) [S330] [Confirmed]. 7. Who the LPs are, and what each one needs

KCS's clients are LPs. Each type has different liabilities (what it owes in future), a different horizon (how long it can wait), different liquidity needs (how fast it may need cash back) and a different ticket size (how much it commits per fund). The descriptions below are the standard picture [Inferred]; the research found no 2026 breakdown of how much each type holds.

Key term

Defined benefit (DB) pension: the employer promises a set income in retirement (say, a share of final salary), so the fund has fixed long-term liabilities and the employer carries the investment risk. Defined contribution (DC) pension: the employer and worker pay into a personal pot; the retirement income depends on how the pot performs, so the saver carries the risk. DC funds need daily pricing and switching, which makes illiquid funds harder to hold [Inferred]. UK change: 17 workplace pension providers, covering about 90% of active DC savers, pledged in the Mansion House Accord (13 May 2025) to put at least 10% of DC default funds in private markets by 2030, with at least 5% in the UK [S315] [Confirmed].

  • Pensions are historically the largest LP group [Inferred]. Many are now over-allocated: S&P Global found 174 of 298 pension funds analysed were over their private equity target in Q1 2025 [S324] [Reported]. In the UK, the Local Government Pension Scheme holds over £400bn across 86 funds and is consolidating into six pools [S347] [Reported]: fewer, bigger clients.
  • Insurers are growing fast as LPs because they want long-dated credit [Inferred]. KKR owns one: Global Atlantic had $220bn of AUM at 30 June 2026 [S330] [Confirmed].
  • Private wealth is the big gap. Bain estimates individuals hold about 50% of global AUM ($275tn to $295tn) but only 16% of AUM held by alternative funds [S293] [Reported]. PitchBook: "Growing inflows from retail investors are becoming the main source of new capital" [S260] [Confirmed].
Source uncertain

8. Evergreen (semi-liquid) funds versus drawdown funds

A wealthy individual cannot easily handle ten years of unpredictable capital calls. So managers built a second kind of vehicle.

Key term

Evergreen or semi-liquid fund: a fund with no fixed end date. Investors subscribe (buy in) monthly at the latest NAV per share, and can ask to redeem (cash out), usually quarterly, up to a cap, often 5% of the fund per quarter [S278] [Reported]. When requests exceed the cap, a gate applies and requests are paid pro rata (everyone gets the same fraction). Examples: non-traded BDCs (US private credit vehicles), non-traded REITs, ELTIFs in Europe, LTAFs in the UK [Inferred].

Why this is the private wealth channel's vehicle

  • Size: semi-liquid evergreen vehicles hold about $500bn of industry AUM (Evercore) [S267] [Reported]. US evergreen PE AUM nearly doubled from $51.3bn (end-2024) to $99.3bn (Q1 2026), which "funnels more capital into the hands of the largest GPs with established wealth products" (PitchBook) [S260] [Reported].
  • KKR's version is K-Series, its family of funds for wealthy individuals sold through banks and advisers. K-Series AUM was $42bn at 30 June 2026, up from $25bn a year earlier [S330, S332] [Confirmed].
  • The stress test: in Q1 2026 wealthy investors asked to withdraw $20.8bn from private credit funds run by Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, Blue Owl, KKR and others; managers paid "just over half" of requests [S337] [Reported]. Blue Owl enforced a 5% quarterly cap on 2 April 2026 [S278] [Reported]. Blackstone's BCRED paid 100% of record requests of 7.9% of NAV [S278] [Reported]. KKR's K-FIT received requests for 6.3% of shares in Q1 and paid about 80% of them; in Q2 requests fell to 1.65% and were paid in full [S338] [Reported].
  • Europe and the UK have their own wrappers: 189 new ELTIFs since start-2024 and about €34bn of ELTIF assets at end-2025 [S302] [Reported]; the UK LTAF was opened to retail investors from 3 July 2023 [S305] [Confirmed].

9. Secondaries, continuation vehicles, co-investment, SMAs and the denominator effect

These five ideas all answer one LP question: "How do I get the exposure I want, on terms I like, without being stuck?"

Key term

LP-led secondary: an LP sells its stake in a fund to a specialist buyer, usually at a price near NAV, to get cash early [Inferred].

Key term

GP-led secondary / continuation vehicle (CV): the GP moves one or more prized assets from an old fund into a new vehicle. Old LPs can cash out or roll over; new investors come in [Inferred].

Bain found CV transaction value grew 62% in 2025, but CVs were still less than 10% of exit value [S262] [Confirmed]. Evercore calls secondaries' shift "from an episodic liquidity solution into a structural portfolio management channel" [S267] [Confirmed]. KKR bought Arctos Partners (closed 4 May 2026); its team now forms "KKR Solutions", which KKR says will house "a scaled multi-asset class secondaries business" [S330, S332] [Confirmed] [S355] [Reported].

Key term

Co-investment: an LP invests directly in one deal alongside the fund, usually with no or low fees [S260] [Confirmed]. It lowers the LP's average fee and lets the GP do a bigger deal. PitchBook tracks $37.5bn of co-investment fundraising in the 12 months to June 2026 [S260] [Confirmed].

Key term

Separately managed account (SMA): instead of joining a pooled fund, one large LP gets its own account run by the GP, with terms, strategy mix and reporting built for it [Inferred]. This is what "customized products" in the KCS job description points at [Inferred].

The denominator effect

Pension funds set a target allocation, for example "15% in private equity". When listed markets fall fast (as in 2022), the value of the listed shares drops at once, but private holdings are revalued only quarterly and more slowly. Private assets become a bigger share of the total even though nothing was bought. The fund may then be "over-allocated" and stop making new commitments [S323] [Reported].

This was not just a 2022 story. S&P Global found 174 of 298 pension funds analysed over-allocated to private equity in Q1 2025 [S324] [Reported], and PitchBook says institutional allocations are "bumping up against risk limits" [S260] [Reported]. Secondaries are one way out: an over-allocated LP can sell stakes to get back under target.

  • Fewer, bigger buyers. UK LGPS pools and £25bn+ DC megafunds want customised, fee-efficient access [S346, S347], which is the "customized products" line in the KCS description [Inferred].
  • Platform, not product. KCS "connect[s] the dots" from PE to infrastructure, real estate, credit, capital markets and insurance [S390] [Confirmed]. Knowing all six asset classes in this part is the entry ticket.
  • Trust is the currency. KKR's own line: "People do business with people they like and trust" [S384] [Confirmed]. Every number you give a client must be one you can source.
Say this in the interview

"Industry fundraising has fallen for several years, but capital is concentrating in large platforms: funds over $1bn took 78% of the money in the first half of 2026. LPs are short of distributions, so the conversation now starts with DPI and liquidity. That makes Client Solutions more important, not less: the team has to explain everything from a drawdown buyout fund to a semi-liquid credit fund, to a pension and to a wealth adviser, in their own language."

Don't say this

"Private equity is booming and everyone wants in." It is wrong on the data (buyout fundraising fell for a fourth straight year in 2025 [S262]), it ignores the 2026 redemption queues, and it tells the interviewer you have read headlines, not numbers. Also avoid quoting one total for "the industry" without naming the provider and scope.
