A. The founding insight: three people, a borrowed-money idea, and a firm built around it

KKR began as an idea that a big Wall Street bank refused to back. Almost everything about how KKR is built today (who owns it, how people are paid, how long client money is locked in) traces back to that idea and to the partnership of the three men who had it.

Bear Stearns and the "bootstrap" deals

Jerome Kohlberg Jr. ran corporate finance at the investment bank Bear, Stearns & Co. His two protégés were first cousins: Henry Kravis and George Roberts [S27, S28] [Confirmed]. From the mid 1960s the three did what they called "bootstrap" acquisitions. They bought mostly family-owned companies using a small slice of their own money (equity) and a lot of borrowed money (debt) [S27, S28] [Confirmed]. A reference history credits Kohlberg with first using the technique in 1965 [S27] [Reported].

Key term

Leveraged buyout (LBO): buying a company mainly with borrowed money, where the debt is repaid from the company's own cash flows. The buyer improves the business, pays down the debt, then sells. Because the buyer put in only a small amount of equity, a modest rise in the company's value can multiply that equity many times. The "bootstrap" deals were the first version of this [S27, S28] [Confirmed]. (Fees, carry and fund mechanics are in the basics section; this part only uses them.)

The early results were mixed, which is worth knowing. Roberts and Kravis completed 14 buyouts between 1969 and 1975 with what one history calls "generally mediocre results" [S27] [Reported]. Some were spectacular: investors in Vapor Corp. (1972, about $37m) got 12 times their stake back when it was sold in 1978, and a Rockwell division bought in 1975 returned 22 times in five years. Others failed: Cobblers Industries (1971) went bankrupt [S27, S28] [Reported]. When Bear Stearns leadership refused to set up an in-house buyout fund, the three left to do it themselves [S28] [Reported].

1 May 1976: "3 guys and a broom"

In KKR's own words: "On May 1, 1976, Henry Kravis and George Roberts co-founded KKR alongside their mentor Jerome Kohlberg with $120,000, launching what we now know as the alternatives industry" [S13] [Confirmed]. KKR describes the early firm as "3 guys and a broom" [S14] [Confirmed]. Its first buyout as KKR was A.J. Industries in 1976 [S28] [Reported].

The first fund still appears in KKR's 2025 annual report. The "1976 Fund" took $31m of commitments and returned $537m: a 35.5% net IRR (the yearly return to investors after fees) and 17.1 times the money invested [S2] [Confirmed]. That track record is the reason pension funds later trusted three people with billions.

Scale, fame and the first break-up

The firm grew fast. Houdaille Industries (1979, about $355m) was then the largest LBO ever and KKR's first buyout of a major public company. Beatrice (1986, about $6.2bn) was financed partly with $2.5bn of "junk bonds" (high-interest, lower-rated bonds) arranged by Drexel [S27] [Reported]. In 1987 Kohlberg resigned, uneasy that KKR's deal hunting "disturbingly echoed the tactics of corporate raiders". He later sued over his reduced ownership; the case settled on undisclosed terms [S27, S28] [Reported]. Kravis and Roberts then ran the firm together as co-CEOs from 1987 until October 2021 [S10, S28] [Confirmed]. RJR Nabisco (1988 to 1989) made KKR famous and is covered straight in section I.

The earnings ladder, one rung at a time

KKR's earnings release builds profit up in steps. Each step has a name that analysts and KKR's own managers use on calls. You do not need the accounting, only the logic.

  • Fee Related Earnings (FRE): management fees, plus transaction and monitoring fees, plus fee-related performance revenues, minus the pay and costs tied to them. The steady, recurring profit of the asset manager [S1, S5] [Confirmed].
  • Total Operating Earnings (TOE): FRE plus Insurance Operating Earnings plus Strategic Holdings Operating Earnings. KKR calls this "the more durable and recurring portion" of earnings. Introduced in November 2023 [S1, S11] [Confirmed].
  • Total Investing Earnings: net realised performance income (carry after the staff share) plus net realised investment income (gains on KKR's own money) [S1] [Confirmed].
  • Adjusted Net Income (ANI): TOE plus Total Investing Earnings, minus interest and taxes. KKR's headline "bottom line" [S1] [Confirmed].
Why this matters

Investors pay more for steady earnings than for lumpy ones. That is why KKR keeps moving revenue into the "recurring" boxes. Example: from Q2 2026, performance fees from its K-Series private equity fund for wealthy individuals moved from carried interest (where 70% to 80% goes to staff) into fee-related performance revenues (where 15% to 20% does). CFO Rob Lewin said this "structurally increases" earnings per share [S1, S3] [Confirmed]. For a client team, this is the link: every dollar KCS raises into a fee-paying fund lands in the most valuable box. At 30 Jun 2026, $72bn of committed money was not yet paying fees, at an average of about 90 basis points (0.90%) [S1, S3] [Confirmed]. Simple maths: that is roughly $650m of yearly fees waiting to switch on once the money is invested (estimate, as of Jun 2026) [Inferred].

C. The contracts: who gives what, and who gets what

A firm like KKR is a bundle of agreements with four groups: the clients who lend it their money (LPs), the people who work there, the shareholders who own the listed company, and KKR's own balance sheet. The trick is that the same people often sit in several groups at once. Employees own about 30% of the shares [S2, S3] [Confirmed]. Clients are sometimes shareholders too.

What LPs give and get

The fund mechanics are in the basics section. Here is what is specific to KKR. Fund lives vary by strategy: infrastructure and real estate drawdown funds invest for up to six years and last 10 to 13 years; credit drawdown funds last 8 to 10 years (up to 12); core private equity vehicles can last up to 25 years; open-ended vehicles have no end date [S2] [Confirmed]. Real examples from the June 2026 fund table: North America Fund XIV ($21.9bn committed at that date, investing April 2025 to April 2031), Global Infrastructure Investors V ($18.6bn, July 2024 to July 2030), European Fund VI ($7.5bn, June 2022 to June 2028) [S1] [Confirmed]. Both flagships later closed larger: about $23bn for North America XIV in April 2026 and $19.2bn for Infrastructure V in August 2026 [S334, S335] [Confirmed].

Key term

Clawback: if a manager takes carry early in a fund's life and the fund later does worse, the manager must hand that carry back. KKR did exactly this in Q4 2025 on Asian Fund II: $344m repaid, $207m net after amounts recovered from current and former employees [S2, S4] [Confirmed]. It is the contract working as designed, and a good example to show you understand alignment.

What employees give and get

KKR pays from one compensation pool across the firm. The 10-K says pay "is based on the performance of KKR as a whole, in addition to an individual's contributions", and that KKR generally does not pay people "based solely on an individual's accomplishments in relation to the profits and losses" of one business [S2] [Confirmed]. Kravis says KKR has always worked this way, "creating a system in which we all succeed and fail together", and that everyone got "phantom equity" from the start [S385] [Confirmed].

Carried interest from all funds is allocated to one vehicle, KKR Associates Holdings L.P., "the carry pool" [S2] [Confirmed]. The pay mix is base salary, a possible cash bonus, discretionary equity and, for select employees, a carry pool allocation [S2] [Confirmed]. On 29 November 2023 KKR made pay "more success based": more from carry, less from fees [S11] [Confirmed]. Bloomberg reported the shift: the staff share of carry went from 60%-70% to 70%-80%, and the fee-related share from 20%-25% to 15%-20% [S30] [Reported; new ranges confirmed by S5].

Say this in the interview

"Because KKR pays from one pool and most pay comes from carry, a person in client solutions and a person on a deal team want the same thing: funds that make money for clients. That is what an LP wants to hear when they ask about alignment, and I think it is a real selling point for the KCS team." [S2, S5] [Inferred]

KKR's balance sheet and its shareholders

KKR's own money is invested next to clients'. At 31 December 2025, KKR and its employees had about $30bn invested in or committed to KKR funds and companies [S12] [Confirmed]. KKR says these "GP commitments" are usually required by contract, and also show "conviction"; it adds that their size has fallen relative to fund sizes as funds grew [S2] [Confirmed]. Employees put $612m of their own cash into KKR vehicles in 2025, mostly without paying fees or carry [S2] [Confirmed].

Shareholders own KKR & Co. Inc. They get a dividend that KKR says it has raised every year since becoming a corporation in 2018: $0.78 a year from Q1 2026 [S1, S4] [Confirmed]. Until the Sunset Date (see section G) they cannot elect directors [S2] [Confirmed]. 891.6m shares were outstanding on 24 Feb 2026 [S2] [Confirmed].

D. The operating model: four layers and what flows between them

The simplest way to picture KKR is as four stacked layers. Clients sit on top. The investments sit at the bottom. In between are the teams that bring money in and the teams that put it to work. The KCS team, the one you are applying to, sits in the second layer and touches every other layer.

Three flows matter most for a KCS candidate. First, KCS is a two-way channel. It takes products to clients and brings client demand back to the investment teams. The Frankfurt KCS intern advert says the team "seeks to 'connect the dots' across all businesses at KKR, from our Private Equity investing teams to Infrastructure, Real Estate, Credit, Capital Markets and Insurance-Funded Strategies" [S390] [Confirmed]. Second, KCS and KCM work together on co-investment and syndication, which is where much of KKR's remaining deal-fee income now comes from, because fund-related deal fees are credited to LPs [S2] [Confirmed; link Inferred]. Third, Global Atlantic is both an engine and a client: its assets are managed by KKR's credit teams, and KKR reports "Total Insurance Economics" of $2.0bn over the 12 months to Q2 2026, which adds those fees to the insurance earnings [S1] [Confirmed].

Governance and risk: who says yes, who keeps watch

KKR runs risk through committees, not single decision-makers. The 10-K says: "Our investment committees, or similar committees, review and evaluate investment opportunities using frameworks that are designed to include qualitative and quantitative assessments of the key investment risks" [S2] [Confirmed]. Committees exist per strategy and region. In London, for example, Tara Davies sits on the Infrastructure Investment Committee and the Infrastructure Portfolio Management Committee, and Mattia Caprioli on the European Private Equity Investment Committee and European Portfolio Management Committee [S16, S17] [Confirmed].

Why this matters

LPs ask about governance in their due diligence questionnaires. A KCS junior helps answer them. Knowing that decisions go through named committees, and that pay is set on the whole firm's results, lets you explain why one bad deal does not lead a team to take wild risks to catch up. Lewin's line from 30 July 2026 sums it up: "Relationships travel, ideas travel, lessons learned travel" [S3] [Confirmed].

The Sunset Date, explained simply

In October 2021, alongside the CEO succession, KKR signed a Reorganization Agreement [S2, S10] [Confirmed]. Its last step is the Sunset Date. In the 10-K's words: "On the 'Sunset Date' (which will occur no later than December 31, 2026), KKR will cancel the Series I preferred stock, establish voting rights for all common stock on a one vote per share basis, and acquire control of the carry pool" [S2] [Confirmed]. Today, ordinary shareholders cannot elect or remove directors: the holder of the Series I preferred share, KKR Management LLP (owned by senior employees including Kravis and Roberts), appoints them. KKR uses the NYSE "controlled company" exemptions [S2] [Confirmed]. On 29 May 2026 directors were again elected by KKR Management LLP [S352] [Confirmed].

In April 2026 shareholders approved three charter changes to prepare: meetings only (no written consent), the board fills vacancies, and the board fixes its own size [S6] [Confirmed]. The fourth, removing the 90% supermajority, failed for lack of quorum at the reconvened meeting on 21 May 2026, even though 97.83% of votes cast were in favour [S7] [Confirmed].

Say this in the interview

"By the end of 2026 KKR becomes a normal one-share-one-vote company. The founders' special control ends 50 years after they started the firm. I see it as the last step of a handover that began with the co-CEOs in 2021, and something an institutional client who is also a shareholder might well ask about." [S2, S10] [Inferred]

I. The skeletons, told straight

Every 50-year-old investment firm has deals that went wrong and fines it paid. Interviewers rarely raise them, but a client asks about them in due diligence, and knowing them calmly is a sign of maturity. Here they are, with the facts and KKR's response.

The pattern in KKR's responses [Inferred]: settle without admitting fault (SEC 2015, SEC 2025, DOJ 2026); stress that the issue is historical and practices have changed; fight first where it sees a principle (the DOJ countersuit); and occasionally make voluntary gestures (the Toys "R" Us fund).

How to talk about this if asked

1. Acknowledge, briefly and with facts. "Yes, RJR is the deal that made KKR famous, but KKR's own filings show the fund behind it returned 8.9% a year, well below its other early funds."

2. Separate type from size. "The recent issues are process ones: a filing case settled for $250m that KKR says outside law firms will reimburse, and an industry-wide messaging fine. They are not about investor losses."

3. Show what changed. "Since 2014 KKR's funds credit 100% of fund-related deal fees to LPs, and in 2025 it repaid a clawback on Asian Fund II. That is the contract protecting clients."

4. Link to the job. "In client solutions you would point LPs to the approved public statement, never improvise, and make sure due diligence answers match the filings." [S2, S4, S349] [Inferred]

Don't say this

"KKR was fined for fraud" (no: the DOJ case is about pre-merger filings, and KKR disputed it). "RJR was a great deal" (KKR's own numbers say otherwise). "Barbarians" as a joke about the firm. And do not volunteer the skeletons unprompted; answer calmly if asked [S2, S21] [Inferred].

What these numbers do and do not mean

They do mean: the London LLP is a substantial team (about 182 employees plus 88 members, so roughly 270 people in this one entity [Inferred]), its costs are rising, and it is run as a sub-adviser to the US parent "in connection with a number of Private Markets funds" [S25] [Confirmed].

They do not mean: that KKR's London business "earns £177m". Under cost-plus, revenue is set by the LLP's own costs plus a margin, not by the fees and carry that London deals generate, which are booked by the US adviser and the funds [S25] [Confirmed; interpretation Inferred]. Nor is this all of London: KKR Credit Advisors (EMEA) LLP and KKR Capital Markets Limited are separate entities whose accounts we did not read. And the profit per member is not a salary: a large share goes to the corporate Managing Member [S25].
