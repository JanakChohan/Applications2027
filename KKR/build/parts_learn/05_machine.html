Module 4: The machine room: co-investment, diligence, distribution rules and the tools

Behind every fund close sits a machine: deals syndicated through walls, questionnaires answered line by line, a rulebook applied to every email, and a record kept of all of it. This module takes the machine apart and shows each moving piece.

Parts 1 to 4 explained what KCS sells and why clients buy. This part explains how the work is actually done. It covers five mechanisms: co-investment syndication with KKR Capital Markets (KCM), the diligence process (DDQs, RFPs, operational due diligence, side letters), the distribution rules a junior applies every day, the tools and records, and a worked week-three task. Every KKR fact carries a source. Every number that is invented for teaching is labelled "illustrative".

Co-investment and syndication: how a deal is shared

The idea in one paragraph

A co-investment is money a client puts directly into one specific deal, alongside a KKR fund, rather than into the fund itself. Syndication is the process of selling that slice of the deal to several clients. KKR describes the mechanism in its 10-K: its capital markets business "provides syndication services for co-investments in transactions participated in by KKR, our investment vehicles, Global Atlantic, and third-party clients, which may entitle the firm to receive transaction fees, management fees, and a carried interest" [S425] [Confirmed]. KCS's official team description gives it a role in exactly this: "Work in partnership with Capital Markets in the syndication/distribution of product" (KKR KCS team description) [Advert].

Key terms

Underwrite: to promise to provide the money first, then sell part of it later. KKR says: "We have used our balance sheet in our capital markets business to underwrite loans, securities or other financial instruments, which we generally expect to syndicate to third parties" [S425] [Confirmed].
Sell-down: the amount the underwriter plans to sell on to others.
Co-underwriting: a client commits before the deal is signed and shares the underwriting risk with KKR. ILPA's due diligence questionnaire asks about it by name [S444] [Confirmed].
Balance sheet: KKR's own money, as opposed to clients' money in funds.

The lifecycle, stage by stage

Figure 1 shows the six stages and who does what in each. Read it as four parallel lanes: the deal team with KCM, the KCS client team, Compliance, and the co-investor. Only the underwrite-then-syndicate logic, the risk of an unsold remainder, and the fee rules are KKR's own disclosures. The exact order of steps is a standard industry sequence that I have reconstructed [Inferred].

Three details in the lifecycle deserve attention, because they are where things go wrong.

  • The unsold remainder. KKR warns that "if we are unable to syndicate all or part of such commitment, or if such co-investors fail to fund their commitments, we may be required to fund the remaining commitment" [S70] [Confirmed]. KKR also uses "arrangements with third parties that reduce our risk associated with holding unsold securities ... which enables our capital markets business to underwrite a larger amount" [S425] [Confirmed]. So the speed and reliability of KCS's client list directly limits how big KKR can underwrite [Inferred].
  • Who gets asked first. Some clients hold co-investment rights in their side letters; others are simply fast. KKR names "increased co-investment rights" among the concessions it may grant in fundraising [S425] [Confirmed]. Allocation policy decides the order, and Compliance checks it [Inferred].
  • The new buyers. Evergreen vehicles now buy co-investments too. K-PEC, KKR's private equity vehicle for individuals, is offered a "first tier" alongside nearly all KKR vehicles "up to a capped amount", but "will not benefit from any priority acquisition allocation" [S420] [Confirmed]. Capital Group KKR U.S. Equity+ "expects to invest approximately 30% of its net assets in K-PEC and approximately 10% of its net assets in co-investments" [S427] [Confirmed]. A retail fund built by a third party is now one of the co-investors.

The economics: why KKR wants co-investment

This is the single most important technical fact in the module, and it comes straight from KKR's 10-K. There are two kinds of deal fee, and they are treated in opposite ways (Figure 2).

The exact words matter, so here they are. "Since 2014, our fund agreements typically require us to share 100% of any monitoring, transaction, and break-up fees that are allocable to a fund ... with fund investors, therefore these types of fees, when generated, are uniquely driven by co-investment opportunities." And: "transaction fees earned in our capital markets business are not shared with fund investors" [S425] [Confirmed]. KCM earned $930m of capital markets transaction fees in 2025 across 404 transactions (49 equity, 355 debt), up from 397 in 2024; fee rates are "generally higher" for equity than debt; about 15% of fees came from unaffiliated third parties [S425] [Confirmed]. In London, the KCM partnership (KKR Capital Markets Partners LLP) had an average of 10 employees and 10 members in 2025 and £244.2m of profit before members' remuneration [S172] [Confirmed]. A small team, a large number.

The formula, in words and symbols

KKR fee income from a deal = (fund-level fees × 0%) + (fees on co-investment capital) + (KCM capital markets fees).
In symbols: FKKR = 0 × Ffund + Fco + FKCM. The first term is zero because of the 100% offset [S425] [Confirmed]. The second and third terms grow with the amount of capital placed outside the fund [Inferred]. One accounting detail: capital syndicated by KCM to third parties is excluded from KKR's "capital invested" metric [S425] [Confirmed], so a large syndication can be invisible in that headline number.

Information walls: inside information and wall-crossing, step by step

Material non-public information (MNPI), often called inside information, is information that is not public and that a reasonable investor would use to decide whether to buy or sell a security. If a co-investment involves a listed company (a take-private, for example), the name of the target is MNPI until the deal is announced [Inferred]. An information wall (or barrier) is the set of rules that keeps such information inside a small group. Wall-crossing is the controlled act of bringing an outsider, such as a client, inside that group, with their consent, so they can evaluate the deal [Inferred].

Why this lands on KCS: KCS works "in partnership with Capital Markets", and KCM handles financings and securities. KKR's UK capital markets entity is FCA-authorised for dealing and arranging [S70] [Confirmed]. Amy Lesch, KCS's Head of Global Product Strategy, is a member of KKR's "Global Conflicts and Compliance Committee" [S121] [Confirmed]. KKR's actual wall procedure is not public. Figure 4 shows the standard shape of a wall-crossing; treat it as a method to learn, not as KKR's manual.

Side letters, MFN elections and the 2025 ILPA reporting templates

Side letters

All investors in a fund sign the same Limited Partnership Agreement (LPA). Individual LPs often negotiate extra terms in a private side agreement called a side letter: fee breaks, reporting rights, ESG commitments, co-investment rights, or "excuse rights" that let an LP skip deals it cannot legally hold [Inferred, from WS4 standard terms].

The most-favoured-nation (MFN) clause

The law firm CMS defines it: an MFN "typically entitles an investor to elect the benefit of rights contained in other investors' side letters and potentially also the right to view the other investors' side letters" [S456] [Confirmed]. Three mechanics make MFNs technical:

  • Tiering. An LP can usually only elect terms given to LPs with equal or smaller commitments [S457] [Reported].
  • Carve-outs. Standard exclusions cover legal, regulatory and tax-status terms, LPAC (advisory committee) seats, co-investment rights and size-based fee breaks [S457] [Reported]. Sponsors use carve-outs to "afford larger, first close or repeat investors more favourable rights" [S456] [Confirmed].
  • The election window. Sponsors "require MFN elections to be submitted within a certain period following closing" and "Late MFN election submissions are at risk of being rejected" [S456] [Confirmed].

The procedure in words. (1) After the final close, the sponsor sends each MFN holder a compendium of side-letter terms it is entitled to see. (2) The client's counsel filters by tier: keep only terms granted to LPs at or below $250m. (3) Strike carve-outs: size-based fee breaks, LPAC seats, co-investment rights, legal, regulatory and tax-status terms. (4) Choose what to elect and return the election form before the deadline. (5) The sponsor updates its obligations register so that, for example, the ESG pack actually goes out every quarter [S456, S457; steps 1 and 5 Inferred]. CMS notes the process adds "financial, administrative and disclosure burdens on sponsors, particularly in open-ended products" [S456] [Confirmed]: an evergreen fund closes every month, so the compendium never stops growing [Inferred].
