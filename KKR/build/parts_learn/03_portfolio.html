Module 2: Thinking like the client: portfolio construction and performance

Before KKR can sell a client anything, someone has to understand the client's whole portfolio better than the client's own spreadsheet does. This module teaches that spreadsheet: liabilities, allocation, pacing, performance maths and insurance capital, each with numbers you can redo by hand.

Part 1 profiled six client types on five constraints. This module goes one level down and puts formulas on them. Every section follows the same pattern: the mechanism from zero, a worked example with numbers, a diagram, then where the same mechanism shows up in real KKR material. Numbers marked "illustrative" are invented to teach the arithmetic; every other number carries its source.

Five words you need before you start

Liability: money a client must pay out in future (pensions, insurance claims, a family's spending). Asset allocation: the split of a portfolio between types of investment. Commitment: a promise to give a private fund money when it asks. NAV (net asset value): what a holding is worth today on the manager's valuation. Benchmark: the yardstick a result is compared against.

Liabilities first: what "enough" means to each client

An investment professional's first question is never "what will make the most money?" It is "what does this money have to do, and when?" The answer is set by the client's liabilities. Return and liquidity targets are derived from them, not chosen freely.

Two jobs a portfolio can do

Liability matching means owning assets whose cash flows or values move with what you owe. If a pension must pay £50m in 2035, a government bond that pays £50m in 2035 removes that risk: whatever interest rates do, the asset and the bill move together. Return seeking means owning assets expected to grow faster than the liabilities, such as shares or private equity, accepting that their value swings. Every institutional portfolio is a blend of the two. The blend is the single most important decision a client makes, and it decides how much room there is for private markets [Inferred].

Key terms

Funding ratio: assets divided by the present value of liabilities. 100% means the client can pay every promise today. Present value: what a future payment is worth today once you discount it at an interest rate. LDI (liability-driven investment): a strategy, mostly with bonds and interest rate hedges, that makes assets move with liabilities. Liquidity budget: how much cash the client must be able to raise, how quickly, in a bad month.

Worked example 1: a DB pension's "enough return" (illustrative numbers)

A UK company pension has £1.0bn of assets. Its promises to members are worth £1.1bn in present value. Assume its liabilities grow 5% a year (interest on the promises plus new accrual, net of payments; illustrative).

  1. Funding ratio = assets ÷ liabilities = 1.0 ÷ 1.1 = 90.9%. The deficit is £100m.
  2. Goal: be fully funded in 10 years with no extra cash from the employer. Assets must reach the liabilities' future value: 1.0 × (1 + r)10 = 1.1 × 1.0510.
  3. Divide both sides by 1.0 and take the tenth root: 1 + r = 1.05 × 1.11/10 = 1.05 × 1.00958 = 1.0601.
  4. "Enough return" = 6.0% a year. Of that, 5.0 points just keep pace with the liabilities and about 1.0 point repairs the deficit.

The lesson: this scheme does not need 12%. It needs 6.0% with as little chance as possible of a big miss. If it hedges most of its liabilities with bonds that earn roughly the 5% liability growth, the remaining "growth" assets have to earn the extra. Private markets compete for that small growth slice, which is why a mature DB scheme is a shrinking buyer of private equity and a natural buyer of contracted, income-producing assets such as core infrastructure or investment-grade private credit [Inferred]. KKR's own institutional page offers corporate pensions "consultative advice to corporations who are considering the benefits and challenges associated with de-risking their defined benefit plans" [S81] [Confirmed].

Worked example 2: a liquidity budget (illustrative numbers)

The same £1.0bn pension pays £60m of pensions a year (£5m a month). Its hedges can demand cash collateral if gilt yields jump; assume a stress call of £80m. It also has £70m of unfunded private commitments that could be called within a year. Stress cash need = 60 + 80 + 70 = £210m. Assets it can sell within a month at a fair price: cash £20m plus listed shares £300m plus a share of the gilts not tied up as collateral, say £100m: £420m. Liquidity coverage = 420 ÷ 210 = 2.0x. If the board's rule is "at least 1.5x", the scheme can afford more illiquid assets only until coverage falls to 1.5x: it could move up to £105m more into private assets, assuming that money comes from the listed shares (each £1 moved cuts liquid assets by £1: 420 − 1.5 × 210 = 105). This is the sum a KCS junior should be able to do before suggesting a commitment size [Inferred].

Strategic asset allocation and KKR's Total Portfolio Approach

Strategic asset allocation (SAA) from zero

Most institutions run a strategic asset allocation: the board fixes a long-term target weight for each asset class ("bucket"), say 50% listed equity, 30% bonds, 8% private equity, 6% infrastructure, 6% real estate. Each bucket has its own team, its own benchmark and a tolerance band (for example 6% to 10% for private equity). When a bucket drifts outside its band, the client buys or sells to rebalance. SAA is simple to govern: the board can see at a glance whether the portfolio is "on policy" [Inferred].

The weakness is that buckets are defined by legal form, not by risk. A private credit loan, a listed high-yield bond and a slice of infrastructure debt can carry very similar risks yet sit in three buckets with three benchmarks. An opportunity that fits no bucket (a capital solutions deal, a reinsurance sidecar) may have no home at all [Inferred].

The Total Portfolio Approach (TPA): what KKR actually published

KKR's Multi-Asset Strategies team published "The Total Portfolio Approach: A Practical Framework" in February 2026, by Henry H. McVey, David McNellis and Christian Olinger [S435, S462] [Confirmed]. A follow-up, "The Dog Has Caught the Bus: The Total Portfolio Approach", appeared in July 2026, adding Amy Dai as an author [S436] [Confirmed]. McVey is Head of Global Macro & Asset Allocation and CIO of KKR's Balance Sheet; McNellis is Co-Head of Global Macro and Head of Portfolio Construction and Multi-Asset Strategies for Private Markets [S436] [Confirmed].

  • Definition. TPA is "a whole-portfolio decision and governance framework that evaluates investment opportunities across Public and Private Markets based on their contributions to total portfolio objectives, rather than within static asset-class sleeves" [S435] [Confirmed].
  • Governance. "The board sets a reference portfolio (for instance, 70% Public Equity/30% Fixed Income) and risk appetite ... and delegates allocation authority to the CIO within a total portfolio risk budget" [S435] [Confirmed].
  • The contrast table. SAA uses fixed asset-class targets, many benchmarks and diversification "Through asset classes"; TPA uses dynamic weights, a "Single reference portfolio and risk budget" and diversification "Through risk factors" [S435] [Confirmed].
  • The analogy KKR uses with clients: TPA is "zero-based budgeting for portfolio construction ... if every investment had to earn its place in the portfolio today, how would capital be allocated?" [S436] [Confirmed].
  • The on-ramp: a "minimum viable TPA", which "can include centralizing portfolio-level functions, strengthening in-house portfolio construction and relative-value capabilities, refreshing views more frequently, and piloting TPA through a dedicated sleeve that has clear risk and liquidity guardrails" [S435] [Confirmed].

Worked example 3: the TPA "funding rule" (illustrative numbers)

The core TPA move is to ask what an investment replaces. Suppose analysis shows a private credit fund behaves, in risk terms, like 30% listed equity plus 70% bonds (an illustrative factor mix, not a KKR estimate). Assume expected returns of 7.0% for listed equity and 4.5% for bonds.

  1. Fund it from its twin. To add £100m of private credit without changing the portfolio's risk, sell £30m of equity and £70m of bonds.
  2. Its hurdle is the twin's return: 0.30 × 7.0% + 0.70 × 4.5% = 2.10% + 3.15% = 5.25%.
  3. Value added: if the private credit is expected to earn 8.0% after fees, the gain is (8.0% − 5.25%) × £100m = £2.75m a year, for roughly the same risk, paid for with illiquidity.
  4. The SAA contrast: in a bucket model the same loan sits in an "alternatives" bucket, measured against its own peer benchmark, and nobody checks what it displaced.

KKR's paper runs the grown-up version: an optimiser matches the factor exposures of a "70% Equities/30% Bonds reference portfolio" while allowing "up to 30% of total portfolio capital to be allocated to Private Markets" and "up to 10% total portfolio leverage" [S435] [Confirmed].

Why TPA matters for private markets in particular

KKR argues TPA gives private markets investors "improved liquidity management, steadier commitment pacing, better vintage diversification, and greater flexibility through drawdowns and denominator effects" [S435] [Confirmed]. The July paper lists three hard problems for private assets inside TPA: the opportunity set and relative value; risk measurement, because "Investors often commit capital before knowing the exposures they will ultimately own"; and dynamism, meaning illiquidity [S436] [Confirmed]. Its answers include strategy-level benchmarks, public comparables, pacing with recycled distributions, and "secondaries and liquidity facilities available during dislocations" [S436] [Confirmed]. It also gives the key pacing input: "Across private market strategies, annual distributions have historically averaged approximately 10-30% of NAV" [S436] [Confirmed]. Commitment pacing: why you cannot just "buy 10% private equity"

With listed shares, deciding on a 10% weight and owning 10% are the same day's work. With a private fund they are years apart. A client commits money; the fund calls it in instalments as deals are found (typically over 3 to 5 years for KKR's drawdown funds [S70] [Confirmed]); it distributes cash as assets are sold; and the client's actual exposure is the NAV in between. So the client controls commitments, but its target is set in NAV. Converting one into the other is called commitment pacing, and the standard tool is a 2002 model from Yale [Inferred].

The Takahashi-Alexander ("Yale") model

Dean Takahashi and Seth Alexander published "Illiquid Alternative Asset Fund Modeling" in the Journal of Portfolio Management in 2002 [S454] [Confirmed]. It is "a discrete-time deterministic model" (it steps year by year and has no randomness) with six inputs: "RC (rate of contribution), CC (capital commitment), L (life of the fund), B (bow factor describing changes on the rate of distribution over time), G (annual growth rate), and Y (yield percentage)" [S454] [Confirmed]. In words: each year the fund calls a share of what is still uncalled; the NAV grows; a rising share of the NAV is paid out, and the bow sets how back-loaded that payout is [S455] [Reported].

The four equations, in symbols and in words

1. Calls: Ct = RCt × (CC − PICt−1). "This year's call = the call rate × the commitment not yet paid in." PIC is paid-in capital to date.

2. Distribution rate: RDt = max[ Y , (t ÷ L)B ]. "The share of NAV paid out rises with the fund's age; a bigger bow B keeps it low for longer." Y is a floor for income-paying assets.

3. Distributions: Dt = RDt × NAVt−1 × (1 + G). "Pay out that share of last year's NAV after this year's growth."

4. NAV: NAVt = NAVt−1 × (1 + G) + Ct − Dt. "Grow it, add what came in, take off what went out."

The six inputs are from the source [S454] [Confirmed]; this written-out form is the widely used textbook version [Inferred].

Over-commitment and the commitment ratio

Look at year 5 of the plan: the client has signed £1.5bn of commitments to hold £1.0bn of NAV, and £506m is still uncalled. Over-commitment is the deliberate practice of committing more than the NAV target, because some of the money will not be called for years and some will already have come back [Inferred]. Two ratios keep it honest:

  • Commitment ratio = total commitments ÷ target NAV = 1,500 ÷ 1,000 = 1.5x in the plan.
  • Unfunded cover = liquid assets ÷ unfunded commitments. The unfunded £506m is a real promise: if all of it were called in a bad year, the client must pay from its liquid assets. A client with £2bn of cash and bonds has cover of about 4x.

The steady-state check uses KKR's own figure. If distributions run at 10% to 30% of NAV a year [S436] [Confirmed], a £1.0bn private book returns roughly £100m to £300m a year. To hold NAV flat, new calls must replace that, which is why mature programmes commit every year rather than in bursts [Inferred]. KKR's TPA paper argues for "steadier commitment pacing" and "better vintage diversification" for exactly this reason [S435] [Confirmed].

Reading performance like an LP

A KCS team spends much of its life presenting performance, and clients' due diligence teams spend theirs taking it apart. ILPA's due diligence questionnaire has a whole section on track record [S444] [Confirmed], and ILPA's 2025 Performance Template standardises IRR and TVPI/MOIC, gross and net, "with and without the impact of fund-level subscription facilities" [S445] [Confirmed]. This section teaches each measure, then shows how each one can flatter or mislead.

IRR: the return that cares about timing

The internal rate of return is the single annual interest rate that makes all of a fund's cash flows, in and out, worth exactly zero today.

Formula

0 = Σ CFt ÷ (1 + IRR)t. In words: discount every cash flow back to day 0 at the rate IRR; IRR is the rate at which the money you put in and the money you got out cancel exactly. Calls are negative CF, distributions positive, and any remaining NAV is treated as a final positive flow.

Worked example 7 (illustrative). An LP pays £100m at year 0 and £50m at year 1, receives £60m at year 2 and £150m at year 4. There is no algebraic shortcut, so you try rates:

  • At 10%: −100 − 50 ÷ 1.10 + 60 ÷ 1.102 + 150 ÷ 1.104 = −100 − 45.45 + 49.59 + 102.45 = +6.59. Positive, so the true rate is higher.
  • At 15%: −100 − 43.48 + 45.37 + 85.76 = −12.35. Negative, so the rate is lower.
  • Straight-line between them: 10% + 5% × 6.59 ÷ (6.59 + 12.35) = 11.7%. A spreadsheet's IRR function gives 11.6%.

The timing trap

IRR rewards speed. Return money early and IRR jumps even if total profit is small. Delay the call with a credit line and IRR jumps again even though the LP pays interest. Neither makes the LP richer. So an LP never reads IRR alone: it reads it next to the multiples below [Inferred].

The multiples: TVPI, DPI, RVPI and MOIC

Formulas

DPI (distributions to paid-in) = cumulative distributions ÷ paid-in capital. "How much cash have I actually had back?" RVPI (residual value to paid-in) = current NAV ÷ paid-in. "How much is still on paper?" TVPI (total value to paid-in) = DPI + RVPI. MOIC (multiple on invested capital) is the same idea measured on the money put into deals, usually gross of fees, often deal by deal (standard industry definitions) [Inferred].

Worked example 8. Take the fund from the pacing section at the end of year 6: paid in £96.9m, distributed £57.0m to date, NAV £73.4m. DPI = 57.0 ÷ 96.9 = 0.59x. RVPI = 73.4 ÷ 96.9 = 0.76x. TVPI = 0.59 + 0.76 = 1.35x. In words: the LP has had 59p back for every £1 paid in, and the manager values what is left at another 76p.

Gross versus net: the fee drag, worked

Gross returns are what the deals earned. Net returns are what the LP kept after management fees, fund expenses and carried interest. The gap is the price of the manager. Drawdown funds traditionally charge a management fee of about 1.5% to 2% a year and carried interest of about 20% above a hurdle, often 8% (standard industry terms; exact terms vary by fund) [Inferred]. KKR's evergreen K-PEC uses a different formula: 1.25% a year of NAV and 15% of total return above a 5% hurdle, with a high-water mark and a 100% catch-up [S420] [Confirmed].

Worked example 9 (illustrative, simplified). An LP commits and pays in £100m over the fund's life. Management fees take £12m in total, so £88m goes into deals. The deals return 2.2 times their cost. Assume a single 5-year holding period, the 8% hurdle is cleared, and a full catch-up gives the manager 20% of all profit.

  1. Gross value = 88 × 2.2 = £193.6m. Gross MOIC = 2.20x.
  2. Profit over everything the LP paid = 193.6 − 100 = £93.6m. Hurdle check: 100 × 1.085 = 146.9 < 193.6, so carry is due.
  3. Carry = 20% × 93.6 = £18.7m. LP receives 193.6 − 18.7 = £174.9m. Net TVPI = 1.75x.
  4. Over five years: gross IRR = 2.201/5 − 1 = 17.1%; net IRR = 1.7491/5 − 1 = 11.8%. Fee drag = 5.3 points a year.

PME: did the fund beat the stock market?

An 11% IRR is good or bad only relative to what the same money would have made in listed shares. Kaplan and Schoar's public market equivalent (2003 working paper, 2005 Journal of Finance) answers that. In their words: "The PME measures the discounted value of the cash outflows of the fund relative to the discounted value of the cash inflows (all net of fees), where discounting is undertaken using the total return to the S&P 500" [S453] [Confirmed]. Above 1.0 means the fund beat the index [Inferred]. Their research found average net returns roughly equal to the S&P 500, with large dispersion and strong persistence between a manager's successive funds [S453] [Reported].

Formula

KS-PME = [ Σ Dt ÷ It ] ÷ [ Σ Ct ÷ It ]. In words: convert every distribution and every call into "units of the index" by dividing by the index level on that date, then compare units out with units in. (Remaining NAV counts as a final distribution.)

Vintage years and quartiles

A fund's vintage is the year it started investing. Funds of the same vintage bought in the same market, so they are ranked against each other, not against funds from other years. A quartile splits that peer group into four: "top quartile" means the best 25% of funds of that vintage on a chosen measure (net IRR, TVPI or PME). Two traps follow. First, the same number can rank very differently in two vintages. Second, a young fund's quartile is mostly RVPI, so it can move a lot before the fund is finished [Inferred].

Why DPI dominates in 2025-26

For a decade, LPs ranked managers mainly on IRR and TVPI. Since 2022 exits have slowed, cash has not come back, and DPI has become the most-watched number [S262] [Confirmed]. The evidence, provider by provider (each defines the base differently, so do not add them up):

  • Bain: distributions as a share of NAV were about 14% in 2025, below 15% for four years running [S262] [Confirmed].
  • McKinsey's 2026 report, via Dakota: distributions fell to 6% of AUM versus a 2015 to 2019 average of 16% [S261] [Reported].
  • PitchBook: almost $5 trillion sits in funds 7 or more years old, 39% of total NAV (December 2025) [S260] [Reported]. Bain counts about 32,000 unsold portfolio companies worth about $3.8tn [S262, S326] [Confirmed].
  • Bain cites an ILPA survey: 53% of LPs said undrawn commitments limit new PE commitments [S262] [Confirmed].

What this means at KKR: an exit is now a client-service event as much as an investment one. When Aon agreed to buy USI Insurance Services from KKR and co-investors for about $17bn including debt in August 2026, coverage put the price at about 6x KKR's original equity [S354] [Reported]. A realisation like that turns RVPI into DPI for every LP in the fund, and becomes evidence in the next fundraise [Inferred]. The KCS question to ask is not "what is the IRR?" but "how much of the TVPI is cash?"

Evergreen performance: a different clock

KKR's K-Series funds have no end date. Investors buy in monthly at NAV and can ask to sell quarterly, up to a cap (5% of NAV per quarter for K-PEC) [S420] [Confirmed]. There are no capital calls and no final distribution, so IRR and DPI do not fit. Evergreen funds report a total return per share class instead.

Three formulas

Total return for a period = (NAV per share at end + distributions paid − NAV per share at start) ÷ NAV per share at start.

Chain-linking: a yearly return is built from monthly ones: (1 + rJan) × (1 + rFeb) × ... × (1 + rDec) − 1. This is a time-weighted return: it ignores when investors added or withdrew money, so it measures the manager, not the investor's timing.

Distribution rate = annualised distribution per share ÷ current NAV per share. It is a cash yield, not a return.

Worked example 10 (illustrative). Three months of NAV moves: +1.0%, −0.5%, +2.0%. Quarter return = 1.010 × 0.995 × 1.020 − 1 = 2.505%, slightly more than the 2.5% you get by adding; over twelve months the gap grows. Now an infrastructure-style share class: NAV starts the year at £25.00, pays £0.30 a quarter, ends at £24.60. Distribution rate = (4 × 0.30) ÷ 24.60 = 4.9%. Total return = (24.60 + 1.20 − 25.00) ÷ 25.00 = 3.2%. A 4.9% payout on a 3.2% return means part of the cash came from capital. K-INFRA's own 10-K warns that its distributions "may and are expected to exceed our earnings and cash flow from operating activities" and may be paid from borrowings or offering proceeds [S421] [Confirmed].

Why evergreen returns and drawdown IRRs are apples and oranges

A drawdown fund's IRR is money-weighted: it measures only the money the fund has called, while it is invested. An evergreen total return is time-weighted and applies to all the money from day one, including a liquidity sleeve held in cash and credit (up to about 20% for K-PEC, about 15% for K-INFRA) [S420, S421] [Confirmed]. A drawdown LP's uncalled commitment sits elsewhere earning less, and the IRR ignores that.

Worked example 11 (illustrative). The same assets grow 10% a year. Investor 1 commits £100 to the drawdown fund from the pacing section and holds the uncalled money and distributions in cash at 4%. Investor 2 puts £100 into an evergreen fund holding the same assets. After five years:

  • Drawdown: NAV £88.6 + cash £48.5 = £137.1. The fund's IRR on called capital is a perfect 10.0%, but the committed £100 grew only 6.5% a year.
  • Evergreen, fully invested: 100 × 1.105 = £161.1 (10.0% a year).
  • Evergreen with a 15% liquidity sleeve at 4%: 100 × (0.85 × 1.105 + 0.15 × 1.045) = £155.1 (9.2% a year).

Four more differences to name when a client compares the two: fees are charged on NAV in evergreen funds but often on commitments in drawdown funds [S420] [Confirmed for K-PEC; drawdown norm Inferred]; evergreen NAVs are set monthly by the manager under board-approved policies, which is why operational due diligence focuses on valuation governance "where transactions are based on the fund's NAV" [S458] [Confirmed]; liquidity is real but capped, and in Q1 2026 K-FIT received requests for 6.3% of shares and paid about 80% of them, then paid in full in Q2 [S338] [Reported]; and evergreen investors who join late buy at today's NAV, so they do not get the early J-curve but also do not get the early discount [Inferred].

Insurance clients: capital, matching and Global Atlantic

Insurers are a third of KKR's AUM when Global Atlantic's balance sheet (22%) and third-party insurers (11%) are added together (31 Dec 2025) [S71] [Confirmed; sum Inferred]. They think about investments differently from anyone else, because every asset uses up regulatory capital.

How a life insurer makes money, from zero

A customer pays a lump sum for an annuity: a promise of fixed payments for years. The insurer invests the money in bonds and loans. Its profit is the spread: asset yield minus the rate it credits to the customer minus costs. Three constraints bind every investment decision [Inferred]:

  1. Matching. Asset cash flows should arrive when the annuity payments are due (asset-liability management, ALM). Self-amortising assets help: KKR describes asset-based finance (ABF) investors receiving "front-loaded principal payments plus interest, similar to the way homeowners make their mortgage payments" [S434] [Confirmed].
  2. Capital. US life insurers hold risk-based capital (RBC) against each asset. "The determination of RBC is based on the NAIC designation of the assets"; and the designation "depends on the applicable NRSRO rating" (a recognised credit rating) [S425] [Confirmed]. The NAIC moved from 6 to 20 bond designation categories with year-end 2021 factors [S461] [Reported].
  3. Liquidity. Enough to pay claims and surrenders without forced selling [Inferred].

So an insurer wants assets that are investment-grade rated (low capital charge) but pay more than public bonds with the same rating. Private investment-grade credit and ABF are built for that [S425 for the rule] [Inferred for the preference].

Capstone: a private markets plan for a UK DC megafund

This exercise pulls the module together. It is the kind of analysis a London KCS team could prepare before meeting a large UK workplace pension. The scheme is hypothetical and all its numbers are illustrative; the rules and KKR facts are sourced.

Step 1: the client and its constraints

Scheme M (hypothetical) is a multi-employer defined contribution master trust. Its default fund is £30bn in 2027, growing to an assumed £40bn by 2030 from contributions and returns. Members are young on average, so contributions exceed withdrawals (illustrative). Its constraints:

  • Scale rule. Under the Pension Schemes Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026), large multi-employer DC schemes need at least one default fund of £25bn or more by 2030, with a transition route for schemes of £10bn that can reach £25bn by 2035 [S346] [Confirmed]. Scheme M already qualifies.
  • Allocation pledge and reserve power. The voluntary Mansion House Accord (13 May 2025): 17 providers pledged at least 10% of DC default funds in private markets by 2030, with at least 5% in the UK [S315] [Confirmed]. The Act's reserve power, usable once and not before 1 January 2028, could require up to 10% in "qualifying assets", no more than 5% UK-specific, with a trustee opt-out where compliance is "likely not to be in the best interests of members" [S346] [Confirmed].
  • Liquidity. The default fund is priced and dealt daily [Inferred]. So private assets must sit in a vehicle that the daily fund can hold.
  • Vehicle rules. The UK Long-Term Asset Fund (LTAF) was opened to DC default and self-select use when PS23/7 recategorised LTAF units from 3 July 2023 [S305, S306] [Confirmed]. LTAFs deal no more often than monthly with at least 90 days' notice for redemptions [S451] [Reported]. LTAF AUM was about £7.5bn in May 2026 [S452] [Reported].
  • Fees and value. DC fees are closely scrutinised, so low-fee routes (co-investment, SMAs, evergreen at scale) matter [Inferred].
