Part 2

Module 1: The plumbing of private markets products

Every KKR product is a pipe that carries money from a client to a deal and back. This module takes the pipes apart: who can use each one, when money moves, who gets paid in what order, and what happens when too many people want their money back at once.

Part 1 showed that a KCS desk matches a client's constraints to a product's terms. This part is the product side in full mechanical detail. By the end you should be able to take any KKR vehicle, say what legal box it sits in, draw how cash flows through it, compute its fees for a year, and explain what happens in a bad quarter. That is the vocabulary every product specialist, relationship manager and analyst in KCS uses every day [Inferred].

1. The vehicle menu: eleven ways to own the same kind of asset

A vehicle (or "wrapper") is the legal container investors put money into. The assets inside can be similar: companies, power grids, loans, buildings. What changes from wrapper to wrapper is four things: who is allowed in, when cash moves, how fees are charged and how (and whether) you can get out. KKR's 10-K describes the range: drawdown private equity funds with management fees of 1.0% to 2.0% of committed capital during the investment period, drawdown infrastructure and real estate funds at 0.75% to 1.50%, and open-ended vehicles (core infrastructure, core+ real estate, evergreen direct lending, K-Series) at 0.50% to 1.25% [S2] [Confirmed]. How the institutional pieces fit around one deal

The top-left corner is not one product but a family. One KKR deal can be funded by five different pipes at once. The main fund and any parallel funds take their fixed shares. Feeders sit above the main fund and simply pass money through. SMAs invest next to the fund under their own contract. Co-investors buy extra pieces of that one deal. Since 2023 a sixth pipe exists: K-PEC (or K-INFRA in infrastructure) joins the deal as a co-general partner of a deal-level joint venture alongside the flagship fund [S420, S421] [Confirmed].

Three wrappers that look odd until you see why they exist

Co-investment is a profit engine, not a giveaway. Since 2014 KKR's fund agreements "typically require us to share 100% of any monitoring, transaction, and break-up fees that are allocable to a fund" with fund investors, but "transaction fees earned in our capital markets business are not shared with fund investors" [S425] [Confirmed]. So when KCS places a slice of a deal with a co-investor, the co-investor pays a low fee, but the deal can generate KCM fees that fund investors do not offset [S425] [Inferred]. KCM earned $930m of capital markets transaction fees in 2025 [S425] [Confirmed]. Industry-wide, PitchBook tracked $37.5bn of co-investment fundraising in the 12 months to June 2026 [S260] [Confirmed].

SMAs are where the biggest clients are going. KKR's 10-K warns that sovereign wealth funds and public pension funds "continue to demonstrate an increased preference for alternatives to traditional fund structures, such as separately managed accounts" [S70] [Confirmed].

Insurance sidecars turn equity into balance sheet. The Ivy vehicles are "commitment-based structures with third-party investors" that "participate alongside Global Atlantic through reinsurance" during their investment periods, and are supervised by the Bermuda Monetary Authority [S425] [Confirmed]. At Q2 2026 KKR described "$6 billion of dry powder that we think translates to north of $60 billion of buying power on the liability side" [S440] [Confirmed]. That is roughly $1 of investor equity supporting $10 of insurance liabilities [S440] [Inferred].

2. Drawdown mechanics: commitment, call, fee, return

Step 1: the commitment

An institution signs the Limited Partnership Agreement (LPA, the fund's contract) and commits, say, $100m. No cash moves yet. The commitment is a legally binding promise to pay when asked [Inferred]. KKR's 10-K describes this model: commit now, and KKR calls the money over the investment period [S70] [Confirmed]. Investment periods are long: North America Fund XIV invests from April 2025 to April 2031, GII V from July 2024 to July 2030, and European Fund VI from June 2022 to June 2028 [S1] [Confirmed]. Infrastructure and real estate drawdown funds have fund terms of 10 to 13 years [S2] [Confirmed].

Step 2: the capital call

When KKR agrees a deal, the fund sends each LP a capital call notice (a drawdown notice): "please wire X by date Y". X is the LP's percentage of the fund times the cash needed. If a $10bn fund needs $400m for a deal plus $25m of fees and expenses, an LP with a $100m commitment (1% of the fund) wires $4.25m (illustrative numbers). The money not yet called is the uncalled commitment, also called "dry powder". KKR reported $142.7bn of uncalled commitments across the firm at 30 June 2026 [S1] [Confirmed].

Key term

Uncalled commitment: commitment minus everything called so far. For an LP it is a liability (cash it must keep ready). For KKR it is future fee-paying capital. For a KCS pacing conversation, it is the number that decides how much more a client can commit this year [Inferred].

A real fund, read like an LP statement

KKR's 10-Q fund table gives the live state of European Fund VI at 30 June 2026: commitment $7,513m, uncalled $1,795m, invested $5,719m, remaining fair value $7,269m [S175] [Confirmed]. Read it the way an LP would. Called so far is about $7,513m minus $1,795m, roughly $5,718m, or 76% of commitments [Inferred]. Remaining fair value of $7,269m against $5,719m invested is about 1.27x on paper, before any distributions already made [Inferred] (the table does not separate realised proceeds).

Step 3: the management fee and its base

The fee base matters as much as the rate. KKR's drawdown private equity funds charge 1.0% to 2.0% of committed capital during the investment period, then 0.75% to 1.50% of invested capital afterwards [S2] [Confirmed]. Worked example, illustrative numbers within those ranges:

PhaseFormulaLP with $100m commitment
Investment period (6 years)rate × commitment = 1.5% × $100m$1.5m a year, $9.0m over 6 years, even in year 1 when only $20m is called
Year 1 effective ratefee ÷ cash called = $1.5m ÷ $20m7.5% of the cash actually at work

This is why large LPs negotiate the base as hard as the rate, and why fees on uncalled money show up in every institutional fee discussion [Inferred]. It also explains a KKR-level number: committed capital that is not yet paying fees is "shadow" revenue that switches on when the fund starts its investment period [S1, S2] [Inferred].

Fee offsets. Portfolio companies can pay transaction or monitoring fees. Under KKR's post-2014 fund agreements, 100% of such fees allocable to the fund are shared with fund investors, in practice by reducing the management fee [S425] [Confirmed].

Recycling. Many LPAs let the GP re-use ("recycle") early proceeds, for example from a deal sold within the investment period, instead of distributing them, which lets more than 100% of commitments be invested over the fund's life [Inferred] (general practice; KKR's specific recycling terms are not in the sources). KKR's portfolio construction team makes the client-side version of the point: investors should use "recycled distributions" in pacing, noting that "annual distributions have historically averaged approximately 10-30% of NAV" [S436] [Confirmed].

2b. The distribution waterfall, worked in full

When the fund sells a company, cash flows down a "waterfall": a fixed order of who is paid first. KKR's carried interest is typically 10% to 20% of fund profits, "usually after a 6% to 8% preferred return (hurdle) with a catch-up to the general partner" [S2] [Confirmed]. The four tiers of a European (whole-of-fund) waterfall are:

  1. Return of capital. 100% to LPs until they have back everything they paid in.
  2. Preferred return (hurdle). 100% to LPs until they have earned, say, 8% a year compounding on that capital.
  3. GP catch-up. 100% (in a "full" catch-up) to the GP until the GP has 20% of all profit paid so far.
  4. Split. Everything after that: 80% to LPs, 20% to the GP.

The worked example (illustrative numbers)

A $1,000m fund calls all its money on day one and receives all its proceeds in year 5. Terms: 8% compounding hurdle, 20% carry, 100% catch-up. These are within KKR's stated ranges [S2] but are not any specific fund's terms.

Read the chart from top to bottom. At $1,300m the hurdle is not met, so KKR earns nothing. At $1,500m KKR is inside the catch-up and has only 6.1% of profit. From the point where catch-up ends (about $1,587m) KKR has exactly 20% of profit, which is the point of a full catch-up: it makes the hurdle a timing protection for LPs, not a permanent discount on carry [Inferred].

The American (deal-by-deal) contrast and the clawback

An American waterfall runs the tiers deal by deal, so the GP can receive carry on an early winner before the fund as a whole has made money. ILPA has long favoured whole-of-fund waterfalls plus a clawback, the GP's duty to hand back carry it turns out not to have earned; ILPA Principles 2.0 (2011) added a Clawback appendix [S317] [Confirmed]. Worked example, illustrative numbers: the same $1,000m fund invests $500m in deal A and $500m in deal B on day one.

This happened at KKR. In Q4 2025 KKR repaid $344m of carried interest on Asian Fund II (a 2013 vintage, $5.8bn fund); net of amounts recouped from current and former employees, the clawback cut net realised performance income by $207m [S2, S4] [Confirmed]. CalPERS reports a 1.0% net IRR and 1.0x multiple on its $350m commitment to that fund [S117] [Confirmed]. A clawback is not theory; it is a line in KKR's 2025 results [Inferred].

3. Evergreen mechanics: how K-PEC actually works

A drawdown fund needs a $10m-plus cheque, a ten-year lock and an operations team to answer capital calls. Individuals cannot do that. An evergreen fund solves it: no end date, money goes in once, fully invested, and a limited amount can come out each quarter [Inferred]. K-PEC, KKR Private Equity Conglomerate LLC, is the flagship. It is a Delaware LLC formed on 6 December 2022 that began operations on 1 August 2023 [S420] [Confirmed].

The legal puzzle: why K-PEC is not an "investment company"

The US Investment Company Act of 1940 regulates funds that hold securities. If K-PEC were caught by it, the Act would restrict its leverage, its dealings with KKR affiliates and its fee structure [S420] [Confirmed]. The Act has two tests, and K-PEC's 10-K explains how it passes each [S420] [Confirmed]:

  • Test 1, "primarily engaged". A company is an investment company if it is "engaged primarily ... in the business of investing, reinvesting or trading in securities". K-PEC says that "through its Joint Ventures, the Company is primarily engaged in the businesses of its Joint Ventures, namely, the business of owning and controlling portfolio companies."
  • Test 2, the "40% test". A company is an investment company if "investment securities" exceed 40% of its total assets (excluding government securities and cash). K-PEC's main assets are wholly owned Operating Subsidiaries (for example K-PEC Holdings LLC). Those subsidiaries hold general partner interests in each deal-level Joint Venture, and "such general partner interests are not 'investment securities'." The only investment securities sit in the liquidity sleeve, which K-PEC keeps below 40%.

In plain English: a limited partner owns a passive stake (a security); a general partner runs the partnership (an operating business). By making K-PEC a co-general partner of each deal, alongside the KKR flagship fund, KKR turned a fund into an operating company, an old-style conglomerate [S420] [Inferred]. The structure has teeth: if the Management Agreement with KKR ends, K-PEC must "forfeit our controlling interest in any Joint Venture, which would likely require us to register as an investment company" [S420] [Confirmed].

Allocation: who gets the deal first

K-PEC co-invests with KKR's flagship private equity funds, but it is at the back of the queue. Its 10-K says: "the Company does not benefit from any mandatory minimum purchase rights or minimum purchase thresholds. As such the Company will not benefit from any priority acquisition allocation." Flagship funds have a mandatory minimum that is filled first. K-PEC is then offered a "first tier" alongside nearly all KKR vehicles "up to a capped amount", and may then be offered part of any "second tier" [S420] [Confirmed]. The institutional flagship LP gets first call; the wealth vehicle fills in behind [Inferred].

K-PEC also bought stakes in two companies in 2025 ($165.5m) "from affiliated KKR-sponsored investment vehicles alongside the relevant flagship private equity vehicle as part of a sale and purchase between successive fund vintages" [S420] [Confirmed]. That is the kind of related-party transfer ILPA's due diligence questionnaire probes in its section on GP-led secondaries [S444] [Inferred].

NAV: how the price is struck every month

NAV (net asset value) is the value of everything the fund owns minus what it owes, divided by shares. For K-PEC, the Manager "determines the NAV of our Shares monthly" and an Administrator computes the per-share figure [S420] [Confirmed]. All portfolio companies are Level III, meaning there is no market price, so value comes from models and comparables; K-PEC discloses that a 10% fall in their fair value would cut net assets by $898.6m [S420] [Confirmed]. The price investors deal at is the Transactional NAV, which differs from accounting (GAAP) NAV mainly because GAAP books the lifetime cost of distribution fees up front: $9.932bn Transactional versus $9.495bn GAAP at 31 December 2025 [S420] [Confirmed], a gap of $437m [Inferred].

Subscribing: you buy dollars, not shares

K-PEC runs "a continuous private offering of our Shares on a monthly basis" [S420] [Confirmed]. An investor subscribes a dollar amount and only learns the share count afterwards: a subscription accepted on 1 September is priced at the 31 August Transactional NAV [S420] [Confirmed]. 3b. The evergreen fee stack and the repurchase plan, worked

The two fees

Management fee: 1.25% a year of month-end NAV for Classes S, D, U and I, paid monthly. The early "R" classes pay 1.00% for 60 months from 1 August 2023, then 1.25% [S420] [Confirmed]. Performance Participation Allocation (the evergreen version of carry): "15.0% of the Total Return attributable to Investor Shares subject to a 5.0% annual Hurdle Amount and a High Water Mark, with a 100% Catch-Up", measured and paid annually and accrued monthly [S420] [Confirmed].

Key terms

Hurdle: the return investors keep before KKR shares in any gain (here 5% a year). Catch-up: once over the hurdle, KKR takes 100% of the next slice until it has 15% of the whole return. High-water mark: KKR earns nothing on gains that only recover earlier losses (the 10-K's "Loss Carryforward Amount") [S420] [Confirmed] (plain-English gloss [Inferred]).

In a year after a loss, say minus $100,000, the next $100,000 of gain is a recovery under the high-water mark and attracts no performance allocation [S420] (numbers illustrative).

The repurchase plan: the exit door and its size

K-PEC lets investors sell shares back quarterly, but the door is narrow [S420] [Confirmed]:

  • The cap (gate): total repurchases of "no more than 5.0% of our aggregate NAV ... per calendar quarter". The Board can repurchase less, or nothing.
  • Early Repurchase Fee: 5.0% of NAV on shares held under 24 months, kept by the fund for the remaining holders; it may be waived on death, disability or divorce.
  • Overflow: if requests exceed 5%, the Board may run a tender for "Excess Shares" "at or about a 10% discount to the most recent NAV". This is unusual: a paid exit valve above the gate [Inferred].
  • Minimum balance after a partial repurchase: $1,000.

In practice K-PEC's door has been barely used so far: 2025 repurchases were about $26.4m net of early repurchase fees against $9.9bn of NAV [S420] [Confirmed], roughly 0.3% for the year [Inferred]. The fund also has a third-party revolving credit facility upsized to $1.0bn in March 2026, with recourse borrowing capped at 30% of total assets [S420] [Confirmed]; a credit line is a further buffer between a redemption request and a forced sale [Inferred].

3c. The rest of the K-Series: three more legal answers

K-INFRA uses the same conglomerate design for infrastructure. It owns "Infrastructure Assets" through Joint Ventures with Global Infrastructure Investors II to V, Asia Pacific Infrastructure Investors I to III, DCIF and the Global Climate Fund. Target mix about 85% infrastructure (no more than 15% non-OECD) and 15% liquidity. Fees: 1.25% of NAV plus 12.5% of Total Return over a 5% hurdle, with a high-water mark and 100% catch-up. Repurchases quarterly, capped at 5% of NAV, 5% fee within 24 months. Distributions are quarterly, and the 10-K warns they "may and are expected to exceed our earnings and cash flow from operating activities" [S421] [Confirmed]. K-INFRA can also buy "Warehoused Assets" that KKR or a bank bought first, "at cost, or cost plus an interest rate or carrying cost" [S421] [Confirmed]: the same buy-then-syndicate engine KCM runs for institutional co-investors [Inferred].

K-FIT takes the opposite legal route. It is inside the 1940 Act framework, as "an externally managed, non-diversified, closed-end management investment company that has elected to be regulated as a business development company", and is a RIC for tax, paying no corporate tax if it distributes its income [S422] [Confirmed]. Its adviser, FS/KKR Advisor, is jointly operated by KKR Credit and an affiliate of Future Standard (formerly FS Investments) [S422] [Confirmed]. Rules: at least 60% of assets in first lien senior secured loans to US middle-market companies; up to 30% in asset-based finance and subordinated loans; quarterly tender for "up to 5% of our Common Shares outstanding"; a 2% Early Repurchase Deduction within 12 months [S422] [Confirmed]. Its fee is income-based, worked below.

Why the catch-up ends at 1.43%: the adviser has caught up when its fee equals 12.5% of all income, so (I minus 1.25) = 0.125 × I, which gives I = 1.25 ÷ 0.875 = 1.4286% [Inferred]. K-FIT's leverage limit is the BDC rule of 150% asset coverage, meaning assets must be at least 1.5 times debt, which allows debt of up to roughly 2x equity [S422] [Confirmed] ("2x" is [Inferred]). At 31 December 2025 it had total assets of $2.75bn and shareholders' equity of $1.54bn [S422] [Confirmed], so liabilities of about $1.21bn, or about 0.8x equity, well inside the limit [Inferred].

KREST is a third answer: a registered closed-end fund under the 1940 Act (the N-2 cover ticks "Registered Closed-End Fund", not "Interval Fund"). It invests at least 80% of net assets in real estate. Minimum initial investment is $10,000 for Classes S, D and U and $1,000,000 for Class I, with $500 subsequent; it is the only K-Series vehicle with a published minimum in the sources. Fees: 1.25% of average daily net assets plus 12.5% of quarterly "Portfolio Operating Income", with no incentive fee on capital gains. It "intends, but is not obligated, to conduct quarterly tender offers for up to 5.0% of the aggregate NAV", and admits repurchases "have in the past been and may in the future be oversubscribed" [S424] [Confirmed].

4. Interval funds: the public-private blend

An interval fund is a US registered fund that continuously sells shares and, under SEC Rule 23c-3, adopts a "fundamental policy" (changeable only by a shareholder vote) to make quarterly repurchase offers for a set share of its stock. Capital Group KKR Core Plus+ commits to offers of "between 5% and 25% of its outstanding shares at net asset value" and "currently expects to offer to repurchase 10%"; oversubscribed offers are pro-rated [S426] [Confirmed]. The key contrast: an interval fund must make the offer every quarter; a K-Series board may repurchase less or nothing [S420, S426] [Inferred].

Why blend public and private?

A fund that promises 10% liquidity a quarter cannot hold only assets that take months to sell. So the blend is the liquidity engine. Core Plus+ allocates "approximately 60% of its net assets to public debt assets (to be managed by the investment adviser) and approximately 40% to private credit assets (to be managed by the sub-adviser)"; the adviser is Capital Research and Management Company and the sub-adviser KKR Credit Advisors (US) LLC, paid "solely by the investment adviser out of the investment adviser's fees" [S426] [Confirmed]. The minimum is "normally $1,000 per account", the management fee 0.61%, and total expenses after a 0.30% waiver range from 0.84% (F-3, R-6) to 1.54% (A-2) [S426] [Confirmed].

U.S. Equity+: a fund that owns K-PEC

Capital Group KKR U.S. Equity+ is also an interval fund, with "approximately 60% of its net assets in publicly traded equity securities and approximately 40% of its net assets in private equity securities". 5. Liquidity mismatch and the toolkit

The core problem of every semi-liquid fund in one sentence: the assets take years to sell, but the investors can ask for money every quarter. If everyone asks at once, the fund must either sell assets cheaply (hurting those who stay), pay some investors and not others, or say no. Each tool below is a rule decided in advance about which of those happens [Inferred].

The tools one by one

  • Gate. A cap on total exits per period. Without it, the first investors to leave get cash from the liquid sleeve and those who stay end up owning only illiquid assets [Inferred].
  • Pro-rata. If requests exceed the gate, everyone gets the same fraction. In the crowded quarter worked above, 62.5%.
  • Redemption fee. Charged to leavers and kept in the fund. It compensates stayers for the cost of raising cash and discourages short-term money [S420] [Inferred].
  • Liquidity sleeve. Cash and tradable credit held to meet exits; it drags returns in good times, which is why it is capped at about 15 to 20% in the K-Series [S420, S421] [Confirmed].
  • NAV lag. K-PEC strikes NAV monthly from Level III models and deals at the prior month-end price [S420]. If markets fall sharply mid-month, a leaver may exit at a stale, higher price. Funds that trade on NAV are exactly where operational due diligence focuses: Cambridge Associates stresses open-end and evergreen funds "where transactions are based on the fund's NAV" and that "Fee calculations rely on accurate valuations" [S458] [Confirmed].

The live example: K-FIT's queue in Q1 2026

In Q1 2026 investors in K-FIT "tried to redeem 6.3% of shares in the prior quarter, causing the fund to prorate requests at about 80%"; in Q2 requests fell to 1.65% and were paid in full [S338] [Reported]. The arithmetic checks: 5% cap ÷ 6.3% requested = 79% [Inferred]. It was part of an industry wave: private credit redemption requests topped $20bn in Q1 2026 [S337] [Reported]. Apollo Debt Solutions received requests of 11.2% and honoured 5%, Blue Owl enforced a 5% cap, and Blackstone's BCRED paid 100% of record requests of 7.9% of NAV [S278] [Reported]. Partners Group said requests at its Delaware private equity evergreen fund would be "slightly above the 5%" limit [S339] [Reported].

Two lessons for a client team. First, a gate working as designed is not a default: it is the contract doing what it said. Second, the reputational cost lands on whoever set expectations at the point of sale [Inferred]. KCS juniors are the people who draft the investor letters and adviser FAQs when a queue forms [Inferred].

The European and UK rulebooks

AIFMD II (EU). Open-ended alternative investment funds must select "at least two appropriate LMTs" (liquidity management tools) from points 2 to 8 of Annex V: redemption gates, extension of notice periods, redemption fees, swing pricing, dual pricing, anti-dilution levies and redemption in kind. Suspension and side pockets sit outside the count as exceptional tools. "This cannot be done by selecting only dual and swing pricing", and redemptions in kind are for professional investors only [S449] [Confirmed] [S450] [Reported]. The Level 2 rules (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/465) apply from 16 April 2026; funds constituted before that date are "deemed to comply with the RTS until 16 April 2027" [S449] [Confirmed]. ESMA recommends, but does not require, "at least one quantitative LMT and one Anti-Dilution Tool" [S448] [Confirmed]. Worked example (illustrative): gate plus notice period is a valid pair; swing pricing plus dual pricing is not.

ELTIF 2.0 (EU). The redemption rules (Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/2759, in force 26 October 2024) set no fixed minimum holding period; redemptions more often than quarterly must be justified; a notice period under 3 months must be explained to the regulator; and matching exiting with incoming investors is allowed under a policy [S447] [Confirmed]. The maximum redemption is sized by one of two methods [S447] [Confirmed]:

MethodRule from the RTS exampleWorked: €500m fund with €100m of liquid assets (illustrative)
Annex I: frequency + notice periodQuarterly with 3 months' notice: up to 33.3% of liquid assets0.333 × €100m = €33.3m a quarter, 6.7% of NAV
Annex II: frequency + minimum liquid assetsQuarterly with at least 20% liquid assets: up to 50%0.50 × €100m = €50m a quarter, 10% of NAV

The liquid-asset base also includes cash flows forecast prudently over 12 months [S447] [Confirmed]. The ELTIF market reached about €34bn at end-2025 with 189 new ELTIFs since 2024 [S302] [Reported].

Source uncertain

UK LTAF. Secondary sources describe the Long-Term Asset Fund as open-ended with dealing no more often than monthly and a notice period of at least 90 days [S451] [Reported]; the FCA rulebook (COLL 15) itself was not read. The LTAF has been open to retail investors as a Restricted Mass Market Investment since 3 July 2023 [S305] [Confirmed]. An FCA speech in May 2026 put LTAF assets at £7.5bn, up from £5bn in under a year [S452] [Reported]. No KKR ELTIF or LTAF was found in the sources; EQT launched an evergreen infrastructure ELTIF in April 2026 [S464] [Reported].

6. The same strategy, three wrappers: KKR infrastructure

Put everything together on one asset class. KKR manages about $120bn of infrastructure equity, "up from just $13 billion in 2019", with about 160 dedicated infrastructure executives [S430] [Confirmed]. The same team sells that exposure through three very different wrappers. KKR itself counts them together: GII V contributes to "approximately $45 billion raised across KKR's latest infrastructure vehicle vintages globally", a figure that includes infrastructure perpetual vehicles such as K-INFRA [S430] [Confirmed].

The three wrappers, row by row

The full-page figure below sets them side by side; here is how to read it.

Who each serves. GII V is for institutions that can commit for a decade and manage capital calls; its investor list includes "private wealth platforms" too, which typically enter through feeders [S430] [Confirmed] (feeder route [Inferred]). DCIF is for institutions that want steady, yield-like infrastructure with "low volatility and strong downside protection" and are willing to accept a three-year hard lock [S432] [Confirmed]. K-INFRA is for individuals who need a monthly price and some quarterly liquidity [S421] [Confirmed].

Why the fees differ. The institutional open-ended core fund charges 0.6% to 0.8% with carry only on cash yield; the wealth vehicle charges 1.25% plus 12.5% over 5% [S421, S432] [Confirmed]. The wealth price pays for things institutions do themselves: monthly valuation and administration, the liquidity sleeve, adviser education, and distribution through platforms [Inferred]. KKR's own fee table shows the same split across products: open-ended vehicles at 0.50% to 1.25% versus drawdown infrastructure at 0.75% to 1.50% [S2] [Confirmed].

Why the performance fee is lower than K-PEC's. K-INFRA's 12.5% versus K-PEC's 15% matches the lower expected return of infrastructure [S421] [Confirmed] (interpretation [Inferred]). K-INFRA's 2025 Class I return was 10.13% against K-PEC's 16.81% [S420, S421] [Confirmed].

Why it matters to a client conversation. A family office asking "should I buy KKR infrastructure?" is really asking three questions: can you meet capital calls for six years, can you accept a multi-year lock, or do you need a quarterly door? The answer picks the wrapper, and the wrapper sets the fee [Inferred].

What you will learn

Module 1 in one paragraph

A private markets product is a contract about four things: who may buy, when cash moves, how fees are charged and how you get out. Drawdown funds answer with commitments, capital calls, a fee on committed capital and a whole-of-fund waterfall with a clawback. Evergreen funds answer with a monthly NAV, a fee on NAV, a hurdle with catch-up and high-water mark, and a narrow quarterly door. Interval funds add a legal promise of liquidity and pay for it with a large public sleeve. When too many people head for the door, gates, pro-rating, fees and notice periods decide who bears the cost. KKR runs every one of these pipes, and often runs them into the same deals [Inferred].
