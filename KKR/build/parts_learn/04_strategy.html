A1. What infrastructure is, technically

What you will learn

To describe any infrastructure asset by five properties (how it is paid, how it tracks inflation, how long it lasts, how much capital it eats, and who carries the price risk), and to tell a concession from a merchant asset in one sentence.

KKR's research files describe infrastructure as "essential physical assets (power, data centres, roads, fibre)" that earn "long-term contracted or regulated cash flows" [S269] [Reported]. That phrase hides five separate technical properties. An LP (limited partner, the investor in a fund) will test each one.

PropertyPlain EnglishWhy an investor cares
1. Contracted or regulated revenueSomeone has agreed in advance what the asset will be paid: a regulator, a government, or one big customer (the "offtaker") on a long contract.Cash flows can be forecast years ahead, so the asset can carry more debt and the equity is less volatile [Inferred].

Key terms

Concession: a government grants a company the right to build or run an asset (a toll road, an airport) for a fixed number of years, then the asset goes back to the state. Merchant: the asset sells its output (electricity, capacity) at whatever the market pays that day, with no long contract. Offtaker: the customer who buys the output under a contract. Contract for Difference (CfD): a UK government contract that tops up a power generator's income when the market price is below an agreed "strike price" and takes back the excess when the market is above it. The CfD explanation is a general description [Inferred]; that Norfolk Vanguard won CfDs is [S357] [Reported].

A2. The risk ladder: core, core-plus, value-add, and KKR's vehicles on it

What you will learn

The industry's four-rung ladder for infrastructure risk, and how KKR uses different vehicles (an open-ended core fund, a drawdown flagship, a climate fund and an evergreen wealth vehicle) to sell different rungs to different clients.

Infrastructure managers sort strategies by how much of the return comes from steady cash today versus growth and building tomorrow. Core means operating assets with contracted or regulated income: most of the return is cash yield. Core-plus adds some growth (expansion, re-contracting, bolt-ons). Value-add takes construction, repositioning or platform-building risk. Opportunistic or development means building new assets or businesses where most of the return is capital gain. A4. How an infrastructure asset makes money: cash yield versus total return

What you will learn

To build an equity cash flow from revenue down (the "waterfall"), to compute cash yield and debt service cover, and to split a total return into the part paid in cash and the part paid at exit.

Step 1: the cash-flow anatomy of a wind farm

Every infrastructure asset is valued from one ladder of deductions. Start with revenue. Take off operating costs to reach EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: the cash the asset makes from running). Take off tax and maintenance capex to reach CFADS (cash flow available for debt service). Pay the lenders interest and scheduled principal. What remains belongs to equity. Lenders watch the DSCR (debt service cover ratio):

DSCR = CFADS ÷ (interest + scheduled principal)

A DSCR of 1.38x means the asset earns 38% more cash than it needs to pay its lenders that year. If DSCR falls below a level set in the loan, cash is "locked up" and equity gets nothing until it recovers. This is general project finance practice [Inferred].

Step 2: worked example, cash yield versus total return (illustrative numbers)

Worked example: a contracted asset held for ten years (illustrative numbers)

Buy. Enterprise value £1,200m. Debt £720m (60%) at 5.5%, repaid £20m a year. Equity cheque £480m.

Year 1. EBITDA £90m. Tax and maintenance capex £15m. Interest 5.5% × £720m = £39.6m. Principal £20m. Cash to equity = 90 - 15 - 39.6 - 20 = £15.4m.

Cash yield = cash to equity ÷ equity invested = 15.4 ÷ 480 = 3.2%.

Years 2 to 10. The contract is indexed, so EBITDA and costs grow 2.5% a year. Debt falls, so interest falls. By year 10 cash to equity is £44.0m, a 9.2% yield on the original cheque. Average over ten years: 6.1%.

Exit. Year 11 EBITDA £115.2m × 12 (the same multiple as entry, rounded down slightly) = £1,382m. Less remaining debt £520m = £862m to equity.

Total return. The internal rate of return (IRR: the single annual rate that makes all cash in equal all cash out in today's money) on -480 then the yearly cash then +862 is 10.5%. If the exit only returned the £480m cheque, the IRR would be 5.8%. So about 5.8 points come from cash and about 4.7 from growth plus debt paydown.

This split explains real KKR documents. K-INFRA's 10-K warns that distributions "may and are expected to exceed our earnings and cash flow from operating activities" [S421] [Confirmed]. In the example, early-year cash yield is low (3.2%) while value is building, so a vehicle promising a steady payout must sometimes pay out more than the assets yield that year [Inferred]. And DCIF's carry on "asset level cash yield" above 4% [S432] pays the manager only for the blue part of the bar [Inferred].

Step 3: the data centre version of the same story

That curve is why a fund like GII V and a platform like Helix can own the same kind of asset and still be different products. The investor who funds the trough earns the climb; the investor who buys at the stabilised stage earns the yield [Inferred]. KKR's earlier $50bn partnership with Energy Capital Partners for data centres and power generation [S35] [Reported] and Helix's focus on "power and connectivity" [S3] [Confirmed] show that power supply, not buildings, is now the binding constraint KKR talks about [Inferred].

B1. Asset-based finance: lending against pools, not companies

What you will learn

What a pool of loans or receivables is, how it is cut into tranches, why amortising cash flows differ from a direct loan, and how to compute who loses what when defaults rise.

KKR defines asset-based finance (ABF) as "private credit investments backed by large, diversified pools of hard or financial assets", and adds that "private ABF is considered non-corporate credit" [S434] [Confirmed]. Translation: you do not lend to one company and hope it survives. You lend against thousands of small loans or leases (car loans, aircraft leases, royalties, development loans) whose combined repayments pay you back [Inferred]. K-FIT's 10-K gives KKR's own examples: "auto loans, royalties, aircraft leasing, real estate development lending" [S422] [Confirmed].

Worked example: a $100m auto-loan pool, tranched (illustrative numbers)

Worked example: who gets paid, who loses (illustrative numbers)

The pool. $100m of car loans yielding 11% a year. Expected annual losses 2.5%. Servicing (collecting payments, chasing arrears) costs 1.5%. Net pool yield = 11 - 2.5 - 1.5 = 7.0%, or $7.0m in year one.

The tranches. Senior $80m paid 5.5% ($4.40m). Mezzanine $10m paid 8.5% ($0.85m). Equity (first loss) $10m gets whatever is left: 7.0 - 4.40 - 0.85 = $1.75m, a 17.5% return on $10m.

Stress. If losses rise to 5%, net yield is 4.5% ($4.5m). Senior still gets $4.40m. Mezzanine gets only $0.10m of its $0.85m. Equity gets nothing and starts absorbing principal losses: roughly -7.5% that year. The senior investor is untouched until losses eat through the $20m below it.

The formula in words. Credit enhancement for a tranche = the share of the pool below it. Senior here has 20% enhancement: pool losses must exceed 20% of principal before it loses a dollar.

KKR's market case: "We estimate the private ABF market today is larger than the syndicated loan, high yield bond, and direct lending markets combined, and it could reach approximately $9.2 trillion by 2029" (KKR and Integer Advisors data as of 31 March 2024) [S434] [Confirmed]. The reason it is private: "Post-crisis regulation and resulting higher capital requirements have reduced banks' risk appetites" [S434] [Confirmed]. KKR began ABF in 2016 [S433] [Reported] and closed Asset-Based Finance Partners II in July 2025 at $5.6bn plus nearly $1bn of separately managed accounts, $6.5bn in total, more than twice Fund I [S433] [Reported]. KKR's 2026 mid-year outlook says "collateral-based cash flows (asset-based finance, real estate credit, structured solutions) matter more" [S360] [Confirmed].

B2. Why insurers want ABF, and how it reaches Core Plus+

What you will learn

The two reasons an insurer buys private credit (capital and matching), the regulatory factors that decide it, and the plumbing that puts KKR's private credit inside a Capital Group fund with a $1,000 minimum.

Reason 1: capital

A US life insurer must hold capital (risk-based capital, RBC) against each asset. KKR's 10-K says "the determination of RBC is based on the NAIC designation of the assets in which Global Atlantic invests", and the designation "depends on the applicable NRSRO rating" (a recognised credit rating agency) [S425] [Confirmed]. So an insurer wants assets that are rated investment grade, so the capital charge is low, but pay more than a public bond of the same rating [Inferred]. Reason 2: matching

An annuity book pays policyholders a little every month for years. Assets that also pay back principal every month (the left shape in the ABF figure) match those outflows better than a bullet loan [Inferred]. KKR's 2026 Insurance CIO Survey (about 50 US insurers, nearly $2.6tn of assets) lists insurers' concerns as "Asset-liability matching, illiquidity limits, regulatory capital, reinvestment risk", and says "Every major Private Credit category is a net add in the survey, led by Asset-Based Finance" [S438] [Confirmed]. Global Atlantic itself had $220bn of AUM at 30 June 2026, of which $164bn is credit and $62bn sits in Ivy and other sponsored reinsurance vehicles [S1] [Confirmed].

Worked example: same rating, more spread, same capital (illustrative numbers)

An insurer holds $100m of A-rated assets. A public A-rated corporate bond pays 1.0% over government bonds. A senior ABF tranche, also rated A, pays 1.8% over (illustrative; the extra is payment for complexity and illiquidity). If the capital factor is the same for both ratings, the insurer earns an extra 0.8% × $100m = $0.8m a year for no extra capital. Across Global Atlantic's $164bn of credit [S1], every 0.1% of extra spread is about $164m a year [Inferred]. That is why spreads, ratings and NAIC rules are KCS conversation topics with insurers.

The plumbing into Capital Group KKR Core Plus+

Core Plus+ is an interval fund (a fund that must offer to buy back a set share of its units every quarter) that allocates "approximately 60% of its net assets to public debt assets (to be managed by the investment adviser) and approximately 40% to private credit assets (to be managed by the sub-adviser)" [S426] [Confirmed]. The private credit is direct lending and asset-based finance [S92] [Confirmed]. KKR Credit Advisors (US) is the sub-adviser and is "paid solely by the investment adviser out of the investment adviser's fees" [S426] [Confirmed].

Compare the promise with KKR's own evergreen funds. An interval fund must make the repurchase offer every quarter under a "fundamental policy" changeable only by shareholder vote; a K-Series board may repurchase less or nothing [S426, S420] [Confirmed]; the contrast is the author's [Inferred]. Firmer liquidity requires more liquid assets, which is why the private sleeve is only about 40% [Inferred].
