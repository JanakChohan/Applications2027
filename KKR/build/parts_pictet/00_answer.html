Part 0

The question, and three answers built from your Pictet seat

"What previous internship experience do you have that will be useful for this internship?" 1,000 characters.

What the question is really testing

The reader wants proof of three things: that you have done real client work, that you understood it rather than just did it, and that you can see how it maps onto KCS. Most applicants list tasks ("I helped with onboarding and client queries"). A strong answer turns each task into a lesson and each lesson into a KKR use, and is honest about what is new [Inferred].

Did
Name the real tasks: servicing, onboarding, relationship management. Only the ones you did.
Learned
Say what each taught you about how a client firm works. One line each.
Transfers
Tie it to KCS: a product (K-PEC), a task (DDQs, onboarding LPs), or a client type (private banks).
The honesty rule (read before you edit)

These drafts use the three areas you said you worked in: client servicing, client onboarding and client relationship management. Some phrases describe tasks that such work normally includes (KYC documents, share class eligibility, CRM records, platform queries). Delete any task you did not personally do, and swap in what you did. An interviewer will ask "talk me through an onboarding you worked on", and the answer must come from memory, not from this guide. Public sources do not use the name "Global Client Group", so the drafts say "client group"; if your contract or email signature used the full name, use it (see Part 1).

Three answers, each under 1,000 characters

Version A: the technical bridge (recommended) · 960 characters including spaces

At Pictet Asset Management I spent six weeks in the client group, working across client servicing, onboarding and relationship management for institutions and intermediaries investing in Pictet's Luxembourg UCITS funds. Onboarding taught me that nothing happens until the detail is right: classifying the client, checking KYC documents and share class eligibility, and chasing each step until the account could trade. Servicing taught me speed with accuracy: answering client queries and keeping CRM records clean, so the next conversation starts from the right place. Above all, I saw that a relationship rests on the teams behind it: sales, product specialists, operations and compliance. KCS is built the same way, around products that work differently. A UCITS fund deals daily; KKR's K-PEC strikes NAV monthly and limits repurchases to 5% a quarter. Explaining that difference clearly to a private bank is where my Pictet experience gives me a head start.

Version B: the personal and partnership angle · 894 characters including spaces

My six weeks in Pictet Asset Management's client group were my first look at how a fund manager earns a client's trust day to day. I worked on onboarding, servicing and relationship management, and learned that the unglamorous parts matter most: a missing KYC document stops an account, a wrong figure in a reply undoes a good meeting, an untidy CRM record costs the next person time. I also saw how a partnership thinks long term. Pictet has been owned by its partners since 1805, and its client group is built for relationships that last decades, which is why KKR's "like and trust" resonates with me. What I want to add is the private markets side. Pictet is moving that way itself, with an ELTIF and a $1.5bn co-investment fund, while KKR is bringing private assets to wealth clients through the K-Series. I would bring a traditional manager's client discipline to a team doing the reverse.

Version C: three skills, cleanly structured · 930 characters including spaces

Three things from my six weeks in Pictet Asset Management's client group carry straight into KCS. First, onboarding: how a new client becomes investable, from KYC and client categorisation to share class eligibility, and why one missing document holds up everything. KCS onboards LPs and wealth distributors with the same discipline, using subscription documents and investor eligibility tests. Second, servicing: answering client and platform queries quickly and accurately, and keeping CRM records clean, the daily rhythm of any client team. Third, working across teams: seeing sales, product specialists, operations and compliance serve one client, which is what KCS means by bringing all of KKR to a client. The big difference is the product. Pictet's UCITS funds deal daily; KKR's evergreen funds price monthly with quarterly repurchase limits, and drawdown funds call capital over years. That is the side I am keen to learn.

The follow-up questions this answer invites

"Walk me through one onboarding you worked on."
Tests: was it real?

Scaffold: client type, what was missing or tricky, who you went to, how it was resolved, how long it took, what you would do differently. Use a real case with names removed.

"What is the difference between a retail and a professional client, and why does it matter?"
Tests: technical understanding.

Under COBS 3, a professional client (per se, such as a pension fund or bank, or elective, if tests are met) gets less protection and can access more complex products and institutional share classes; retail clients get the most protection [S543]. At KKR, the same logic decides who may be offered a private fund [Inferred].

"Why would a client accept a 5% quarterly limit on getting their money out?"
Tests: whether you understand the KKR side.

Because the assets underneath are private companies that cannot be sold quickly; the gate protects investors who stay from fire sales. In exchange they get private market exposure without capital calls [S420] [Inferred]. Then the duty: explain it before they invest, which is what UK Consumer Duty calls consumer understanding [S542].

"Pictet is a traditional manager. Why should that help you at KKR?"
Tests: whether you can turn a perceived weakness into a strength.

"The clients overlap: pensions, insurers and private banks. KKR is selling private assets to wealth clients that have mostly bought daily-dealing funds, so knowing how those clients and platforms work is useful. And the two worlds are converging: Pictet now runs an ELTIF and co-investment funds, KKR runs interval funds with Capital Group" [S531, S529, S426].

"What did you find hardest at Pictet?"
Tests: self-awareness.

Pick a true, specific difficulty (learning a dealing calendar, chasing a document across time zones, getting a factsheet number checked). Say what you changed. Never pick "nothing".
