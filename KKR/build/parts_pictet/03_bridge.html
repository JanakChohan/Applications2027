The bridge: from Pictet's client group to KKR Client Solutions

Same job family, different product. This part lays the two firms side by side, then walks every bridge a client-group intern can cross: product, onboarding, servicing, relationship and culture, with the gaps stated honestly.

You spent about six weeks in the client group (the candidate's team name) at Pictet Asset Management in London, the team that services, onboards and manages relationships with clients. KKR's question asks what in that experience will be useful in KKR Global Client Solutions (KCS). The honest answer is not "I already know private markets". It is "I know how a client business works end to end in a daily-dealing world, and I know exactly which mechanics change when the product becomes a drawdown or evergreen private fund". This part builds that answer from structure, one bridge at a time [Inferred].

Say this plainly: there is no known Pictet and KKR link

The research found no public evidence that Pictet distributes KKR products, that Pictet Alternative Advisors invests in KKR funds, or that the two firms have done deals together. Pictet does not publish its list of private equity managers [S535] [Confirmed]. Pictet Wealth Management's third-party fund shelf is not public, and searches of KKR wealth news and Pictet's US filings found no link [S550, S556] [Confirmed]. So never say "Pictet works with KKR". The bridge in this part is built from structure: similar clients, a similar client function, and a product gap that KCS exists to explain [Inferred].

1. Two client businesses, side by side

Start with the facts. Both firms sell investment products to institutions and to wealthy individuals through intermediaries. Both have a dedicated client-facing team. They differ in who owns the firm, how big it is, and above all in what the product does with the client's cash. The figure below puts eleven dimensions on one page so you can see the pattern before the detail.

Five contrasts that matter in an interview

  1. Ownership. Pictet is a partnership in law and in name: the Managing Partners are the "significant equity owners" [S490] [Confirmed], and a December 2024 interview reposted by Pictet described "no external shareholders, no debt and no listing" [S536] [Reported]. KKR is a listed corporation, but it keeps partnership-style economics: one P&L, one compensation pool and about 30% employee ownership [S2, S5] [Confirmed].   2. Size. KKR's $796bn of AUM [S74] is a little over twice Pictet AM's USD 354bn [S504] [Confirmed] (ratio [Inferred]). But KKR's figure includes $220bn of Global Atlantic insurance assets [S175] [Confirmed].
  3. Product. Pictet AM's core is the open-ended, daily-dealing UCITS fund [S527] [Confirmed]. KKR's core is the closed-end drawdown fund plus, since 2023, the evergreen K-Series [S70, S73] [Confirmed]. This one difference drives almost every other difference in the client job [Inferred].
  4. Clients. Overlap is real: pension funds, insurers, financial institutions and intermediaries appear in both firms' descriptions [S491, S70] [Confirmed]. KKR now gets 18% of its AUM from private wealth, more than from pensions (14%) [S71] [Confirmed].

2. The product bridge: three clocks for the same money

A product, technically, is a set of rules about cash: when it goes in, at what price, what it costs each year, and when and how it comes out. Every client conversation you had at Pictet sat on top of one set of rules, the UCITS rules. KCS sits on top of two other sets: the drawdown rules and the evergreen rules. The next figure shows the three as clocks that tick at different speeds.

Key terms

UCITS: the EU's regulated open-ended fund format for the general public; Pictet's Luxembourg funds are UCITS [S524] [Confirmed]. NAV (net asset value): the fund's assets minus its debts, divided by the number of shares; the price you deal at. Forward pricing: you place an order now and deal at the next NAV, which nobody knows yet [S527] [Inferred]. Drawdown fund: investors promise ("commit") money now; the manager asks for it ("calls" it) over several years as it finds deals [Inferred]. Evergreen fund: no end date; investors subscribe periodically at NAV and can ask for limited repurchases [S70] [Confirmed].

Forward pricing at Pictet, and its cousin at K-PEC

At Pictet you learned that a client never buys "2,500 shares at today's price". The client sends an amount of money before the cut-off, and the transfer agent (the firm that keeps the fund's register of shareholders) works out the share count after the next NAV is struck. Miss the cut-off by five minutes and the order rolls to the next Valuation Day [S527] [Confirmed] (cut-off rule; the mechanics are [Inferred]). K-PEC works on the same principle with a monthly clock: "An investor subscribes a dollar amount and only learns the share count afterwards", and a subscription accepted on 1 September is priced at the 31 August Transactional NAV [S420] [Confirmed]. The figure below puts the two side by side.

Liquidity tools: from suspension to the 5% cap

A UCITS promises daily dealing, so its main emergency tool is a suspension. Pictet's board suspended NAV calculation and dealing in Pictet - Russian Equities in February 2022 [S527] [Reported]. K-PEC builds the brake into normal life. Repurchases are quarterly and capped at "no more than 5.0% of our aggregate NAV ... per calendar quarter"; the board can buy back less or nothing; shares redeemed within 24 months pay a 5.0% Early Repurchase Fee, kept by the fund for the remaining holders; if requests exceed 5%, the board may tender for the excess "at or about a 10% discount to the most recent NAV" [S420] [Confirmed]. In Europe, AIFMD II now requires open-ended alternative funds to pick at least two liquidity management tools from a list that includes gates, notice periods and redemption fees [S449] [Confirmed]. The next figure shows what the 5% cap does to one client's request.

Share classes: the same decoding skill, a different fee stack

At Pictet, a share-class name is a compressed label. On UK platforms you see "Pictet-Water P dy GBP" and "I dy GBP": "P" is a retail-type class, "I" institutional, "dy" distributing, and the suffix is the currency [S524, S526] [Confirmed]. Exact minimums sit in the prospectus, which the research did not read (research gap). K-PEC also has letters, but each letter mostly encodes how the seller is paid. Class S carries an upfront sales load up to 3.00% plus a 0.50% dealer-manager fee (cap 3.50%); Classes S and U pay 0.85% a year in distribution and servicing fees; D pays 0.25%; I pays none. All four pay a 1.25% management fee [S420] [Confirmed]. The figure below decodes both.

What the work really is

Product knowledge in a client team means knowing four rules for each product: entry (price and timing), cost (every fee layer), exit (frequency, caps, penalties) and eligibility (who may buy which class). At Pictet those rules were daily and stable. At KKR they vary by wrapper, and the client team must explain each one before money moves [Inferred].

A London KCS junior supporting the evergreen range would answer exactly the dealing, class and repurchase questions above. The numbers, step by step

K-PEC Class I, EUR 1m (illustrative)

Management fee. 1.25% x EUR 1,000,000 = EUR 12,500 a year if NAV stays flat (charged monthly on month-end NAV) [S420].

Performance allocation, good year. Total return 10% = EUR 100,000. The investor first gets the 5% hurdle (EUR 50,000). Then KKR takes 100% ("catch-up") until it holds 15% of everything paid so far, then 15% of the rest. Because 10% clears the hurdle comfortably, KKR ends with 15% of the whole return: EUR 15,000; the investor keeps EUR 85,000.

Weak year. Return 5.5% = EUR 55,000. The investor gets 50,000; the next 5,000 all goes to KKR as catch-up, which is not complete (15% of 55,000 would be 8,250). So KKR gets EUR 5,000 [Inferred] (arithmetic; high-water mark and loss carryforward ignored).

Early exit at month 18. 5% x 1,000,000 = EUR 50,000 kept by the fund [S420].

Same order in Class S. Up to 3.50% upfront (up to EUR 35,000) plus 0.85% a year (EUR 8,500) on top of the 1.25% [S420].

GII V-style commitment, EUR 1m (illustrative)

Calls. Year 1: 200k; Year 2: 250k; Year 3: 200k; Year 4: 150k; Year 5: 100k. Total called: 900k (90%); 100k never called.

Distributions. Years 4 to 11: 50k, 100k, 150k, 200k, 250k, 250k, 200k, 150k. Total: 1,350k, so 1.5x the cash called.

Net cash out (calls minus distributions, cumulative): 200k, 450k, 650k, 750k, 750k, 600k, 400k, 150k, then the client is net positive from Year 9.

The lesson. The client never has the full EUR 1m out at once: peak net exposure here is EUR 750k. That is why institutions "over-commit" and run pacing models [S454, S455] [Reported], and why the KKR TPA paper notes private market distributions have historically averaged about "10-30% of NAV" a year [S436] [Confirmed].

Fee. At an illustrative 1.0% (inside the 10-K's 0.75% to 1.50% range [S2]) on commitment: EUR 10,000 a year during the investment period.

What the work really is

Walking one order through three wrappers is exactly what a client servicing team does when a client asks "what happens to my money?". The skill is to separate what is a rule (cut-off, cap, fee), what is a judgement (board discretion, valuation) and what is unknown (terms not public) [Inferred].

How it transfers to KKR KCS

KKR's GWS internal sales posting asks for "depth of expertise across alternative assets classes in both private and registered structures (interval funds, BDCs, REITs)" [S103] [Confirmed]. Comparing wrappers for one order is that expertise in miniature. The technical guide covers each wrapper in full (Module 1, sections 2 to 4).

3. The onboarding bridge: from account opening to subscription documents

Onboarding answers three questions in a fixed order: may we deal with this client, who exactly are they, and is this product right for them. A UCITS client group answers them with MiFID client categorisation, KYC and anti-money-laundering checks, tax status, and target market data. A private fund team answers them with marketing permissions, investor eligibility tests, due diligence, subscription documents and side letters. The figure below lines the two processes up.

The eligibility tests, with their numbers

At Pictet the key question is "which category is this client in?", because categorisation decides which share classes and documents a client can get [S543] [Confirmed] (the consequence is [Inferred]). In private markets the question gets sharper: some clients may not be offered the product at all. The next figure sets the four tests you are most likely to meet in KCS London next to each other.

Side letters: the part of onboarding a UCITS never has

A UCITS investor accepts the prospectus as written. A large LP in a drawdown fund often negotiates a side letter: a private agreement that adds rights such as fee breaks, extra reporting, ESG terms, co-investment rights or excuse rights [Inferred]. A most-favoured-nation (MFN) clause then "typically entitles an investor to elect the benefit of rights contained in other investors' side letters"; sponsors "require MFN elections to be submitted within a certain period following closing", and "Late MFN election submissions are at risk of being rejected" [S456] [Confirmed]. Notice the job inside that sentence: a deadline, a list of elections, and a client who must not miss it. That is a servicing task with the same shape as a dealing cut-off [Inferred].

What the work really is

Onboarding is a control process: classify, verify, document, approve, then let money move. The Swiss Attorney General's CHF 2m fine on Banque Pictet in June 2025 turned on failing to classify accounts as high risk and to monitor 54 transfers [S498] [Confirmed], which shows the stakes are real. The private-fund version adds legal negotiation and a commitment that lasts a decade [Inferred].

How it transfers to KKR KCS

The KKR EMEA Institutional Client Relations Analyst (London) is asked to partner "with clients to help with KYC & investment onboarding" and to collaborate with "Operations, Finance, Legal, Compliance, Risk" [S101] [Confirmed]. KKR's 10-K notes that "Financial crime laws may limit our investment and capital raising activities" [S70] [Confirmed]. KYC discipline from a UCITS seat is directly relevant; subscription documents, eligibility tests and side letters are what you add.

Say it in one line

"I learned onboarding as a control process at Pictet; at KKR the same controls sit inside subscription documents, investor eligibility tests and side letters." [Inferred]

5. The relationship bridge: themes, wealth channels and convergence

Selling a theme: Pictet megatrends and KKR's thematic view

Pictet AM's flagship is thematic equity: about USD 70bn across 16 strategies built on 14 themes (31 Mar 2025), and Pictet's claim to be "the largest active thematic fund provider by AUM globally" [S505] [Confirmed] (Pictet's own claim). Pictet tracks 21 megatrends [S537], and Pictet-Water (launched January 2000) was among the first water funds [S506, S524] [Confirmed]. A client group sells these funds by explaining a long-run story (water scarcity, robotics, security) and then showing how a fund captures it [Inferred].

KKR also talks in themes, but it buys whole assets rather than listed shares. Its 2026 Outlook lists mega-themes including "Security of Everything" and "Productivity and Worker Retraining" [S361] [Confirmed]; CFO Robert Lewin named megatrends in "AI, digital and energy infrastructure, defence" as a growth driver in July 2026 [S3] [Confirmed]. KKR's Total Portfolio Approach (TPA) then asks the client a bigger question: treat portfolio construction like "zero-based budgeting ... if every investment had to earn its place in the portfolio today, how would capital be allocated?" [S436] [Confirmed]. A theme sells one fund; TPA sells a relationship across many [S435, S436] [Inferred].

The wealth channel: Pictet is the bank; KKR sells to the bank

Pictet is unusual. The same group owns an asset manager (Pictet AM, CHF 267bn) and a private bank (Pictet Wealth Management, CHF 285bn, 346 private bankers) [S491] [Confirmed]. KKR has no private bank. Its Global Wealth Solutions (GWS) team, part of KCS, sells K-Series and other products through "wirehouses, independent broker-dealers, registered investment advisers, global private banks, and family offices" [S70] [Confirmed]. In the UK, Quilter Cheviot began offering KKR's evergreen private equity strategy from January 2026; Markus Egloff (MD, London, head of GWS for EMEA and Asia Pacific) called it "an important milestone in our efforts to deliver greater access to KKR's strategies" [S177] [Confirmed] (quote as returned by a fetch tool). The release names the strategy, not the vehicle, in the research extract; K-PRIME is KKR's Luxembourg evergreen PE fund, product-managed from London [S133] [Confirmed]. KKR opened a Zurich office in December 2023 to reach Swiss private banks and wealth managers [S553] [Reported].

Convergence: the two firms are walking toward each other

Pictet is moving into private markets: private debt in Pictet AM since November 2022 [S532], an environmental co-investment ELTIF launched in April 2024 [S531], its sixth co-investment fund closed at USD 1.53bn in June 2026 [S529] and its first direct private equity fund at EUR 403m in March 2026 [S530] [Confirmed]. KKR is moving toward wealth and liquidity: K-Series grew from $6bn (2023) to $42bn (June 2026) [S72, S73]; Capital Group KKR interval funds blend about 60% public and 40% private credit with quarterly repurchases of up to 10% and a $1,000 minimum [S552, S426] [Confirmed]. In September 2026 Bloomberg reported that KKR had passed Partners Group as Europe's largest manager of European-domiciled evergreen private markets funds, at EUR 13.6bn (Q1 2026); Egloff: "We recently became the largest manager of evergreen private markets funds in Europe" [S550] [Confirmed].

Pictet's own view on that middle ground is worth knowing. Laurent Ramsey, a Pictet Managing Partner, said in December 2024: "you need to be careful when making something illiquid liquid", noting that semi-liquid funds typically allow "3-5% redemption per quarter", while also calling them a possible stepping stone into private markets [S536] [Reported]. Partners Group saw $3.8bn of redemptions against $4.2bn of evergreen demand in H1 2026 [S239] [Confirmed]; Scott Nuttall said K-Series was "up 20% net year-to-date" [S76] [Reported]. A Pictet alumnus who can hold both views at once (the product works, and the liquidity limits must be explained before the sale) is useful to KCS [Inferred].

What the work really is

Relationship management is matching a client's goals and constraints to products, then keeping the match true over time. A thematic fund is matched to a belief; a private markets programme is matched to a whole portfolio, its liquidity needs and its pacing [Inferred].

Your Pictet experience is from the manufacturer side of a firm that also owns a private bank; at KKR you would be on the manufacturer side selling to banks [Inferred].

7. What you have seen that most applicants have not, and what you have not

Most KCS applicants describe client work in general terms.
