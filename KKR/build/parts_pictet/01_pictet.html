Pictet from zero: the firm you worked for

What Pictet is, who owns it, how it makes money, what its asset management arm sells and to whom, and the parts of its record you should know before an interviewer does.

KKR's question is "What previous internship experience do you have that will be useful for this internship?" A good answer starts with one confident sentence about where you were. You cannot write that sentence until you understand the firm better than the person reading it. This part gives you that understanding, from first principles, with every fact sourced.

What kind of firm Pictet is

Pictet is a Geneva-based group that does four related jobs for other people's money: it advises wealthy families, it runs investment funds, it invests in private assets, and it holds and administers assets for other financial firms [S491] [Confirmed]. It says plainly what it does not do: "The Pictet Group does not engage in investment banking, nor does it extend commercial loans" [S490] [Confirmed]. So it does not advise on takeovers and it does not lend to companies. Its income comes from looking after and investing client money [Inferred].

Key terms
  • Private bank: looks after the whole financial life of a wealthy family: advice, portfolios, safekeeping, succession. Pictet Wealth Management is one [S491] [Confirmed].
  • Asset manager: paid a fee to invest clients' money through pooled funds (many investors, one portfolio) or mandates (one client, one portfolio). Pictet Asset Management, where you interned, is one [S491] [Confirmed].
  • Custody: safekeeping and administering assets that belong to someone else (settling trades, collecting dividends, keeping records). Pictet Asset Services does this [S491] [Confirmed].
  • AuM: assets under management, the money a firm invests for clients. Pictet's headline figure is "assets under management or custody", which also counts assets it only holds [S492] [Confirmed]. Always say which one you mean.

Traditional versus alternative: where Pictet sits and where KKR sits

Investment firms split roughly into two families. Traditional managers buy shares and bonds that trade on public markets, so their funds can be priced and dealt every day. Alternative managers buy things that do not trade on an exchange: whole private companies, infrastructure, property, private loans. Their funds lock money up for years because the assets cannot be sold quickly [Inferred]. KKR says its founders launched "what we now know as the alternatives industry" when they started the firm on 1 May 1976 [S13] [Confirmed]. KKR managed $796.5bn at 30 June 2026 [S1] [Confirmed], and 93% of that was perpetual capital or capital committed for 8+ years at inception [S1] [Confirmed].

Pictet is mostly traditional, with a growing alternatives arm. Its group total of CHF 810bn (assets under management or custody, 30 June 2026) converts to about USD 1,004bn on Pictet's own site [S493] [Reported]. That looks bigger than KKR, but it is not like for like: Pictet's number includes custody assets it only holds, and listed assets it can sell daily [Inferred].

The single most useful idea for your answer is liquidity: how quickly a client can turn an investment back into cash. It shapes every conversation a client team has, because it decides what you can promise. Figure 2 shows the spectrum from Pictet's world to KKR's.

History: 220 years in one picture

Pictet dates itself to 1805, when the de Candolle and Mallet families formed "De Candolle, Mallet & Cie" in Geneva, "the partnership that was to become Pictet" [S491] [Confirmed]. Edouard Pictet-Prevost joined in 1836 and became a Partner in 1841. Between 1848 and 1926 the firm was always headed by a Pictet, and the ninth generation of the family still works in it [S491] [Confirmed]. 2025 was its 220th anniversary, marked by the start of a move into a new Geneva headquarters, the "Campus Pictet de Rochemont" [S490][S491] [Confirmed].

The modern shape arrived in steps. Pictet has managed assets for institutions since 1980 [S491] [Confirmed] and has been in London since the same year [S510] [Confirmed]. Its alternatives arm made its first private equity investments in 1989, added hedge funds in 1990, did its first co-investment in 1992 and added real estate in 2004 [S491] [Confirmed]. Its thematic funds, today its flagship, go back to the 1990s [S504] [Confirmed].

The partnership model: who owns Pictet and how it decides

Most large financial firms are companies owned by shareholders who can buy and sell their shares on a stock exchange. Pictet is not. Its legal form is a "société en commandite par actions, a partnership limited by shares" [S491] [Confirmed], abbreviated SCA. The top holding entity is Pictet & Cie Group SCA, and its "significant equity owners are the Managing Partners" [S490] [Confirmed].

Key term

SCA (partnership limited by shares): a company form with two kinds of owner. Managing partners run the firm and carry the ultimate responsibility; other shareholders own shares but do not manage. Pictet chose it to keep partner control while ending unlimited personal liability [S497] [Confirmed]. Unlimited liability means a partner's own house and savings could be taken to pay the firm's debts. Until 2014 that was true for Pictet's partners [S497] [Confirmed].

The change was announced on 6 February 2013, the same day as fellow Geneva bank Lombard Odier, and took effect on 1 January 2014. The Swiss bank, Banque Pictet & Cie SA, became a limited company and the group moved under an SCA. The new structure required a consolidated annual report and an independent supervisory board [S497] [Confirmed]. In plain words: Pictet kept partner ownership but started publishing group accounts and accepting outside oversight [Inferred].

Partnership versus listed company: why it matters to clients

KKR started as a partnership too, but it listed on the New York Stock Exchange in July 2010 and converted from a partnership to a corporation in July 2018 [S5] [Confirmed]. Its employees still own about 30% of its common stock [S2][S3] [Confirmed]. Figure 5 sets the two side by side. Neither model is "better". They create different pressures, and a client notices those pressures [Inferred].

Why a client cares: a firm owned by its managers can take decisions that cost growth today to protect trust tomorrow. Pictet AM says: "We always prioritise our clients' interests over asset growth and do not hesitate to soft-close strategies to protect their returns" [S491] [Confirmed]. A soft-close means a fund stops taking new money (existing investors stay in) because more money would make it harder to invest well [Inferred]. Pictet also says, unusually for a sales organisation, "We do not aspire to be all things to all clients" [S491] [Confirmed]. KKR makes a parallel argument through its "one firm" approach, where pay depends on the performance of the firm as a whole [S2] [Confirmed].

Key term

Fund ranges and vehicles: what a client actually owns

Key term

UCITS: the EU rulebook for funds sold to the general public. It limits what the fund may hold, requires diversification and regular dealing, and lets a fund registered in one EU country be sold across others. Since January 2024 the UK treats EEA UCITS as equivalent under its Overseas Funds Regime [S546] [Confirmed].

Key term

Share class: a version of the same fund with different terms. "P" classes are retail-type and "I" classes institutional; "dy" means distributing (it pays out income) and the suffix gives the currency, as in "Pictet-Water P dy GBP" [S524][S526] [Confirmed]. Minimums sit in the prospectus, which is not covered here.

Two mechanics follow from this structure, and a client group meets both. First, dealing: a UCITS investor buys or sells at the next price the fund calculates (the net asset value, or NAV), not at a price known in advance. Orders must reach the fund's administrator before a cut-off. A 2018 Pictet notice moved many compartments to a 1:00 pm Luxembourg-time cut-off on the valuation day, with settlement for some within two week days [S527] [Confirmed] (dated 2018). Second, distribution data: Pictet sends platforms standard data files describing who each fund is for. AJ Bell shows the target market for Pictet-Water I dy GBP: available to retail, professional and eligible counterparty clients, risk indicator 5 of 7, objective growth, recommended holding period 5 years, and the statement "Product expected to provide fair value for reasonably foreseeable period" [S526] [Confirmed]. The skeletons, told straight

Every old bank has a record. Pictet's recent one has three items. You should know them precisely, never raise them yourself, and be calm and factual if asked.

  1. December 2023, US Department of Justice. On 4 December 2023 Pictet resolved a legacy DOJ investigation into its US private banking clients from 2008 to 2014 through a three-year deferred prosecution agreement. It paid USD 122.9m (CHF 106.8m), of which USD 38.9m was a fine. The DOJ recognised Pictet's "substantial assistance" [S496] [Confirmed]. A deferred prosecution agreement means prosecutors agree not to pursue charges if the firm meets agreed conditions for a set period [Inferred].
  2. March 2024, US Senate inquiry. On 21 March 2024 Senate Finance Committee Chair Ron Wyden opened an inquiry into whether Pictet kept helping a US citizen evade tax, writing to Laurent Ramsey. He said: "I also wish to better understand why Pictet only paid a paltry penalty of just $122.9 million" [S499] [Confirmed]. No outcome was found.
  3. June 2025, Switzerland. On 17 June 2025 the Office of the Attorney General of Switzerland fined Banque Pictet CHF 2m under corporate liability rules in a money-laundering case linked to the "Lava Jato" (Petrobras) investigation. A former relationship manager received a six-month suspended sentence. The OAG found the bank failed to classify the accounts as high risk and failed to properly monitor 54 transfers [S498] [Confirmed].
