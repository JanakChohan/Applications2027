The one idea behind this whole part

An asset manager has two halves. The investment teams decide what the fund owns. The client group decides who can own the fund, on what terms, with what information, and how they are looked after afterwards. Almost everything in this part is a rule, a document or a process sitting on that second half. KKR's Global Client Solutions (KCS) is the same second half, attached to private markets products instead of daily-dealing funds [Inferred].

Key terms used throughout

UCITS: "Undertakings for Collective Investment in Transferable Securities". The EU's standard rulebook for funds that ordinary savers can buy. A UCITS must hold mainly liquid securities and let investors buy and sell regularly, usually every business day (standard definition) [Inferred].

SICAV: a Luxembourg open-ended investment company. Pictet's main fund range is one SICAV called "PICTET", and each fund inside it (Pictet - Water, Pictet - Robotics and so on) is a "Compartment", also called a sub-fund [S527] [Confirmed].

NAV (net asset value): the value of everything the fund owns, minus what it owes, divided by the number of shares. It is the price at which you buy or sell a share of the fund (standard definition) [Inferred].

Transfer agent (TA): the firm that keeps the fund's register of who owns how many shares, takes in buy and sell orders, and issues confirmations. Pictet's current TA for the SICAV is not named in the sources read [Gap].

Intermediary: anyone who sits between the fund and the final saver: a fund platform (AJ Bell, Hargreaves Lansdown, Fidelity), a wealth manager, a discretionary fund manager or an independent financial adviser (IFA) [S513, S524, S525, S526] [Reported].

2. Client onboarding, start to finish

Onboarding is everything that happens between "this client wants to invest" and "this client's first order is accepted". At a UCITS manager it has six questions, asked in a fixed order: Who is the client legally? Are they who they say they are, and are they clean? What may they buy? What must we give them to read? Where do they deal? Is the data right? Each question has a rule behind it. This section takes them one at a time.

The whole pipeline on one page

Here is the whole thing first: a seven-stage pipeline in which each stage hands one artefact to the next. The steps below then take the hard ones in turn. Interns in client groups commonly sit at stages 3 to 6: chasing documents, checking forms are complete, and tracking the account at the transfer agent [Inferred].

Step 1. Client categorisation: retail, professional or eligible counterparty

UK rules sort every client into one of three boxes. The rulebook is the FCA Handbook's Conduct of Business Sourcebook, chapter 3 (COBS 3), which copies the EU's MiFID II categories [S543] [Confirmed]. The box decides how much protection the client gets, which share classes and documents they can access, and which rules the firm must follow when dealing with them [Inferred].

  • Retail client: the default. Everyone who is not shown to be something else. Maximum protection: full disclosure documents, suitability or appropriateness checks, Consumer Duty [Inferred].
  • Professional client, "per se": automatically professional because of what they are. COBS 3.5 lists credit institutions, investment firms, insurers, collective investment schemes, pension funds and large undertakings among others [S543] [Confirmed].
  • Professional client, "elective": a retail client who asks to be treated as professional and passes the tests. The firm must run a qualitative test (does the client have the expertise, experience and knowledge?) and, for MiFID business, the client must meet two of three quantitative tests: about 10 significant trades per quarter over the previous four quarters; a portfolio over EUR 500,000; or at least one year working in the financial sector in a relevant role [S543] [Confirmed].
  • Eligible counterparty (ECP): the top tier, for certain financial institutions in certain kinds of business such as order execution. Fewest protections, because both sides are professionals (from the COBS structure in S543) [Inferred].
Why categorisation comes first

Everything downstream depends on the box. A retail client must get a retail disclosure document and can only buy share classes open to retail. A pension fund (per se professional) can access institutional classes. The categorisation is recorded on the client file, and if it is wrong, every later step is wrong too [Inferred]. In a client group, you meet this as a field in the onboarding form or the CRM, and as a question you must answer before anyone sends a factsheet [Inferred].

Step 2. KYC and anti-money-laundering checks

KYC ("know your customer") means proving who the client is, who ultimately owns or controls it, where its money comes from, and how risky it is. AML (anti-money laundering) is the law that requires it. Pictet's own group shows both the process and the penalty for getting it wrong.

  • The process. A July 2026 advert for Banque Pictet's AML onboarding team describes analysing account-opening files by risk level (PEP, High, Medium, Low), checking tax items (FATCA and CRS) and drafting summaries for committees, under the Swiss AML Act and FINMA's AML Ordinance [S523] [Reported]. A Pictet Asset Services relationship role asks staff to "evaluate end client compliance and tax profiles and ensure document completion" [S522] [Reported].
  • The penalty. On 17 June 2025 the Swiss Office of the Attorney General fined Banque Pictet CHF 2m in a money-laundering case linked to Petrobras ("Lava Jato"). It said the bank failed to classify the relevant accounts as high risk and failed to properly monitor 54 transfers. A former relationship manager received a six-month suspended sentence [S498] [Confirmed]. That is a client-facing employee personally sanctioned over a risk rating.
Key terms

PEP (politically exposed person): someone with a prominent public role, or their close family and associates. Higher bribery and corruption risk, so extra checks (standard definition) [Inferred]. UBO (ultimate beneficial owner): the real person who ultimately owns or controls a company client, found by tracing the ownership chain [Inferred]. FATCA: the US rule requiring financial firms to report US persons' accounts. CRS (Common Reporting Standard): the OECD equivalent for many countries. Both mean collecting a tax self-certification at onboarding (standard definitions; named as checks in S523) [Inferred].

For an asset manager selling through intermediaries, there is a twist. When a platform or wealth manager invests, the fund's register usually shows the intermediary, not the thousands of savers behind it. The manager does KYC on the intermediary (is it regulated, who owns it, is it on a sanctions list?) and relies on the intermediary's own KYC for its end clients [Inferred]. That is why onboarding a platform is a firm-level exercise: regulatory status, ownership, signatories, dealing contacts and bank details, rather than passports of individual savers [Inferred].

Step 3. What may they buy? Share classes and eligibility

One fund, many share classes. A share class is a different "wrapper" around the same portfolio, with its own fees, currency, minimum and income treatment (standard definition) [Inferred]. Pictet's fund names encode the class in letters. Read one from left to right:

Retail, clean and institutional classes. A "retail" class is built for individual savers, often with a higher fee. A "clean" class is the usual term for a class with no commission to the distributor built into the fee: the platform or adviser charges the client separately. An "institutional" class has the lowest fee and a high minimum, meant for large investors (general industry usage; the research files do not cover Pictet's own class terms) [Inferred]. One observation from the sourced data: AJ Bell and Hargreaves Lansdown list Pictet's I classes for their retail customers [S525, S526] [Confirmed], and AJ Bell's target market data for Pictet-Water I dy GBP says the class is open to retail clients [S526] [Confirmed]. The likely reading is that the platform aggregates many savers and meets the institutional class's terms on their behalf [Inferred]. The exact minimums and eligibility conditions for each Pictet class sit in the prospectus, which was not read for this guide [Gap]. Never quote a minimum you have not checked.

Step 4. What must they read? Target market, EMT and disclosure documents

Before a fund can be sold, two sets of information must flow down the chain from the manager (the "manufacturer") to the distributor (platform, wealth manager, bank) and on to the saver.

First, product governance data. Under MiFID II, manufacturers define a target market for each product along five categories: client type, knowledge and experience, financial situation (ability to bear losses), risk tolerance, and objectives and needs (including sustainability preferences) [S548] [Reported]. The industry sends this in a standard spreadsheet format set by FinDatEx: the EMT (European MiFID Template, version 4.3 dated 15 January 2026) carries target market and costs; the EPT (European PRIIPs Template, version 2.1, with a UK section) carries the data behind the KID; the EET (European ESG Template, version 1.1.3 dated 10 April 2026) carries sustainability data [S547] [Confirmed].

Second, the disclosure document the saver actually reads. For a UCITS sold to UK retail this has been the two-page UCITS KIID (Key Investor Information Document); for other packaged products, the PRIIPs KID (Key Information Document) [S544] [Reported]. That is now changing (see the timeline below).

The UK rules are changing right now: CCI and the Overseas Funds Regime

Consumer Composite Investments (CCI). FCA policy statement PS25/20, published 8 December 2025, replaces both the UCITS KIID and the PRIIPs KID in the UK with a new "Product Summary Document". The rules start on 6 April 2026; the UK UCITS exemption expires on 31 December 2026; firms must have moved over by 8 June 2027. The risk indicator moves to a scale of 1 to 10, ongoing costs are shown as one headline figure, and core data is to be supplied in machine-readable form such as the EMT and EPT [S544, S545] [Reported]. So from April 2026 to June 2027, client groups handle two document regimes at once [Inferred].

The Overseas Funds Regime (OFR). Pictet's UK-sold funds are mostly Luxembourg UCITS [S524, S526] [Confirmed]. After Brexit, an EU fund needs UK permission to be marketed to UK retail investors. In January 2024 the UK found EEA UCITS (excluding money market funds) equivalent under the OFR. Funds move from the Temporary Marketing Permissions Regime (TMPR) into the OFR in FCA "landing slots", and the TMPR was extended to the end of 2026 [S546] [Confirmed]. This is the legal route by which a Luxembourg Pictet fund appears on a UK platform [Inferred].

3. Client servicing: dealing, reporting, queries and fund events

Once a client is onboarded, the client group's job becomes keeping them informed, keeping their transactions clean, and answering whatever they ask. This section explains the machinery underneath: how a UCITS deals every day, why nobody knows the price when they place an order, what goes in a factsheet, how a question travels through the firm, and the two UK value tests that shape what clients ask.

How a daily-dealing fund works

A UCITS like Pictet-Water calculates a NAV on each Valuation Day (normally each business day). Investors can subscribe (buy new shares, so the fund grows) or redeem (sell shares back to the fund, so it shrinks). There is no stock exchange in the middle: the fund itself creates and cancels shares at NAV (standard mechanism) [Inferred].

Three rules govern every order:

  1. Cut-off time. Orders must reach the transfer agent before a fixed time. A July 2018 Pictet notice to shareholders moved many compartments to a 1:00 pm Luxembourg time cut-off on the Valuation Day, and Pictet - Global Megatrend Selection to 11:00 am. Hong Kong distributors had a separate 5:00 pm Hong Kong time deadline through Bank Pictet & Cie (Asia) [S527] (dated 2018) [Confirmed]. Current cut-offs are in today's prospectus, which was not read [Gap].
  2. Forward pricing. An order placed before the cut-off is dealt at the NAV calculated after the cut-off, using that day's closing prices. You never know the exact price when you place the order [S527] (the mechanism is [Inferred]). This stops anyone trading on yesterday's stale price after news breaks [Inferred].
  3. Settlement. The cash moves a set number of days later. The 2018 notice moved settlement for some compartments to "within 2 week days after the Valuation Day" [S527] (dated 2018) [Confirmed], usually written T+2.

When dealing stops

A daily-dealing fund can suspend. In February 2022 the board of Pictet - Russian Equities suspended NAV calculation and dealing [S527] [Reported] (from a separate notice found alongside it). That is the daily-dealing world's version of a "gate": the promise of daily liquidity holds only while the assets can be valued and sold [Inferred]. Pictet's annual review also says it will "soft-close strategies to protect their returns" [S491] [Confirmed], meaning it can stop new money coming into a fund that has grown too large for its market.

Fund events and notices

A fund event is any change to a fund that existing investors must be told about: a new cut-off time, a change of manager, a fee change, a soft close, a merger of two compartments, a liquidation, or a suspension. The fund's board decides; legal drafts a formal notice to shareholders; the client group's job is to make sure every affected client and distributor receives it, understands it and gets answers to their questions before the effective date [Inferred]. The 13 July 2018 Pictet notice (cut-off and settlement changes) is a real example of exactly this kind of document [S527] [Confirmed]. A practical intern task in such a week is building the list of affected clients from the CRM and tracking who has acknowledged [Inferred].

Factsheets and reporting

A factsheet is the one- or two-page monthly summary of a fund: objective, managers, launch date, size, price, performance against benchmark, top holdings, sector and country weights, charges and risk indicator (standard content) [Inferred]. Platforms republish parts of it: Fidelity's page for Pictet-Water P dy GBP shows the managers, launch date (19 January 2000), price and charges [S524] [Confirmed], and the current managers are listed as Louis Veilleux and Ola Obanubi [S524] [Confirmed]. Behind the factsheet sits a reporting team; Pictet's London reporting internship described producing "investment, marketing, product and regulatory reports for clients and investors", working with Sales and Investment teams before client meetings, and running data quality checks [S518] [Reported]. Institutional clients usually also receive monthly or quarterly portfolio reports tailored to their mandate [Inferred].

Consumer Duty fair value and the Assessment of Value

Two UK tests decide whether a fund is "worth the money". Clients and distributors ask about both, so the client group needs to know which applies.

Consumer Duty. The FCA's consumer principle reads: "A firm must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers" [S542] [Confirmed]. It applied to open products from 31 July 2023 and closed products from 31 July 2024 [S308] [Confirmed]. Manufacturers must assess whether each product offers fair value and pass that assessment down to distributors, which is why the AJ Bell page shows "fair value assessment completed" with a review date [S526] [Confirmed]. The pass-down mechanism is [Inferred].

Assessment of Value (AoV). UK authorised fund managers must publish an annual value assessment against seven criteria: quality of service, performance, AFM costs, economies of scale, comparable market rates, comparable services, and classes of units [S549] [Reported]. The FCA found in August 2023 that reports had "significantly improved", but that some independent directors "did not provide sufficient challenge" [S541] [Confirmed]. The key point for Pictet: its UK-sold funds are mostly Luxembourg UCITS, not UK authorised funds, so the AoV rule does not apply to them; the Consumer Duty fair value assessment is the test that matters [Inferred] (supported by [S555]). Knowing which value test applies to which fund is a small fact that shows real understanding.

What the work really is

Client servicing at a UCITS manager is three systems running every day: the dealing system (cut-off, forward-priced NAV, settlement), the information system (factsheets, reports, EMT and other data files, fund notices) and the question system (log, route, check, reply, close). Each has a rulebook behind it: the prospectus, MiFID II product governance and the Consumer Duty. An intern who can explain why an order missed today's price, what an EMT field means, and which value test applies to a Luxembourg fund has learned the technical core [Inferred].

Pictet's own Laurent Ramsey put the servicing risk in one line: "you need to be careful when making something illiquid liquid" [S536] [Reported]. 4. Relationship management: CRM, meetings, coverage, pipeline and the thematic pitch

Servicing keeps existing clients happy. Relationship management decides which clients the firm spends time on, what it says to them, and how new money arrives. Juniors do not run relationships, but they build the materials and records that relationships run on [Inferred].

Coverage models: who looks after whom

A coverage model is the rule for which salesperson covers which client. Pictet's public titles show two cuts: by client type (Niall Quinn joined in 2016 as "Global Head of Institutional Business (excluding Japan)" [S509] [Confirmed]; a 2017 appointment covered "UK intermediaries" [S513] [Reported]) and by region (Head of UK & Ireland [S512]; Head of Sales Europe [S507] [Confirmed]). Within a region, firms commonly tier clients: a few strategic relationships get a senior RM and regular meetings with fund managers; a long tail is served by a smaller team, by phone and by email [Inferred]. The ETF hire in 2026 [S559] [Reported] shows a third cut: by product channel, because ETFs are bought differently from mutual funds [Inferred].

CRM hygiene: the record is the relationship

A CRM (customer relationship management system) is the database of every client, contact, meeting, call, opportunity and open query. Which CRM Pictet AM uses is not public [Gap], so on your CV name it only if you used it. Meeting preparation: the briefing pack

Before an RM meets a client, someone builds a briefing pack. Pictet's reporting team says it works "with Sales and Investment teams before client meetings" [S518] [Reported]. A typical pack has six parts [Inferred]: (1) who is attending and their roles; (2) what the client holds with us and recent flows; (3) notes from the last meeting and open follow-ups; (4) performance of their funds against benchmark and peers, with a short explanation from the product specialist; (5) relevant news (for Pictet in 2026: new hires such as Badjie [S507], the European AI-enhanced ETFs launched 1 July 2026 [S539]); (6) the objective of the meeting and the one ask. After the meeting, the notes and follow-ups go into the CRM the same day [Inferred].

Fund selectors, buy lists and ratings

Most intermediary money is controlled by a fund selector: an analyst at a wealth manager, platform or private bank who decides which external funds go on the firm's approved "buy list" [Inferred]. Selectors typically judge people, process, performance and price, and look at independent fund research ratings alongside their own work (general industry practice; no specific rating for Pictet funds is in the research files) [Inferred]. The visible end of this process is the platform listing: Hargreaves Lansdown lists Pictet Global Thematic Opportunities (I GBP Acc), managed by Hans Peter Portner, Gertjan van der Geer and Jan Andreas Maager in Geneva, as eligible for ISAs and SIPPs [S525] [Confirmed]. Institutional money has its own gatekeepers, the investment consultants, who run RFPs; Pictet's RFP team answers requests from "central banks, pension funds, insurers and distributors" [S521] [Reported].
