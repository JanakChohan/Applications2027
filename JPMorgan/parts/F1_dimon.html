Part 2. Dimon's operating manual, in his own words

In plain English The 2024 shareholder letter (published April 2025) contains a lightly edited transcript of a talk Dimon gave to about 400 senior leaders. It is the closest thing to an operating manual the firm has ever published. The 2023 letter has a companion section on how he thinks and decides, and the 2025 letter adds the "small teams" idea. Senior people at the firm know these passages. If you quote one, you are speaking their language.

The stories he tells to explain the rules

Dimon teaches by anecdote, and the anecdotes are what people at the firm remember. These are the ones from the letters, in his words or close to them.

StoryWhat happenedThe rule it teaches
The 500 coachesHe discovered the firm employed about 500 outside management coaches. "I changed my mind. I'm going to micromanage this one. I want all coaches out by the end of the week." Because "it's a leader's job to coach, and we basically had outsourced management!"Managers manage. It is also why Private Bank training is taught by producing advisers, not by HR or outside firms (Part 5).
The FedEx boxAt Bank One a branch employee dropped a FedEx box on his desk full of the corporate memos a branch received in a week. The fix was a single weekly booklet, "Since We Last Met" or "You Must Read This".See the firm through the eyes of the person in front of a customer. "We are here because we have a customer, a branch or an investment banker in front of a client."
The 50 black carsAt Shearson "there were, literally, 50 black cars" waiting for executives.Perks are bureaucracy made visible. "Cutting waste", not "cutting costs".
The ATM at WalgreensHis wife reported a broken ATM. The vendor insisted it worked. It did not. "I told the guy that we were firing the vendor and wouldn't pay him for the last six months.""I don't give a damn whether we're technically responsible or not. You've got to do the right thing." Also: he reads customer complaints and calls the people in charge to say "I agree with the customer."
The Old Greenwich branchA branch making about $500,000 a year, "all profit", was about to be closed on a net-present-value analysis. He asked who would open in the same spot: "Wells Fargo, Bank of America or Citizens." It stayed. "It was the pawn blocking the queen. Judgment calls.""A P&L is not an assessment of a business." Spreadsheets do not override judgement about competitors and clients.
The partners' dining room, 2008In the crisis he built a dining room for senior staff. "The whole Operating Committee said, 'Don't do it.'" His reason: "we don't actually know each other after the merger." "I call it a good expense."Some spending buys trust and speed. The same logic funds the five-week analyst training and the bus tours.
The security guardsOn arrival at JPMorgan Chase he found the guards had been outsourced to halve their healthcare. "This was a heartless thing to do, and the second I found out, I reversed the decision." "JPMorgan Chase's success will not be built off the backs of our guards.""Treat everyone well, from clerks to CEOs." The 2014 principles ask: "would you let your kids report to them?"
The abusive rich clientA wealthy branch client abused staff. Dimon: "I want you to take all your business out of the bank." "If we have a client who is disrespectful to our people and the way we operate, we will fire them. And life will go on."The Private Bank can and does decline clients. Character before revenue; the Pujo line from 1912 (Part 5) is the root.
Bank One branch hoursBranches were open two hours a day less than competitors and nobody had told him. "I apologize. I thought I was a pretty good CEO, but I messed up, I made a mistake." And: "morale sucks because we suck."Admit mistakes publicly and specifically. Morale is downstream of performance, not the other way round.

How he actually runs a meeting, according to people who have been in one

  • The follow-up sheet. A folded sheet of paper in his shirt pocket "covered with dozens of little rectangles in blue ink: follow-ups and to-do reminders" (Crisafulli, 2009). Fortune in 2006 called it the "people who owe me stuff" list. The 2024 letter confirms it: "Always make follow-up lists. I keep my own follow-up lists with me almost all the time."
  • Hours of questions. Fortune, 2006: he "requires managers to present exhaustive monthly reviews, then grills them on the data for hours at a time." Steve Black, then co-head of the investment bank: "If you just want to run your business on your own and report results, you won't like working for Jamie."
  • Push back or lose respect. He would say "That's the dumbest thing I've ever heard" and expect an argument. Jes Staley: "If not, he won't respect you." In 2026: "You're not going to hurt my feelings by telling me we have a crappy product. If you're right, I'm gonna thank you."
  • Meetings end with owners. Oslo, April 2026: a meeting that ends with "we'll pick it up again next week" is "usually a bad meeting". "The meeting should end with, Okay David, you're going to do X."
  • No phones. "If you have an iPad in front of me and it looks like you're reading your email... I tell you to close the damn thing." He receives notifications only from his children.
  • Process versus outcome. On managers who like process: "They like the process, not the outcome. Whereas I like the outcome."
What this means for you An intern will never present to Dimon. But the London Private Bank's team heads were promoted by people who were trained in this style, and it shows up as: bring the number and the source; say the bad news first; if you disagree, say so in the room, once, with a reason; end every conversation with who does what by when; and never look at your phone while a client advisor is talking. The candidate who does the pre-read on a client before a meeting is doing the single thing the CEO says he always does.
