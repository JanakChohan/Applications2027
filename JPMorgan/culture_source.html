Part 1. The map: seven beliefs and what each one built

Part 2. Dimon's operating manual, in his own words

In plain English The 2024 shareholder letter (published April 2025) contains a lightly edited transcript of a talk Dimon gave to about 400 senior leaders. It is the closest thing to an operating manual the firm has ever published. The 2023 letter has a companion section on how he thinks and decides, and the 2025 letter adds the "small teams" idea. Senior people at the firm know these passages. If you quote one, you are speaking their language.

How he actually runs a meeting, according to people who have been in one

  • The follow-up sheet. A folded sheet of paper in his shirt pocket "covered with dozens of little rectangles in blue ink: follow-ups and to-do reminders" (Crisafulli, 2009). Fortune in 2006 called it the "people who owe me stuff" list. The 2024 letter confirms it: "Always make follow-up lists. I keep my own follow-up lists with me almost all the time."
  • Hours of questions. Fortune, 2006: he "requires managers to present exhaustive monthly reviews, then grills them on the data for hours at a time." Steve Black, then co-head of the investment bank: "If you just want to run your business on your own and report results, you won't like working for Jamie."
  • Push back or lose respect. He would say "That's the dumbest thing I've ever heard" and expect an argument. Jes Staley: "If not, he won't respect you." In 2026: "You're not going to hurt my feelings by telling me we have a crappy product. If you're right, I'm gonna thank you."
  • Meetings end with owners. Oslo, April 2026: a meeting that ends with "we'll pick it up again next week" is "usually a bad meeting". "The meeting should end with, Okay David, you're going to do X."
  • No phones. "If you have an iPad in front of me and it looks like you're reading your email... I tell you to close the damn thing." He receives notifications only from his children.
  • Process versus outcome. On managers who like process: "They like the process, not the outcome. Whereas I like the outcome."
What this means for you An intern will never present to Dimon. But the London Private Bank's team heads were promoted by people who were trained in this style, and it shows up as: bring the number and the source; say the bad news first; if you disagree, say so in the room, once, with a reason; end every conversation with who does what by when; and never look at your phone while a client advisor is talking. The candidate who does the pre-read on a client before a meeting is doing the single thing the CEO says he always does.

Part 3. Three weekends: how the firm behaves when something breaks

In plain English Every bank says it is strong. The test is what happens on the weekend a rival collapses and the government needs a buyer by Monday morning. J.P. Morgan has been that buyer three times in fifteen years. The stories below show a pattern: refuse at first, then mobilise hundreds of people overnight, then explain it afterwards as duty and as business at the same time. The Private Bank's own crisis behaviour, in Part 5, follows the same script.
Figure 3. The pattern: initial refusal, overnight mobilisation, a public framing of duty, then an honest ledger of what it cost.

What the First Republic weekend did to the Private Bank

This is the part most candidates miss. First Republic was not just a bank; it was a wealth business built around a "concierge" model where one banker handled everything for a rich client. Dimon wrote in the 2023 letter: "First Republic had done an outstanding job serving high-net-worth clients and venture capitalists, and we are developing what is effectively a new business for us following First Republic's servicing model." And then the sentence that a candidate should carry into any interview: "We will serve these high-net-worth clients through a single point of contact, supported by a concierge service model." In the same letter he admitted: "We underinvested for years in the wealth management business because we were always focused on the value of deposits versus investments."

So the deal is a culture story twice over. Once because the firm acted as buyer of last resort. Twice because the firm, having bought a rival, openly said the rival did something better and copied it. The J.P. Morgan wealth centres and the "single point of contact" language in the branches came from an acquired competitor. That is the "full and constant assessment" principle in practice: "It's okay to be a fast follower. Just do a postmortem."

The sequel: "Bloody Friday", April 2024

The honest end of the story. On 19 April 2024 six former First Republic adviser teams, about 48 people managing roughly $15bn, left J.P. Morgan in a single day for Merrill, Morgan Stanley, Wells Fargo and boutiques. The reasons reported were the "repapering" of clients onto J.P. Morgan's platform and a preference for "a boutique, client-centric atmosphere rather than JPMorgan's large-bank environment". The firm later said it kept about 75% of First Republic's advisers and clients. What it tells you: J.P. Morgan's culture is a system, with controls, documentation and a team model, and people used to a personal-franchise model sometimes leave rather than adapt. That is a real cost of the "our client, not my client" belief, and a good interviewer will respect you for knowing it.

The crisis muscle inside AWM

  • 2008, in the Private Bank. Erdoes, who then ran it: "From October through December 2008, we were taking in as much as $1 billion a day at the private bank." Every employee was trained on the account-transfer system "so they could calm clients down". The 8am morning meeting "happened three times a day". The rule for staff: "It was an important time not to be disparaging." The Private Bank added $80bn of assets that year.
  • March 2023, after Silicon Valley Bank. "On Friday at 7:25 PM, that was the timestamp of the 40,000th account being opened since March 10." "We took people from the mortgage business, we took people from the first-year analyst classes. It didn't matter where you were from." "If you are smart, and you are driven, we flew you to another city, we put you up in a hotel for the last 4, 6 weeks." "Just on the institutional side, we did two years' worth of account opening in basically two months."
  • The self-image. "We know what to do when we run our war rooms inside. We know how to stay in front of clients." And to American Banker: "The moments where we really shine are the moments when there's a crisis." "We've really established ourselves as the flight to quality institution."
What this means for you Two things. First, a first-year analyst in this firm can be put on a plane to open accounts in another city for six weeks; that is a feature, and they are proud of it. Second, the "not disparaging" rule matters in London now: UBS is integrating Credit Suisse, Citi is restructuring, Coutts is absorbing Evelyn. The firm's instinct is to be calm, present and useful to the clients of those banks, not to mock them. Say "flight to quality", never "our rivals are a mess".

Part 4. The wound that shaped the controls: the London Whale and "How We Do Business"

In plain English In 2012 a trading team in London, inside the unit that manages the bank's own spare cash, built an enormous position in credit derivatives. When it went wrong the loss reached about $6.2bn. Dimon had dismissed the first press reports as "a tempest in a teapot". He later called it "the stupidest and most embarrassing situation I have ever been a part of". The episode is the reason the firm wrote down its principles in 2014, and the reason risk staff now sit next to traders and bankers in Asset & Wealth Management. You will hear it referred to twelve years later as if it were yesterday.

What this means for you In the Private Bank the Whale lesson translates directly: when a client wants something that feels wrong, the failure mode the firm fears is not the bad trade, it is the person who keeps it to themselves. If a HireVue or interview question asks what you would do if a client asked for something you thought was wrong, the J.P. Morgan-shaped answer is: listen for the goal behind the request, say what you think, and escalate to the right person early. The words "raise a red flag" and "the right people in the room" are the firm's own.

Part 5. The Private Bank's own creed: Erdoes and the sentences everyone learns

In plain English Mary Erdoes joined J.P. Morgan in 1996 at 29, ran the Private Bank from 2005 to 2009, and has run all of Asset & Wealth Management since. She is a mathematician by training, and what she says about the Private Bank at every Investor Day has hardened into a set of sentences that new analysts are taught. Ten of them are below, all verbatim, all dated. They are more useful to you than any values page because they describe how decisions are actually made.

Who she is, in the details that explain the business

  • The mathematician. Georgetown, mathematics, 1989; reportedly the only woman to complete the major there at the time; then Harvard Business School. Her grandmother taught her to balance a chequebook at six. Her first job she called "a glorified mailroom job".
  • What she found in 1996 and what she did about it. "Private banking was trusts and estates, wealth transfer, banking, but nothing really impressive on the investments side." Her stated mission, in her own words: "Two decades ago, we set out to turn a bank into an investments house that does banking by hiring the smartest investment people we could find." This is why the London Investment Solutions track exists.
  • The 8am morning meeting. She started it as Private Bank CEO in 2005; Euromoney calls it "now an institution at the bank", where "all advisers and employees are invited to hear the daily views from the capital markets group". In 2008 it ran three times a day. It will be the first thing you attend every morning in London.
  • The mentor in the closet. Her mentor was Jimmy Lee, the legendary investment banker. "She opens a closet in her office where a poster of Lee is taped. 'He made me put it there,' she jokes. 'He pushed me to be bolder, to try harder.'"
  • Work. Euromoney: "known for working all hours and regularly getting on planes to visit clients to discuss investments, working twice as hard if performance takes a hit." Her advice to juniors: "Sit in the front. Take notes. Ask questions. Dress for success and always send thank-you notes." To women: "The front row is not saved for anyone; I especially tell that to females."
  • Her one-line strategy. "You will never build a great asset management company if you strive to be the biggest. You have to strive to be the best."
  • Standing. American Banker's most powerful woman in finance in 2023, 2024 and 2025; on the Operating Committee since 2009; given a $20m retention award in June 2026 when the firm named its co-presidents. The Private Bank's ultimate boss is one of the longest-serving people in the building.

Where it comes from: character before collateral, 1912

The oldest sentence in the firm's culture is from Pierpont Morgan's testimony to the Pujo Committee in December 1912, when asked whether credit was based on money or property: "No, sir. The first thing is character... before money or anything else. Money cannot buy it." And: "A man I do not trust could not get money from me on all the bonds in Christendom." That is the root of "two ways out" (character and collateral, not one), of the "empty tombstone", and of Dimon firing abusive clients. Two more heritage facts you will hear in London pitch books: the firm dates its UK presence to 1838, and Junius Morgan, Pierpont's father, built J.S. Morgan & Co. in London in 1854. And one you can use as a metaphor: the original headquarters at 23 Wall Street had no name on the facade, and after the 1920 bombing that killed 38 people the firm refused to repair the pockmarks. The team that serves the largest families is called 23 Wall for that reason.

Not verified: sayings about Erdoes that circulate but cannot be sourced
  • "There is no such thing as a bad market, only bad portfolios." No source attributes this to her. The nearest real line is from CNBC in 2022: "there is alpha everywhere."
  • "We hire for character." Not found as an Erdoes line. Use Pujo 1912, or Dimon's hiring test from 2020: "Would you work for that person? Would you want your kid to work for that person?"
  • That she reads every client complaint, gives clients her mobile number, or sits on the trading floor rather than in an office. None found. Euromoney describes "her office" with a closet.
  • A "call every client" order in March 2020. Not found. What is documented is the 2020 letter: over 90% of AWM went remote in two weeks and "clients were our focus".
  • A numbered list of "Erdoes' 10 rules". Does not exist. The nearest is her 2021 Bloomberg money rules: "Don't lose what you already have" and "diversify".
What this means for you Say "our client". Know that the morning meeting is where the firm's view is set and that you are expected to be there with a question. If asked about lending, mention "two ways out". If asked about advice, mention the "empty tombstone". If asked why J.P. Morgan rather than a boutique, quote "we seed books of business" and explain that you want to be seeded. Do not quote the unverified lines; interviewers who sit in the 8am meeting will notice.

And the firm has been the buyer on three crisis weekends, which tells me the capital and the culture are both real. I want to be trained by a firm that expects me to be as productive at 25 as a mid-career hire, and says so in print."
"Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news."Structure the story around the firm's own rule: you found the fact, you put it on the table in the room rather than in a corridor afterwards, you did not wait, and you proposed the fix. Close with what changed. You do not need to cite Dimon; the shape of the story will be recognised.
"A client insists on putting 60% of their wealth into one private company. What do you do?""First I would want to understand the goal behind it, because the request is rarely the goal. Then I would say plainly what the risk is, using the firm's language of two ways out: if that company fails, what is the second path? If the client still wants it, I would bring in the investor and the team head early rather than carry it alone, and document the advice. Erdoes calls the deal you talk a client out of the empty tombstone, and says the firm celebrates it. I would rather earn that than the fee."
If you only remember one page The firm is built on a handful of beliefs that have been tested in public: hold more capital than you need, and you become the buyer when others fail; face facts in the room, because the one time a team hid a problem it cost $6bn; the client belongs to the firm, not the banker, so nobody counts credits and nobody is paid a grid; advice must be independent of what sells, and the regulator has twice made that expensive to forget; and people are grown, not bought, so the analyst class is the strategy. Those beliefs produced the integrated team, the separate Solutions engine, the five-week training, the Manchester office and the 8am meeting. They also produced five-day office weeks, long hours, blunt bosses and some genuine failures. Say that you know both halves, and mean it.
