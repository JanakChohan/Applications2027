Ropes & Gray began in Boston in 1865 and spent most of its life as a well-regarded regional firm with one unusual asset: it was counsel to the fund complexes that grew up around Boston's money-management industry. That accident of geography became the strategy.

The firm never tried to become a global generalist. It made a narrower bet — own the private capital economy (the sponsors, their funds, their deals, their portfolio companies, their exits) and own healthcare and life sciences as the sector where that capital is most concentrated and most heavily regulated. Everything else in the firm exists to serve those two.

That is why the comparison set is Kirkland & Ellis and Latham & Watkins rather than the Magic Circle. A Magic Circle firm sells breadth: it will do your bond issue, your competition defence and your shipping dispute. Ropes sells depth in one lane and prices accordingly.

The number that shows the strategy

Revenue per lawyer of roughly $2.5 million. That figure is what deliberate concentration looks like on a profit and loss account: high-value mandates, relatively few lawyers, no volume business. It is a more revealing statistic than the headline revenue, and almost no applicant will mention it.

London opened in 2010 on Ludgate Hill and is now the firm's second centre of gravity — described internally as the hub of a European private capital platform, not as a European full-service firm. London, Paris, Milan and Dublin are run as one European unit.

Where the $3.7 billion comes from

Ropes is a private partnership. It reports a top-line revenue figure and a profit-per-equity-partner figure to the legal press, and nothing else. It does not publish a practice-by-practice revenue breakdown, and neither does any comparable firm.

Say this instead

"Ropes doesn't publish a practice split, but revenue per lawyer of around $2.5 million tells you it's running a concentrated, high-value model rather than a volume one." That is defensible, and it quietly signals that you understand what firms do and don't disclose.

Why the four sit together

They are not four businesses; they are one. A sponsor raises a fund (pillar one), buys a healthcare asset that needs regulatory expertise to underwrite properly (pillar four), and eventually exits it into a continuation vehicle (pillar two) — while the same firm advises the registered-fund managers now buying into private assets (pillar three). Each pillar makes the others more valuable. That is what a genuinely integrated firm looks like, as opposed to one that has simply collected practices.

Standing where the industry is rebuilding itself

Most firms sit on one side of a wall. Either they do private funds — closed-end, illiquid, institutional money, ten-year lock-ups — or they do registered funds, where investors are retail and the regulation is about daily liquidity, fee levels and independent boards. Different skills, different regulators, different clients. Ropes is top-tier on both sides at once.

Section 08 · the differentiators

Six things that are actually rare

Ranked by how few other firms could claim the same. Each one is a claim, the evidence for it, and why it is hard to copy.

01

Top-tier on both sides of the private / registered fund divide

Almost every competitor is a private funds firm or a registered funds firm. Ropes is both, at scale, just as the two worlds merge into evergreen and semi-liquid products.

Evidence: counsel to 2,000+ registered funds; simultaneously the #1-ranked secondaries practice. Hard to copy: you cannot lateral-hire a forty-year fund-board relationship.

02

Revenue that survives the deal cycle

Most elite transactional firms are a leveraged bet on M&A volume. Ropes built secondaries and restructuring alongside, so the same client list pays whichever way the cycle turns.

Evidence: 600+ secondaries deals in 2025; a new global restructuring chair hired with a four-partner team in 2026. Hard to copy: requires deliberately building capacity you hope not to use.

03

An information advantage over its own clients' market

The firm maintains a proprietary database of buyout fund terms. Because it papers so many funds, it sees where the market actually is on fees, hurdles, GP commitments and carry before LPs do.

Evidence: cited in Chambers commentary as "state-of-the-art technology". Hard to copy: it is a by-product of volume — you need the mandates first.

04

Client and firm are one talent pool

The firm hired EQT's general counsel into London as a partner. A former head of that same London office left to join Bain Capital. That traffic runs in both directions and it is unusual.

Evidence: Paul Dali, ex-GC of EQT, joined in 2026. Hard to copy: people only move like that where the relationship is already genuine.

05

A sector vertical deep enough to change the underwriting

Life sciences sits beside private capital because healthcare buyouts turn on things a generalist cannot price: patent cliffs, reimbursement policy, regulatory pathways.

Evidence: a Chambers UK 2026 Band 1 life sciences regulatory partner hired into London, July 2026. Hard to copy: sector credibility takes a decade.

06

Expansion that follows clients rather than flags

Fifteen years of London alone, then Paris and Milan inside six months — not to be a European full-service firm, but because sponsor clients were deploying there and referrals leak relationships.

Evidence: ten-lawyer team from Linklaters and eight-lawyer team from Latham into Paris, 2025–26. Hard to copy: restraint is harder than growth.

Section 09 · your angle

It is a relationship business wearing a law firm's clothes

If you are drawn to client-facing work, this is the section that matters. Ropes' economics only work because it holds relationships rather than winning transactions — and that is a structural fact about the firm, not a line from its brochure.

Three mechanisms that make it compound

One · switching costs

By fund III the firm holds every precedent document, the fund's own negotiated positions, and the institutional memory of what the LPs asked for last time. Changing adviser means paying someone else to relearn it.

Two · no internal competition

The matrix on page 4 exists so five practices can serve one client without fighting over it. That is what lets a funds partner introduce a restructuring partner rather than guarding the relationship.

Three · usefulness between mandates

The buyout fund terms database means the firm has something to say when there is no live deal. Advisers who only appear when they are billing are the ones who get re-tendered.

And the risk

Concentration cuts both ways. A firm this tied to private capital is exposed if sponsors stop raising funds — which is precisely why secondaries and restructuring were built up alongside. Worth raising if a partner asks what you would worry about.

Section 10

How you can prove the relationship claim

Anyone can assert that a firm is "relationship-led" — it is on every law firm website in the world. The reason you can say it about Ropes and be believed is that there are four pieces of hard evidence, and almost no applicant will have found them.

A

People move between firm and client

Ropes hired Paul Dali, formerly general counsel of EQT — a client — into London as a private equity partner in 2026. In the other direction, a former head of the London office left to join Bain Capital, another major client.

Why it proves the point: a general counsel only joins their outside counsel when they already know the firm from the inside. Careers do not cross that line where the relationship is purely commercial.

B

The firm opens offices where clients deploy capital

Paris and Milan launched within six months of each other in 2025 — the first continental European offices after fifteen years of London alone. Four European private equity co-heads now meet weekly, in London.

Why it proves the point: the alternative was referring a French deal to a French firm. Referrals leak relationships. This is a defensive move to keep a client whole, not an ambition to be a European firm.

C

It gives clients information, not just advice

The proprietary database of buyout fund terms lets the firm tell a sponsor where the market actually is on fees, hurdles and carry — before that information is public.

Why it proves the point: this is the classic relationship move in any advisory business. You become valuable between mandates by bringing something the client cannot get elsewhere.

D

Lawyers go and sit inside the client

London runs client secondments — to Liberty Global among others — plus a three-month antitrust secondment to the Dublin office. Secondments are allocated when a client asks for one.

Why it proves the point: a client only asks for a seconded lawyer when they already treat the firm as an extension of their own team.

The sentence this earns you

"Ropes doesn't win deals so much as win sponsors — and the proof isn't in the marketing, it's that the firm hired EQT's general counsel while a former head of its London office left for Bain Capital. Adviser and client are effectively one community." Say that and you are no longer a candidate who has read a website.

Section 11 · read this before the event

What "client-facing" honestly means here

One caution, because it will save you from the single most common mistake at an event like this. A trainee solicitor's first two years are not client-facing in the way a coverage, investor relations or sales role is. If you tell a partner you are drawn to Ropes because you want client contact from day one, they will know you have not understood the job. The relationship skill matters enormously — it just arrives on a different timetable, and it starts internally.

Where relationship skill actually shows up early

This is the more interesting point, and it is well evidenced. At Ropes, internal relationships are the mechanism for almost everything that determines your career there.

Seat allocation

Informal. Trainees rank preferences in conversation with graduate recruitment. One trainee's summary: "The onus is on you to reach out to people informally."

Qualification

Typically no formal interview unless a department is oversubscribed — it happens through conversations with teams, partners and associates. The firm retained all qualifiers in 2025.

Supervision

Trainees share an office with a supervising associate who volunteered for the role. Proximity, not process, is how the learning happens.

Pro bono

A 20-hour annual target, with a family law clinic in East London. One trainee: "they do trust you to run with it." Often the first matter where you are the one the client speaks to.

Say it this way instead

Not "I want client contact early." Instead: "I'm interested in the part of this job where you understand a client's business well enough to be useful before you're asked — and I know that starts with being the person in the team who is trusted with the relationship internally first." That shows you understand the trajectory rather than romanticising it.

Section 14

Questions, and how to follow up

The day runs 10:00–15:30 and includes a trainee Q&A, an application workshop, a diversity panel and a negotiation exercise. These four questions are answerable, non-obvious, and signal that you did the reading.

01

Now that London, Paris and Milan run as one platform, does a trainee get staffed on European deals, or does the seat system keep you in London work?

Tests whether the platform strategy reaches the bottom of the firm. Trainees will have a real answer.

02

Is there a funds seat in the London rotation, and do trainees see continuation vehicle work, or does that sit mainly with the US offices?

Shows you know the practice map, and it is genuinely useful information for you.

03

With so much growth coming from lateral partner hires rather than internal promotion, how does that change what it's like to be an associate here?

A real question about the strategy's cost. Ask it neutrally, not as a challenge.

04

Does the firm expect the retail push into private markets to become a meaningful part of the London practice, or does that stay a US registered-funds story?

The asset management question. Connects your interest to their business, and very few candidates will ask it.

The negotiation exercise

Worth knowing what the firm's own negotiators actually do. In a fund formation context the negotiation is GP against LP over management fees, hurdle rates, key-person clauses and side letters — not a haggle over price. If the exercise is transactional, the same instinct applies: look for the terms that allocate risk, not just the number. Candidates who chase price look junior; candidates who ask "who carries this if it goes wrong" look trained.

If relationships are your angle, behave like it on the day

You are attending an event about relationship-building, so the event is itself the test. Three practical things:

One. Get two or three names, not ten business cards. Depth beats coverage, which is the same argument the firm makes about itself.

Two. Ask a trainee what they were doing last week. It is a better question than "what's the culture like", it gets a real answer, and it gives you something specific to reference later.

Three. Follow up within 48 hours with something useful rather than something grateful — a short note referencing the specific thing they told you. That is the actual skill being tested, and it is the one most candidates skip.
