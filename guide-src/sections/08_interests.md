# The Interests Section {#sec7}

<p class="sectionmeta">The most under-rated line on a client-facing CV. For roles where "can we put this person in front of a client?" is the real question, rapport is part of the job — and this is where you seed it.</p>

## 7.1 Do recruiters actually read it? The evidence, both ways

<div class="box disagree" markdown="1">
<span class="lbl">The disagreement — with sources on each side</span>

**Yes, and it's leverage (majority, especially early-career + client-facing).** An r/FinancialCareers poster in Sales & Trading added "*bowling, film photography, basketball*" after months of silence and got "*five first-rounds in two weeks*," with interviewers repeatedly raising the interests. "*Resumes are boring. Interests are a chance to add a bit of your personality… a great way to connect with an interviewer.*" A hiring manager's logic: "*every college kid equally knows nothing, so who's the coolest to spend over 12 hours a day with*" *(r/FinancialCareers "Started getting more interviews after including interests" — Appendix §7)*. The Trackr calls it "*the most underrated line on any CV.*"

**No, we ignore it (minority but real).** "*As a Corporate Recruiter, please don't do this. We really don't care*"; one IB professional said they "*never examine the interests section*" — and got pushback in-thread. Gentis: "*Recruiters want to know what you can do — not what you like to do… in most cases, interests and hobbies dilute the impact of your resume*" *(Appendix §7)*.
</div>

**The reconciled position:** the downside of a *good* interests line is near-zero (one line); the upside — an interview hook, a rapport opener, a tie-breaker between equal juniors — is real and disproportionately valuable for **client-facing** roles where likeability *is* the competency. Keep it, make it excellent, keep it to one line. The people who say "we don't care" are not harmed by it; the people who *do* care can be won by it.

## 7.2 Why it works: interview bait and small-talk fuel

An interests line is the cheapest way to **control what you get asked about.** Interviewers latch onto something concrete and human — it "*gives icebreakers for what would otherwise be kinda awkward conversations.*" A well-chosen interest steers the small talk onto ground you own, buys rapport in the first two minutes (which colours everything after), and — for coverage/sales — is a live demonstration of the exact skill the job needs: being someone a client enjoys talking to.

## 7.3 Specificity: why "reading" is dead

Every red-flag list names the same offenders — "*reading, watching films, listening to music, travelling, socialising with friends*" — because they "*tell the recruiter nothing about what makes you different*" *(Area CV; Gentis; CV Centre — Appendix §7)*. The fix is **specificity and a verb**: not "basketball" but "*played local-league basketball; organised weekly training and fixtures*"; not "photography" but "*film photography — a street-photography project I've shot for two years.*" Specificity turns you from an anonymous applicant into "*the marathon runner*" — a person the interviewer remembers.

## 7.5 Interests that backfire or read as try-hard

<div class="box warning" markdown="1">
<span class="lbl">Cut these</span>

- **Generic filler:** reading, films, music, travelling, socialising, cooking, "sports", "keeping fit" — signal nothing.
- **Faked passion:** anything you can't discuss for three minutes. WSO: you *will* get grilled — a poster who listed **chess** got quizzed on "*opening theory*." "*If you can't talk about it well, it's a red flag.*" The Trackr's test is whether you "*can excel at whatever it is you enjoy.*"
- **Try-hard "I love finance" hobbies:** listing "trading", "M&A modelling", "macroeconomics", "economic history" as *interests* reads as pandering and, per Kenji, as inauthentic if you can't speak to them passionately. Markets interest belongs in Experience/Extracurricular, not the hobbies line.
- **Polarising:** partisan politics, religion, controversial social issues — cut outright.
- **Risk/liability flags:** some recruiters read skydiving / base-jumping as a red flag; weigh it.
</div>

## 7.6 Your actual interests — assessed

Your CV lists: **"Boarding pass collecting, boxing, spice blending, supermarket own-brand taste tests."** This is, frankly, one of the strongest interest lines I've seen from a student — keep it, with light tuning:

<div class="box good" markdown="1">
<span class="lbl">Why yours already works</span>

- **Boarding-pass collecting** — Category D (unusual/memorable). A genuine curiosity hook; an interviewer *will* ask. Have a 20-second story ready (how many, oldest, why).
- **Boxing** — Category B (discipline/competitive/resilience). Signals exactly the "can take pressure" trait client-facing roles want. If you spar/compete or hold any grade, add it: "*boxing (train [N]×/week / white-collar bout)*."
- **Spice blending** — Category D (precision + patience + a bit memorable). Defensible and distinctive.
- **Supermarket own-brand taste tests** — quietly your best one. It's Category C **commercial awareness disguised as a quirk** — it signals genuine interest in consumer brands, pricing and value, and it *ties directly to your URBN retail pitch.* In a coverage/sales interview this is a gift: "*tell me about that*" leads straight to a conversation about consumer economics.
</div>

**The only risk:** all four are quirky/curiosity-type; a couple of interviewers prefer to see one "grind/team" signal too. Boxing covers that. If you ever want to rebalance, you could swap one quirk for a competitive/team item — but don't dilute what is already a memorable, defensible, on-brand line. Above all: **be ready to talk about each for two minutes.** The line's entire value is the conversation it starts.
