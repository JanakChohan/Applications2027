Part 2

The firm from the inside

LSEG as an operating model: who pays it, what it keeps, who it answers to, and where the Markets division you would join sits in the machine.

This part treats LSEG (London Stock Exchange Group) as a machine you could draw on a whiteboard, not as a brochure. Each section answers one plain question: where did it come from, how does money come in and go out, who has signed up to what, how is risk controlled, what has grown, why the share price fell, what the leaders say comes next, and where the bodies are buried. Every figure carries a source marker; the source table sits in Part 15.

LSEG in one breath

LSEG sells two things: trusted information (data, indices, screening) on multi-year subscriptions, and market plumbing (trading venues and clearing houses) paid per trade or per unit of risk. In 2025 it turned £8,986m of income into £4,523m of adjusted EBITDA and returned about £2.8bn to shareholders [S72]. You would join the plumbing side, the Markets division, which is about 40% of revenue and was the fastest-growing division in H1 2026 [S70, S71].

A. Origins and the founding insight

The first product was data, not trading

In 1698, at Jonathan's Coffee House in London, John Castaing began publishing a list of stock and commodity prices called "The Course of the Exchange and other things" [S78] Confirmed. That is the founding insight worth remembering: the business that became LSEG started by selling prices. People paid for a trusted number. Trading rules came later, in 1801, when "the first regulated exchange comes into existence in London" [S78].

Today LSEG calls itself "a leading financial markets infrastructure and data provider" [S74]. In a sense the group has come full circle: the bulk of its income is again from selling trusted information, with the trading and clearing machinery as the second engine Inferred.

Key term

Financial market infrastructure (FMI): the shared systems that let markets work: exchanges (where buyers meet sellers), clearing houses (which guarantee trades), settlement systems and data feeds. If an FMI stops, many firms stop with it, which is why FMIs are tightly supervised.

From coffee house to FTSE 100 group

Four shifts made the modern group Inferred framing: club to company (1986 Big Bang; listed on its own market in July 2001) [S78]; exchange to group (Borsa Italiana, 2007) [S78]; trading to clearing (majority of LCH, 2013, the deal that made LSEG systemically important) [S78]; and exchange to data and index giant (FTSE Group 1995, Frank Russell 2014, Refinitiv 2021, about $27bn) [S78, S84]. Refinitiv brought the Workspace desktop (then Eikon), real-time data, FXall and FX Matching, and the majority stake in Tradeweb [S300]. LSEG is now a FTSE 100 company, ticker LSEG [S70].

Source uncertain

Dates the research did not re-verify: demutualisation (around 2000), FTSE becoming wholly owned (reported as December 2011), the Refinitiv announcement (reported as August 2019) and David Schwimmer's start as CEO (2018) [S78]. The history page itself omits the 2021 Borsa Italiana sale. Use the years, not the exact days, for these.

Why this matters

The two failed combinations in the table below (Deutsche Börse 2017, HKEX 2019) share one lesson: LSEG's clearing assets are so important that regulators and the board treat any change of control as a public-interest question [S80, S82] Inferred. That is why LCH is run at arm's length with its own board, and why "open access" keeps coming up.

Key term

Total income excl. recoveries: LSEG's headline revenue. "Recoveries" are fees it collects for other firms' content (for example other exchanges' prices) and passes on, so they are stripped out.

Key term

Organic growth (constant currency): growth with acquisitions, disposals and exchange-rate moves removed. It answers "did the existing business sell more?" This is the number analysts watch.

Key term

EBITDA: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Roughly the operating profit before paying for past investment. "Adjusted" strips out one-offs. The margin is EBITDA divided by income.

Key term

Recurring revenue: income that repeats without a new sale each time, such as subscriptions and licences. It is valued highly because it is predictable.

Key term

The trace: from a customer's pound to dividends and buybacks

Follow one pound; Figure 11 draws the same chain to scale [S70, S72, S74] Inferred chain:

  1. A customer pays. A bank pays for Workspace seats and data feeds (D&A). An ETF issuer pays FTSE Russell a fee tied to the fund's assets. A bank pays for World-Check. A trader pays per trade on LSE, Turquoise, FXall or Tradeweb. A clearing member pays clearing fees to LCH and posts margin, on which LCH earns treasury income.
  2. Cost of sales (£1,113m in 2025) mostly pays for third-party content and revenue shares, such as the SwapClear surplus share to founding banks. Gross profit: £8,233m.
  3. Operating costs (£3,711m adjusted, 71% people) leave adjusted EBITDA of £4,523m, a 50.3% margin.
  4. Investment and claims: capex of £919m (10.2% of income), tax at about 24%, interest, and the minorities' share (Tradeweb's other owners above all).
  5. Equity free cash flow of £2.4bn (guided at least £2.7bn for 2026) funds dividends (£718m cash in 2025) and buybacks (£2.1bn), within a target of 1.5x to 2.5x net debt to EBITDA (1.8x at Dec 2025, 2.1x at June 2026).
Key term

Capex (capital expenditure): money spent on long-lived assets, mostly software and platforms here. It does not hit EBITDA, but it does use cash. LSEG wants it down from 10.2% of income to about 8% by 2029 [S72].

Key term

Share buyback: the company buys its own shares and cancels them, so each remaining share owns more of the profits. It returns cash without committing to a higher dividend every year.

Key term

AEPS (adjusted earnings per share): profit per share excluding one-offs and acquisition amortisation. FY2025: 420.6p, +15.7% [S72].

How the money has grown

Income excl. recoveries rose from £7,428m in 2022 to £8,986m in 2025, with organic growth between 6.3% and 7.7% every year and the EBITDA margin rising from 47.8% to 50.3% [S73, S77, S76, S72]. Adjusted EPS rose from 272.4p (2021) to 420.6p (2025) and the dividend from 107.0p (2022) to 150.0p (2025) [S73, S77, S72]. Staff numbers rose more slowly than income: 27,386 at end 2024, 28,516 at end 2025 [S74].

Why this matters: the partnership pattern

LSEG keeps signing deals where customers co-own the infrastructure: founding banks in SwapClear, 11 banks in Post Trade Solutions, the user community in Turquoise (15.8% minority) [S72, S300]. D. The risk and control architecture

LSEG is not one regulated firm. It is a set of separately licensed companies, each with its own regulator, board and capital. Regulation forces that: a clearing house takes on the risk of every member defaulting, so its money must be ring-fenced from the exchange's [S304] Inferred rationale.

Key term

RIE (Recognised Investment Exchange): the UK legal status of a full regulated exchange, granted and supervised by the FCA. The London Stock Exchange is one.

Key term

MTF (Multilateral Trading Facility): a trading venue run by an investment firm under lighter rules than an exchange. Turquoise and FXall's venues are MTFs.

Key term

CCP (central counterparty): a clearing house that becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. If one side defaults, the CCP still pays the other. LCH is LSEG's CCP group.

Key term

Initial margin: collateral a member posts up front to cover the loss LCH could suffer closing out that member's positions if it failed. Variation margin moves daily with prices.

The LCH default waterfall, in order

LCH's own words: "The collateral and default fund contributions of the defaulting Clearing Member are utilised first, followed by the CCP's own funds and then by the default fund contributions of non-defaulting Clearing Members." [S93]

Scale: total margin and default fund collateral across LSEG's CCPs was £277bn at 31 Dec 2025 (cash £91bn, non-cash £186bn), peaking at £303bn during 2025 (£334bn in 2024). The £90bn cash portfolio was 99.95% invested securely, with a weighted average maturity of 82 days [S74]. Track record: LCH says it has "managed 9 member defaults and, in all cases, losses were within the initial margin held" [S351]; in the 2008 Lehman default SwapClear handled a $9trn book using about 35% of Lehman's initial margin [S354].

Key term

Default fund: a pot every clearing member pays into, used only after the defaulter's own margin and contribution and the CCP's own capital are exhausted. It mutualises extreme losses.

Key term

Skin in the game: the CCP's own money placed in the waterfall ahead of other members' funds, so management loses first if its risk models are wrong.

Three lines of defence and the group risk framework

"LSEG operates a three lines of defence model, providing appropriate segregation of duties and clear roles and responsibilities, including Risk, Compliance and Internal Audit." [S74]

  • First line: the business owns its risks. Example: a "First Line Business Continuity Management team at LCH Ltd" running switch tests and recovery-site tests [S318].
  • Second line: Risk and Compliance give independent oversight and challenge. Group Risk has central teams (policy, tooling, cyber, third-party risk) and coverage teams for divisions including Markets [S314].
  • Third line: Internal Audit gives independent assurance [S74].

An Enterprise Risk Management Framework and a Group Risk Taxonomy feed risk appetite statements the Board approves each year. Committees include the Board Risk Committee, the Board Audit Committee and an executive Group Executive Risk Committee with sub-committees for financial, model, and technology and cyber risk [S74]. Each principal risk has a named executive owner: central counterparty risk sits with the Head of Markets, technology and cyber with the CIO, regulatory change with the General Counsel and CEO [S302].

Key term

Three lines of defence: the people who take a risk manage it (1st), a separate team checks them (2nd), and auditors check both (3rd). The JD's "risk teams" track lives mostly in the first and second lines.

Why this produces stability Inferred

The waterfall puts the defaulter's money first, so members have a reason to manage their own risk. LCH's capital comes next, so its managers carry real downside. Mutual funds are a last resort sized by stress tests. Commercially, stability comes from high retention (92.8% subscription revenue retention in H1 2026), multi-year data contracts and a mix of data and transaction income: the Annual Report calls this "all-weather growth" [S70, S74].

New: an AI pricing framework

In H1 2026 LSEG set out how it will charge for AI use: a recurring subscription to license its datasets for AI models; a recurring charge for MCP access, with usage bands to come; and AI Search priced into the annual Workspace price review up to a usage cap, with Deep Research as a premium add-on [S70]. Key term

MCP (Model Context Protocol): an open standard for connecting AI applications to data sources. LSEG uses it to pipe licensed data into customers' AI tools.

F. LSEG as a public company: the derating, told straight

LSEG is listed on the London Stock Exchange, ticker LSEG, and is in the FTSE 100 [S70]. Its own share price is the market's running verdict on the strategy. Over 2025 and early 2026 that verdict turned sharply negative, even while the business kept growing.

The sequence

  1. Peak: a close of 12,095p on 5 Feb 2025 [S90].
  2. 31 Jul 2025, H1 2025 results: shares fell 7.9% on the day as growth in subscription value (ASV) slowed; Bloomberg headline: "LSEG Shares Plunge on Slowing Growth in Subscription Value" [S90, S102, S103] Reported.
  3. 3 Feb 2026, the AI scare: down 12.8% in one day (8,234p to 7,180p) as worries that large language models, "like Anthropic's Claude", would let customers bypass terminals and aggregated data triggered a software selloff. Low close 7,170p on 4 Feb 2026, about 41% below the peak [S90, S88].

The two sides of the argument

The bear case

Redburn: shifting to "modular, API- and AI-driven data consumption may prove structurally deflationary for parts of the model" [S89]. UBS's Michael Werner: "There is still a 'show me' story for AI. It's one thing to have usage, it's another to start charging people." [S88] Workflows growth is under 3% [S70].

The bull case

Schwimmer: "90% of our data revenues come from real-time or data that is proprietary." [S71] About 98% of revenue comes from proprietary data, IP and market infrastructure [S73]. MCP customers rose from 60 trialling (Feb 2026) to over 200 engaged (Jul 2026) [S72, S70]. Markets, untouched by the AI worry, grew 11.9% [S70].

Key term

Derating: the share price falls further than profits, so the multiple investors pay for each pound of earnings shrinks. LSEG fell to about 18 times forward earnings (share price divided by next year's expected earnings per share) [S88].

Say this in the interview: "why join a stock that fell 40%?"

"The share price and the business told different stories. From the 2025 peak to the February low the shares fell about 41%, but over the same period income grew 7% and H1 2026 was a record half. The fall was about one fear: that AI tools shrink the value of terminals and aggregated data. The division I'm applying to, Markets, is mostly exchanges and clearing, the plumbing that AI trading still has to run through, and it grew 11.9% with a 58.6% margin. So I see the derating as a debate about one part of the group, and I'd rather join the part that's growing fastest while that debate is being settled."

Don't say this

"The market has got it wrong" or "the stock is cheap." You do not know, and interviewers at a listed company are careful about share price talk. Talk about the business, not the price.
