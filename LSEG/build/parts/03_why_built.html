Part 3

Why LSEG is built this way

LSEG is a set of separately licensed businesses held together by shared data, technology and customers, and kept apart on purpose where trust depends on it.

The short answer, and how to use this part

Part 2 explained what LSEG sells and how the money flows. This part answers the harder interview question: why is it put together like this? An interviewer who asks "why LSEG?" or "how does the Markets division fit with the rest of the group?" is checking whether you can connect the dots, which is the JD's own phrase. The answer has four parts, and each needs a mechanism, not an adjective.

Key terms for this part

RIE (Recognised Investment Exchange): the UK legal status of a full stock exchange, supervised by the FCA. London Stock Exchange plc is one. MTF (Multilateral Trading Facility): a trading venue run under lighter rules than an exchange; Turquoise and the LSEG FX venues are MTFs. CCP (central counterparty): a clearing house that becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, so one bank's failure does not spread. LCH is LSEG's CCP group. Open access: a CCP clears trades from any venue on the same terms, rather than only from its owner's venues. Vertical silo: one group owns the venue, the CCP and often the settlement system, and trades flow straight down inside the group.

There are seven mechanisms behind this shape. The first four explain why the businesses are kept apart. The last three explain why LSEG still wants to own them all.

Mechanism 1: regulation forces separate entities with their own capital and boards

A CCP, in LCH's own words, "sits in the middle of trades as the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. If either party defaults on the trade, the Company owns the defaulter's risk" [S304]. That risk has to be fenced off. LCH Ltd has its own board and its own audit, remuneration, risk and operational resilience committees, and the Bank of England supervises it [S304]. The exchange is an RIE supervised by the FCA; the FX MTFs are licensed separately in Ireland and the UK [S341]. Confirmed

The logic: if a problem in one business could drain capital from another, a failure at a data business could weaken the CCP that guarantees trillions of dollars of swaps. Separate entities with separate capital stop that. Inferred

Mechanism 2: open access is written into LCH's constitution

When LSEG bought control of LCH.Clearnet in 2012 to 2013, the UK competition authority (the OFT) recorded that an open-access provision, "considered a core operating principle", would go into LCH's Articles of Association, the company's legal rulebook [S305]:

The clause to know by heart

"LCH.Clearnet's services must be offered on terms that are fair, reasonable, open and non-discriminatory, and on a basis such that LCH.Clearnet's risk is adequately controlled. No exchange will be favoured over any other and LSEG's trading services users will not be favoured over any other exchange's users." [S305] Confirmed

LCH Ltd still describes itself as "operating through an open access model that clears for major exchanges and platforms as well as a range of over-the-counter ('OTC') markets" [S304]. LSEG's 2025 Annual Report puts it as strategy: "When other exchange groups focused on vertical integration of trading and clearing, we championed open access" [S300]. So LSEG cannot run LCH as a captive that only serves its own venues. It chose not to, and it is legally bound not to.

Mechanism 3: horizontal, not a vertical silo

The OFT described the two ways to build a CCP: "stand-alone companies such as LCH.Clearnet or vertically integrated with a trading venue such as Deutsche Börse/Eurex Clearing" [S305]. Deutsche Börse uses the same words about itself: its plans "are based on the Group's vertically integrated business model ... The broad coverage of the entire value chain continues to be a key success factor" [S127]. Figure 18 shows the two shapes side by side.

In a silo, the venue and its CCP pull each other along: trade on Eurex and you clear at Eurex Clearing. In LSEG's horizontal model, LCH competes for flow from any venue, and LSEG's own venues get no privileged route in. Confirmed for the descriptions; the competitive logic is Inferred.

Mechanism 4: user governance keeps the banks inside

Before 2013, LCH was "owned 83 per cent by its clearing members and 17 per cent by trading venues" [S305]. The banks that use a CCP are also the ones whose money is at risk if another member fails, so they want a say. The 2012 deal kept that say:

  • LSEG appointed only 4 of 17 directors at the start, alongside user directors, venue directors and independent non-executives [S305].
  • Important "push matters" needed "60 per cent of votes cast, which must consist of at least 25 per cent of user shareholders" [S305].
  • Today "representatives of clearing members sit on the Company's Board", and the LCH Ltd Board Risk Committee includes clearing member and client representatives, "with no one constituent of the Risk Committee having a majority" [S304].
  • Product Advisory Groups and Risk Working Groups let participants comment on "risk policies, models and frameworks" [S304].
Source uncertain

The 4-of-17 board and the 60%/25% voting rule are from the 2012 OFT decision. LSEG now owns about 94% of LCH, so those exact numbers have probably changed. What is confirmed for 2025 is the member seats on the board and the risk committee rule [S304]. Quote the 2012 rules as history, not as today's governance.

Mechanism 6: venues create data, and data is what LSEG sells most

LSEG's investor deck has a page called "Our integrated offering creates more value" with four panels: "Creating our own data flywheel", "Fully integrated workflow", "End-to-end offering" and "Integrated enterprise offering" [S303]. A flywheel here means a loop where each part speeds up the next: trading creates prices, prices become data and indices, and data products bring customers back to trade.

The concrete evidence:

Open access helps the flywheel: the more venues clear at LCH, the more LCH data exists to sell into Workspace. Inferred

Mechanism 7: the Refinitiv logic

The 2021 Refinitiv deal brought in FXall and FX Matching, the Workspace desktop, real-time data, and the majority stake in Tradeweb [S320, S300]. LSEG's stated pitch: "The combination of our trade lifecycle and data value chains is unmatched. We increasingly combine the two to develop differentiated products" [S303]. A trade lifecycle is the chain from pre-trade data to execution, clearing, settlement and reporting; a data value chain is the chain from raw prices to cleaned data, analytics and indices. Refinitiv gave LSEG the second chain at scale, to sit beside the first.

Why open access pays for itself: the netting mechanism

If open access lets customers leave, why choose it? Because a CCP's product gets better as it gets bigger. Netting means offsetting a bank's bought and sold positions in the same product so it only posts margin on the difference. Initial margin (IM) is the collateral a member posts up front to cover losses if it fails. The more trades from more venues that sit in one CCP, the more of them cancel out, and the less margin each bank has to post. Figure 19 shows the mechanism with invented numbers.

Two real numbers show this at work. SwapClear holds over 90% of cleared interest rate swap notional outstanding, by LSEG's own claim [S303], and in 2023 it compressed 7.8 million trades [S356]. Compression means tearing up offsetting trades and replacing them with fewer, smaller ones that carry the same risk. A pool that big is hard for a rival to match, which is why open access gives LCH more than it takes away. Inferred

Say this in the interview

"LSEG is built as separate regulated businesses partly because regulators require it, but mainly because LCH's value depends on being neutral. Its articles say no exchange will be favoured, so trades come in from rival venues and bilateral deals as well as LSEG's own. More trades in one pool means more netting and less margin for everyone, which is why SwapClear holds over 90% of cleared swaps. The cost is real: Euronext could take its clearing away, and it did."

Don't say this

"LSEG is an integrated exchange that keeps trading and clearing in-house." That describes Deutsche Börse's model, not LSEG's. Also avoid "LCH is 82.6% owned": the current figure is about 94% [S300, S121].

Against: the banks still take 10% for 20 years, and the cash could have funded buybacks. Suggested position: worth it, because it bought both margin and loyalty in the business with the deepest moat; but say you would want to see how the 10% share is measured and reviewed.

Why this matters

The pattern is clear: LSEG's businesses meet through data, distribution, technology and account management. They do not meet through clearing favours. That is the line the law draws (Figure 20), and it is what lets LSEG say "open model" and "integrated offering" in the same breath. Inferred

Where they deliberately do not work together

  • No clearing favours. LCH must not favour LSEG venues or their users [S305].
  • Information barriers. The OFT required "conflict of interest provisions and information barriers" so that rival venues could talk to LCH "assured of the confidentiality of those discussions from LSEG" [S305].
  • A listed subsidiary with its own board. Tradeweb has its own share price, buybacks and board, and 49.1% of its economics belong to others [S300, S137]. Group decisions must respect Tradeweb's public shareholders. Inferred
  • Client confidentiality. The FX Global Code requires firms to "clearly and effectively identify and appropriately limit access to Confidential Information" (Principle 19) [S343], and LSEG's Code says staff "must never disclose confidential information... unless there is a legitimate need to know" [S307].
Don't say this

"LSEG bundles SwapClear clearing with Tradeweb swaps trading", or "ForexClear is sold together with FXall". No LSEG statement ties these pairs commercially, and the open-access clause makes it unlikely [S305]. If you want to show you know the links, use RepoAgent, FTSE Russell pricing off Tradeweb, or FXall inside Workspace instead.

Say this in the interview

"The businesses meet through data, distribution and technology rather than through clearing. RepoAgent is the clearest joint product: Post Trade Solutions and Tradeweb built it together to reduce repo settlement fails. And LSEG says a large proportion of FXall's functionality is now inside Workspace. I'd be curious how the FX team measures whether clients actually move their workflow across."

What they buy, and why they accept the fees

Customers do not buy "infrastructure". They buy six concrete things, each of which saves them money, capital or risk that they would otherwise carry. Figure 21 maps them.

1. Default protection that has been tested

When Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, SwapClear held a "$9 trillion OTC portfolio" of "over 66,000 trades", backed by "$2 billion" of Lehman's initial margin. LCH used "only 35% of its initial margin", ran five currency auctions and had all positions dealt with by week three [S354]. No default fund money was used [S354, S355]. LCH says it has "managed 9 member defaults and, in all cases, losses were within the initial margin held" [S351]. Confirmed Part 2 explains the default waterfall that sits behind this.

Key term

Default fund and waterfall: if a member fails, its own margin and default fund contribution are used first, then LCH's own capital ("skin in the game"), then other members' default fund contributions [S351]. Lehman never got past the first step.

2. Netting and compression free up capital

See Figure 19. LCH charges fees for compression, and OTC Derivatives revenue rose 11.6% in 2025 "driven by growth in clearing and compression activity" [S301, S304]. 7.8 million SwapClear trades were compressed in 2023 [S356]; a 2025 figure was not found.

3. Margin efficiency

LCH sets initial margin at "an enhanced 99.7% confidence interval" [S351], which means the margin should cover losses on all but about 3 days in 1,000. ForexClear uses "historical simulation expected shortfall... ten years of historical market data" [S352]. Customers can post bonds rather than cash: average non-cash collateral was €209.6bn against €101.3bn of cash in 2025 [S301]. Cash tied up in margin earns little, so being able to post bonds instead is worth money to the customer. Inferred

4. Liquidity in one place

SwapClear has over 90% of cleared interest rate swap notional outstanding [S303]. It cleared $1,941 trillion of notional in 2025 and a record 13.4 million trades [S301, S304]. Tradeweb's average daily volume (ADV) was $2.6 trillion in 2025 and $3.2 trillion in H1 2026 [S301, S302]. Liquidity attracts liquidity: a buyer goes where most sellers already are.

5. Data accuracy

"Data quality issues raised by customers have fallen 51% despite total content volumes rising 113%" over four years [S301]. For a bank, a wrong price in a risk model is a regulatory and financial problem, so accuracy is part of what it pays for.

6. Access to capital

Debt issuers use LSE's markets to raise money from professional investors, with LSEG's own figure of 1,300 borrowers raising $500 to $600bn a year [S316]. Part 6 covers the primary markets process. Reported
