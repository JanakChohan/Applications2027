The industry from zero

What financial market infrastructure is, how money moves through it, who pays for it, and the forces that will reshape it while you are at LSEG.

This Part assumes you know nothing about how markets are wired together. By the end you should be able to do three things in an interview: walk through a trade from start to finish and name the LSEG business at each step, explain how an exchange group makes money (and why it is mostly not from the stock exchange), and give a reasoned view on where the industry goes in the next three to five years.

What financial market infrastructure is

Think of a financial market as a giant marketplace. The traders (banks, fund managers, hedge funds) are the shoppers and stallholders. Financial market infrastructure is the plumbing that lets them deal safely: the shops, the tills, the receipts and the delivery vans. FMIs do not usually take bets themselves. They make it possible for others to trade, and they charge a toll for doing so. Inferred

Key term

FMI (financial market infrastructure): the shared systems that let financial trades happen and complete: trading venues, clearing houses, settlement systems, trade repositories, plus (in a wider sense) the index and data businesses that sit around them. LSEG calls itself a financial markets infrastructure and data group, not an exchange.

Key term

Regulated market vs MTF: both are trading venues. A regulated market is the "full" exchange, where admission brings the heaviest disclosure rules (for example a prospectus). An MTF is lighter. That is why LSE runs its Main Market as a regulated market but AIM (for growth companies) and ISM (for professional-only bonds) as MTFs [S176].

Key term

Clearing house (CCP): after a trade is agreed, the CCP becomes the counterparty to both sides. It holds margin (collateral each member posts) and a default fund (a shared pot members pay into) so one firm's failure does not spread. Not a bank: it mutualises risk rather than lending.

The point to carry into interview. Most firms in this table do one or two of these jobs. LSEG does five at once: exchange (LSE), MTF (Turquoise, AIM, ISM), CCP (LCH), index provider (FTSE Russell) and data vendor (Data & Analytics). That "vertical" mix is why it describes itself as an infrastructure and data group rather than a stock exchange. The trade lifecycle, step by step

Every trade, whether a share, a bond, a swap or a currency, goes through the same five steps. Learn them in order. The interviewer question "walk me through what happens when someone buys a share" is really a test of whether you know this chain.

  1. Pre-trade data. Before trading, an investor looks at prices, quotes, news, indices and analytics. Data vendors and venues sell this. (LSEG Data & Analytics, LSE market data, FTSE Russell index levels.)
  2. Execution. The buyer and seller agree a price and size, either on a venue (an exchange, an MTF, an RFQ platform like Tradeweb, an FX platform like FXall) or directly between two parties, which is called over the counter (OTC).
  3. Clearing. The trade is confirmed and, if centrally cleared, a CCP like LCH becomes the counterparty to both sides and collects margin.
  4. Settlement. Cash and securities actually change hands at a CSD, or for FX through a system like CLS. Under the UK's planned T+1 cycle, settlement must happen no later than one business day after the trade, from 11 October 2027 [S158]. Confirmed
  5. Post-trade reporting. Trades are published (transparency) and reported to the regulator or a trade repository. Consolidated tapes pull published trade data from all venues into one feed [S155].
Key term

Netting: offsetting buys against sells so only the net amount needs settling or margining. The more trades sit in one CCP, the more can be netted, which lowers members' margin. This is the network effect behind LCH SwapClear's dominance. Inferred

Key term

T+1: settlement one business day after the trade date ("T"). The UK and EU both plan to move from two days to one on 11 October 2027 [S158, S159]. Less time to fix errors means more automation.

Key term

Settlement risk and PvP: in FX you pay one currency and wait for the other. If the counterparty fails in between, you lose the full amount (also called Herstatt risk). PvP (payment versus payment) settles both legs at once or not at all. CLS, operating since September 2002, is the main PvP system [S204]. About $1.4tn a day of FX still settles with no protection [S205]. Confirmed

Key term

CLOB (central limit order book): a matching engine that lists every bid and offer by price and time and matches them automatically. Anonymous, continuous, good for small standard trades.

Key term

RFQ (request for quote): the client sends a request to, say, five dealers, they reply with prices, the client picks one. Good for large or illiquid trades where nobody wants to show their hand in public.

Key term

Liquidity: how easily you can trade something in size without moving its price. A "liquidity pool" is a place where many buyers and sellers meet. Liquidity attracts liquidity: traders go where other traders are, which is why venues compete hard and why market share is sticky once won. Inferred

Why the order book vs RFQ split matters for the JD. Shares trade mostly on order books, bonds and FX mostly by RFQ or bilateral dealing. Tradeweb (RFQ) contributed £1,539m to LSEG Markets in 2025 under "Fixed Income, Derivatives & Other", far more than the £412m from Equities [S120]. Confirmed So the RFQ world is where most of LSEG's trading income sits, not the famous order book. Inferred

Don't say this

"LSEG makes most of its money from the stock exchange." It does not. Data & Analytics alone is about 44% of income, and the equities trading line is £412m out of nearly £9bn [S120]. Say "LSEG is a data and infrastructure group; the London Stock Exchange is the brand people know, not the biggest earner."

Key term

Tokenisation: representing a security or asset as a digital token on a shared ledger (DLT, distributed ledger technology). The promise is faster settlement and easier movement of collateral. The threat to incumbents is that token-native systems could bypass today's post-trade chain. The Bank of England's line: "Real-time payments, electronic securities settlement and central clearing were once novel concepts. Today, they are foundational to the functioning of the financial system" [S184].

Key term

Consolidated tape (CT): one feed combining trade data from every venue for an asset class, so users do not have to buy data from each venue separately. The UK bond tape is run by ETS Connect UK [S155].

Your opinion goes here

Prompt: "Where do you see our industry in five years?"

Side A (optimist): Oliver Wyman's base case of about 8% a year to around $200bn by 2030 [S185], record data spend [S188] and accelerating subscriptions at LSEG [S195] say the incumbents keep compounding. Network effects in clearing (LCH has 99.995% of GBP SONIA swaps [S140]) are very hard to break.

Side B (disruption): Up to $45bn of revenue could move to token-native rails [S185]; about $12bn is exposed to AI [S185]; regulators are actively pushing data prices down [S165]; the EU is pulling clearing away [S160]. LSEG shares once fell 13% in a single day on AI fears [S146] Reported, so investors take the threat seriously.

Suggested position: "I think the core plumbing (clearing, venues, benchmarks) gets more valuable, not less, because AI and tokens both need trusted data and trusted settlement. The pressure falls on undifferentiated analytics and on per-unit data pricing. The winners will be groups that turn their data into something AI can use and that own the post-trade rails tokenised assets will need. The signals I would watch are PvP adoption in FX, DSS firms going live, take-up of the tapes, and whether EMIR 3 moves real euro swap share." Inferred
