03Alternatives from zero: how the industry actually makes money

This section assumes you know nothing beyond your Pictet experience. If you understand the six ideas below, you will follow every session on the day and you will not be the person nodding blankly.

1. What "alternative" means

Alternative simply means not public stocks, bonds or cash. It is defined by what it is not. It is not a statement about risk, and it is not exotic. At Pictet you were on the traditional side: funds that buy listed securities, priced daily, that a client can buy and sell more or less at will. Alternatives are the other side: assets bought and held privately, valued quarterly by estimate rather than by a live market price, and locked up for years.

The industry has gone from roughly $7.2 trillion of assets in 2014 to over $20 trillion today. Private equity alone went from $744bn in 2004 to $9.7 trillion by December 2024. This is not a niche any more; it is where a very large share of the industry's fee income and hiring now sits.

2. GP and LP: the two sides of every conversation

Every fund has two sides, and almost every piece of jargon you will hear on the day refers to one of them.

TermWho they areWhat they want
LP
Limited Partner		The investors who supply the money: pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, endowments, foundations, family offices, and increasingly private wealth clientsReturns above what public markets give them, diversification, and — critically right now — their cash back on time
GP
General Partner		The firm itself: Blackstone, KKR, Ares, EQT, Permira. The people who raise the fund and invest itTo raise the next, larger fund, which requires having returned cash on the last one

The Pictet bridge. Your Global Client Group work — onboarding, servicing, relationship building — is the LP-facing function. In an alternatives firm that same function is called Investor Relations, Client Solutions or Capital Formation, it is more technical, it is more commercially prized, and it is currently one of the fastest-hiring parts of the industry. You already have a year of the hardest-to-teach part of it: dealing with actual institutional clients. Hold that thought until Section 5.

3. The fund lifecycle: why nothing happens quickly

A closed-end private fund runs about ten to twelve years, in four phases:

  1. Fundraise (1–2 years). The GP goes to LPs and asks for commitments. An LP commits, say, £50m. Crucially they do not hand it over. It sits with them until called.
  2. Investment period (roughly 5 years). The GP finds deals and issues capital calls — "send £8m in ten working days." Committed-but-not-yet-called money across the industry is called dry powder.
  3. Hold and value creation (3–7 years per asset). The GP owns and improves the business or asset.
  4. Exit and distribution. The GP sells, returns capital to LPs, and keeps a share of the profit. Then it goes back to step one with a bigger fund.

4. How the GP gets paid, and why it drives everything

  • Management fee — roughly 1.5–2% a year, historically on committed capital rather than invested capital. This pays salaries and rent. It is predictable and it is the reason firms want ever-larger funds.
  • Carried interest ("carry") — typically 20% of profits, but only above a hurdle (also called the preferred return, usually 8% a year to LPs first). This is where real wealth is made and it is why the industry is obsessed with exits.

You will hear "two and twenty". That is the shorthand for the model. Fee pressure on the "two" is a live commercial issue, covered in Section 7.

5. The five metrics you must not confuse

MetricWhat it measuresThe catch
IRRAnnualised percentage return, sensitive to timingCan be flattered by borrowing at the fund level to delay capital calls, or by an early quick win. A high IRR does not mean anyone got paid
MOICMultiple on invested capital. 2.0x means you doubled the moneyIgnores time completely. 2.0x in three years and 2.0x in twelve years are not the same investment
TVPITotal value to paid-in. Cash returned plus the estimated value of what is still held, over cash put inThe "plus estimated value" half is the GP's own mark, not a market price
DPIDistributions to paid-in. Cash actually returned, over cash put in. Real money in an LP's bank accountNothing. This is the honest one, and it is why DPI is currently the most watched number in the industry
J-curveThe shape of returns over a fund's life: negative early, positive laterFees are charged from day one while value takes years to appear, so early performance always looks bad

If you say one technical thing on the day, make it this: "DPI has become more important than IRR." It is true, it is current, and it demonstrates you understand that the industry's present problem is liquidity rather than performance. Section 7 gives you the numbers behind it.

6. Where returns come from in a buyout

Three levers, and only three. Every case study answer you ever give will use this frame:

  • Earnings growth — grow the company's EBITDA. Revenue growth, margin improvement, bolt-on acquisitions. This is the only lever that is unambiguously value creation, and it is the one that impresses interviewers.
  • Debt paydown — the company's own cash flow repays the borrowing, so the equity slice grows even if nothing else changes.
  • Multiple expansion — sell at a higher multiple of earnings than you paid. Partly skill, largely market conditions. Interviewers are sceptical of cases that rely on it.

04The asset classes, one at a time

Session one of the programme is called "Asset Class Discovery". This is that session, in advance. For each one: what it is, what a junior person actually does all day, and who the recognisable firms are.

Private equity · buyout · the default meaning of "PE"

Leveraged buyouts

What it is. Buy control of an established, cash-generative company using a mix of borrowed money and fund equity. Own it for three to seven years, make it bigger and better, sell it. The debt is borrowed against the company itself, not the fund, which is what makes returns on the equity slice large in both directions.

What a junior does. Screening companies against an investment thesis, building LBO models, reading commercial due diligence from consultants, writing investment committee memos, sitting on calls with lawyers and bankers, and monitoring portfolio companies once owned.

Firms. Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Carlyle, TPG, Bain Capital, Advent, CVC, EQT, Permira, Cinven, Hg, Bridgepoint, Silver Lake.

Private equity · minority

Growth equity and venture capital

Growth equity buys minority stakes in companies that are already growing fast and often already profitable. Little or no debt, so returns come almost entirely from the business getting bigger. Venture capital is the earlier, riskier version: most investments fail, and the fund's return is carried by one or two outliers. This is called the power law, and it makes VC a fundamentally different job to buyout — more judgement about people and markets, much less modelling.

Firms. General Atlantic, Summit, TA Associates, Insight (growth); Index, Balderton, Accel, Atomico (venture).

Private credit · the fastest-growing corner

Direct lending and private credit

What it is. Lending money directly to companies rather than buying their equity. Banks retreated from mid-market leveraged lending after the financial crisis and after regulation made it expensive; funds moved in. You earn a spread over a floating base rate, plus fees. You do not get the upside if the company triples in value, but you get paid first if it fails.

Why it matters right now. This asset class has grown to over $2 trillion and it is currently having its first proper stress test. See Section 7 — it is the single richest source of commercial awareness material available to you.

What a junior does. Credit analysis, downside cases, covenant modelling ("at what point does this company breach its terms?"), documentation review, monitoring borrowers.

Firms. Ares, Blue Owl, HPS, Blackstone Credit, Golub, Arcmont, Park Square, Barings, Pemberton.

Real assets

Infrastructure

What it is. Physical assets that society needs and that produce long, predictable, often inflation-linked cash flows: airports, toll roads, water and energy networks, fibre, renewables, and increasingly data centres. Target returns are lower than buyout — roughly 8–12% for "core" and 12–18% for "value-add" — but the cash flows are far more certain, which is why pension funds and insurers love it.

What a junior does. Long-dated cash flow models rather than LBO models, regulatory and concession analysis, energy price forecasting, and a lot of work on how the asset is financed.

Firms. Brookfield, Macquarie Asset Management, Global Infrastructure Partners (now BlackRock), Antin, KKR Infrastructure, EQT Infrastructure.

Real assets

Real estate

What it is. Property, in both equity and debt form, sorted along a risk spectrum: core (fully let, prime, low return), core-plus, value-add (refurbish and re-let), opportunistic (development, distress). Logistics and data centres have been the structural winners; offices the structural question mark.

Firms. Blackstone Real Estate (the largest owner of commercial property in the world), Brookfield, Westbrook Partners, QuadReal, MARK Capital Management, plus advisers like CBRE. Several of these are SEO London sponsors.

Public markets, alternative strategy

Hedge funds

What it is. The odd one out. Hedge funds trade public securities, so the assets are not private at all; what makes them alternative is the strategy and the structure — the ability to short, to use leverage and derivatives, and to charge performance fees. Broad families: long/short equity, global macro, credit, event-driven, quantitative, and multi-strategy "pod shops" where many small teams run tightly risk-limited books under one roof.

What a junior does. Depends entirely on the strategy. At a pod shop: deep single-name research, earnings models, and living inside a strict risk framework where losing money quickly ends the seat.

Firms. Millennium, Citadel, Point72, Balyasny, Marshall Wace, Man Group, Brevan Howard.

The plumbing · increasingly where the growth is

Secondaries, fund-of-funds and GP stakes

Secondaries buy existing positions in private funds from LPs who want out early, usually at a discount, and increasingly buy assets from GPs directly through continuation vehicles. Because the fund is already several years old when you buy it, you skip most of the J-curve and see cash back sooner. This is now a record-sized market and one of the most interesting places to work; see Section 7. GP stakes funds buy minority equity in the management companies themselves — you are buying the fee stream rather than the deals.

Firms. Ardian, Lexington, Coller, Blackstone Strategic Partners, HarbourVest (secondaries); Blue Owl's GP Strategic Capital and Petershill (GP stakes).

How to talk about asset classes without faking expertise

Nobody expects you to have a view on infrastructure valuation five days from now. What signals genuine interest is being able to say why the differences exist: that private credit is paid first and capped on the upside while buyout equity is paid last and uncapped; that infrastructure accepts a lower return in exchange for certainty; that secondaries exist because the ten-year lock-up does not suit every investor. Structural understanding beats memorised firm names every time.

"Case Study interview prep"

What it is. Training for the assessment that decides most alternatives hires. It comes in three formats:

  • Paper LBO. Delivered cold, verbally, no Excel. You are given round numbers and asked to reach a return. Ten to fifteen minutes. Tests whether the mechanics are actually in your head.
  • Modelling test. One to three hours in Excel, building an LBO from a blank sheet or a template.
  • Take-home case. You get an information memorandum on a company, anywhere from two hours to seven days, and produce a recommendation — invest or pass — with a model behind it, then defend it.

What is actually being tested. Not arithmetic. Interviewers want to know whether you can take a position and defend it. The commonest failure is a candidate who builds a beautiful model and then refuses to say whether they would buy the company. The second commonest is a candidate whose case rests entirely on selling at a higher multiple than they paid.

Get out of it The structure of a good answer, and one worked example you can rebuild from memory. See the paper LBO below — take it into the session so you are following rather than starting from zero.

A paper LBO, worked, so the session is not your first exposure

The prompt. "We buy a business with £100m of EBITDA at 10x, funded half with debt. EBITDA grows 5% a year. The company pays down £150m of debt over five years. We exit at the same 10x. What is the return?"

  1. Entry. £100m × 10 = £1,000m enterprise value. Funded with £500m debt and £500m equity.
  2. Grow. £100m growing 5% for five years → £128m EBITDA.
  3. Delever. £500m debt less £150m repaid = £350m net debt at exit.
  4. Exit. £128m × 10 = £1,280m enterprise value. Equity = £1,280m − £350m = £930m.
  5. Return. £930m / £500m = 1.86x over five years, which is roughly a 13% IRR.

Learn these four conversions so you never need a calculator: over five years, 2.0x ≈ 15%, 2.5x ≈ 20%, 3.0x ≈ 25%. Over three years, 2.0x ≈ 26%.

Then add the judgement. "At 13% this deal does not clear a typical 20% target on those assumptions, so the case has to come from somewhere else — faster earnings growth, bolt-on acquisitions, or buying below 10x. I would not underwrite multiple expansion." That last sentence is what gets you hired.

07Commercial awareness: the six arguments happening right now

You asked how this connects to what is in the financial press. It connects tightly. Below are the six live debates in alternatives as of September 2026, each with the numbers, and each with a line on how you would actually use it. Verify anything you plan to quote in an interview — figures move.

01 · the defining problem

The liquidity drought: nobody is getting their money back

Private equity funds have been unable to sell their companies at the pace they promised. Annual distributions to investors have been stuck at 8–10% of net asset value for three years, against a historical norm of about 20%. The IPO window has not properly reopened, strategic buyers are selective, and sponsor-to-sponsor deals face valuation scepticism. Global exit volume fell again in Q1 2026.

The consequence. LPs cannot fund new commitments because their existing ones have not paid out. Fundraising slows. DPI has replaced IRR as the number LPs care about, because you cannot spend an unrealised mark.

Use it: this is the context for everything else on this list. If someone asks what is happening in the industry, start here.

02 · the response

Secondaries and continuation vehicles as the release valve

If you cannot sell a company, you engineer liquidity another way. The secondaries market hit a second consecutive record, with $226bn of volume, up more than 34% year on year. Continuation vehicles — where a manager sells an asset from an old fund into a new one it also runs, so investors who want cash get it and those who want to stay can — have become the primary liquidity tool, now including second-generation vehicles on assets already moved once.

The critique, which is the interesting half. There is an obvious conflict of interest when a manager is both buyer and seller and sets the price. And the exit backlog is far too large to clear through secondaries alone; restoring distributions properly needs real M&A and a functioning IPO market.

Use it: a strong answer to "tell me something you find interesting about private markets." It shows you can hold both the innovation and the objection.

03 · the stress test

Private credit meets its first real test

Private credit has passed $2 trillion and has grown up entirely during a benign period. That period has ended.

  • The First Brands and Tricolor collapses in late 2025 rattled the market. Both were fraud-driven, direct private credit exposure was modest, and the losses landed largely on banks — UBS disclosed over $500m of exposure to First Brands and Jefferies flagged $715m of questionable receivables after allegations that the same assets had been pledged more than once.
  • Fitch reported a 6.0% default rate in April 2026. Deutsche Bank disclosed $30bn of private credit exposure in March 2026 and warned about indirect risk through interconnected portfolios.
  • By early 2026, some semi-liquid funds faced redemption requests and moved to limit withdrawals.

The honest read: these were idiosyncratic frauds rather than proof of systemic rot, but they exposed how little visibility anyone has into valuations, leverage and the bank-to-fund linkages.

Use it: the best available answer to "what worries you about this industry?" Balanced, specific, and it shows you read beyond the marketing.

04 · your theme

Retailisation: private markets meet the wealth channel

Institutions are tapped out, so managers are going to individuals. Assets in US private equity interval and tender-offer funds passed $99bn by Q2 2026. In the UK, Long-Term Asset Funds reached £7.3bn and became eligible to be held inside stocks and shares ISAs from April 2026, which is expected to widen demand substantially. The FCA has named private markets a supervisory priority for the next 18 to 24 months.

The tension. These are illiquid assets inside structures that promise periodic liquidity. That works until everyone wants out at once, which is precisely what some funds began seeing in early 2026.

Use it: this is your theme. It is the exact point where your Pictet distribution and client-servicing experience becomes commercially relevant, because building and servicing these vehicles is a distribution problem as much as an investment one. If you get one substantive conversation on the day, have it about this.

05 · where the money is going

Private capital is financing the AI build-out

Individual AI data centre campuses now cost more than any single bank wants to lend, and private capital has filled the gap. Private credit has already deployed an estimated $200–250bn into data centres, with Blue Owl, Blackstone, Apollo, Brookfield and KKR dominant. Meta sold an 80% stake in its Hyperion project to Blue Owl in a joint venture that then issued $27.3bn of debt, the largest single US investment-grade bond ever, at roughly 90% leverage. Morgan Stanley has suggested private credit could supply a further $800bn over two years.

The question being asked. Is this infrastructure, with contracted long-term cash flows, or concentrated corporate credit risk dressed as infrastructure? The tenant quality is excellent; the concentration in a handful of hyperscaler counterparties, and the leverage, are not obviously conservative.

Use it: the most current thing on this list, and it sits at the intersection of infrastructure, private credit and real estate, so it works whoever you are talking to.

06 · the business model

The managers themselves are changing shape

Fee pressure on core buyout, consolidation into a small number of multi-asset platforms, insurance and annuity money becoming a permanent capital base, and GP stakes funds buying pieces of the managers themselves. The strategic question for every large firm is whether to be a specialist or a supermarket, and the answer increasingly determines what the graduate job looks like.

Use it: good for "why this firm rather than that one", where the honest answer is usually about what kind of business the firm is trying to become.

10Glossary and sources

Glossary

Alternatives
Anything that is not public equities, bonds or cash. Defined by exclusion.
GP / General Partner
The investment firm that raises and manages the fund.
LP / Limited Partner
The investor in the fund: pensions, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, endowments, family offices, wealth clients.
Commitment
The amount an LP promises to a fund, drawn down over time rather than paid upfront.
Capital call / drawdown
The GP demanding part of that commitment, usually at ten days' notice.
Dry powder
Money committed to funds but not yet invested.
Carried interest
The GP's share of profits, typically 20% above a hurdle.
Hurdle / preferred return
The return LPs receive before the GP earns carry. Usually 8% a year.
LBO
Leveraged buyout. Acquiring a company using significant borrowed money secured against that company.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. The rough proxy for operating cash generation that almost all deal pricing is quoted against.
Entry / exit multiple
Enterprise value divided by EBITDA, at purchase and at sale.
Sources and uses
The table showing where the money for a deal comes from and what it pays for. The first thing you build in an LBO model.
Unitranche
A single blended loan replacing separate senior and junior tranches. A staple of private credit.
PIK
Payment in kind. Interest added to the loan balance instead of paid in cash. Watched closely as a stress signal.
Covenant
A condition in a loan agreement that, if breached, hands the lender control.
IRR
Annualised return, sensitive to timing.
MOIC
Multiple on invested capital. Ignores time.
TVPI
Cash returned plus current estimated value, over cash invested.
DPI
Cash actually returned over cash invested. The metric of the moment.
J-curve
Early negative returns caused by fees preceding value creation.
Continuation vehicle
A new fund a GP raises to buy an asset out of its own older fund, giving existing investors the choice of cash or continued exposure.
Secondaries
Buying and selling existing stakes in private funds.
Evergreen / semi-liquid
Open-ended private markets funds offering periodic redemptions, aimed at wealth and retail investors.
LTAF
Long-Term Asset Fund. The UK's FCA-authorised open-ended structure for illiquid assets, ISA-eligible since April 2026.
DDQ
Due diligence questionnaire. The document LPs send GPs before investing. Investor relations lives on these.
Pod shop
A multi-strategy hedge fund of many small, independently risk-limited teams.
AIP
SEO London's Alternative Investments Programme.
