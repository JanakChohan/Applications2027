The "why this, not that?" test

Every reader has a silent question: "Could this person have written the same answer for a different industry?" If your reason for Investment Management works just as well for investment banking, it isn't a reason for Investment Management. It's a reason for "finance".

The distinctiveness ladder

The five ingredients of a compelling reason

1 · Distinctive. A feature that is true of this industry, or truer here than anywhere else (Sections 1–3 list these).
2 · Personal. It connects to something you value or experienced (Finding Your Why workbook).
3 · Evidenced. Something you did shows the interest is real, not theoretical.
4 · Informed. It uses the industry's real mechanics correctly (how it makes money, who the client is).
5 · Forward-looking. It points to what you'll contribute and become, not just what you like.
The quick check: replace the industry name in your sentence with its nearest neighbour. If the sentence still works, it isn't distinctive. Rewrite it.
Why this matters more for 10,000 Black Interns: you're matched to a firm at random within the workstream. That firm's reader knows you also chose two other workstreams, and may wonder whether you really want their industry. A clearly distinctive reason in each answer removes that doubt.

1B · Seven things only (or mostly) true of IM

These are your raw material for a distinctive answer. Pick the one or two that genuinely resonate, and back them with your own experience.

1 · You're the buy-side: the decision-maker

Banks advise and execute; asset managers decide where trillions go and live with the result. The client side represents that decision-making to the people whose money it is. Distinctive vs: IB, consulting, S&T.

2 · Relationships measured in years, not deals

Revenue is a recurring fee for every year a client stays, so the business is built on retention and trust, not one-off wins. A mandate can last a decade or more. Distinctive vs: IB, trading, consulting.

3 · The end client is ordinary savers

Behind every pension scheme are thousands of teachers, nurses and shop workers. IM's fiduciary duty links daily work to whether real people retire comfortably. Distinctive vs: IB, PE, trading (where clients are institutions or companies).

4 · Results are visible and measured constantly

Funds are priced daily and compared with benchmarks and peers. There's nowhere to hide, which demands honesty and humility when explaining performance. Distinctive vs: consulting and advisory, where outcomes are harder to measure.

5 · Breadth: the whole economy is your subject

A global portfolio touches every industry, country and economic trend. Few careers make you think about technology, energy, healthcare and interest rates in the same week. Distinctive vs: sector-specific industries.

6 · Influence through ownership (stewardship)

As shareholders, asset managers vote and engage with company boards on strategy, pay and climate. That is long-term influence over how companies behave. Distinctive vs: banking, consulting, insurance.

7 · Compounding: patience is the strategy

The industry rewards long-term thinking, both in investments (compounding returns) and in relationships (compounding trust). In a short-term world that's genuinely unusual. Distinctive vs: trading, deal-driven roles.

Bonus · Translating complexity into confidence

Client-facing IM roles exist to turn complex processes (attribution, risk, private markets) into clear explanations for trustees and advisers, a skill increasingly central under Consumer Duty. Distinctive vs: back-office or purely technical finance roles.

The strongest IM reasons combine #2 and #3: a business built on long-term trust with clients whose money ultimately belongs to ordinary people. That combination doesn't exist in IB, trading or consulting, and it's the natural justification for wanting the client-facing side.

Ready answers to "why not…?"

…investment banking?
"Banking is transactional: each deal ends. I'm drawn to IM because relationships and results build over years, and the firm is accountable for outcomes that affect ordinary savers."
…sales & trading?
"Trading is short-term and serves the buy-side. I want to be on the buy-side, where decisions are made with a long horizon and explained directly to the end client."
…wealth management?
"Wealth is about one individual's whole financial life. I'm more interested in how investment strategies are built and distributed at scale, to pensions and to the wealth managers themselves."
…the investment team (PM / analyst)?
"I'm fascinated by investing, but what I enjoyed most was the client side: understanding what a client needs and explaining the process so they trust it. I want to understand portfolios deeply in order to do that well."
…private equity?
"PE is concentrated in a few companies. I like IM's breadth across asset classes, and that its clients reach millions of savers."
…consulting?
"Consultants recommend; asset managers decide and are measured every day. I want that accountability."
What's needed to make the IM reason believable: (1) your AM experience described as a specific task and lesson; (2) one correct mechanic (fees on AUM, retention, institutional vs wholesale); (3) one live trend (Guide B13); (4) a signal of commitment, e.g. following markets in the FT or Citywire, an investment society, or starting the IMC.

Six things only (or mostly) true of Sales

1 · The clearest line to impact

Few roles can point to a number and say "I brought that revenue in". Your contribution is visible.

2 · Instant, honest feedback

Every call, meeting and proposal gives a yes, no or not yet. You learn faster because the market tells you immediately.

3 · Meritocratic

Results are measured and rewarded directly (commission, OTE, promotion), largely independent of background or connections.

4 · You learn the whole business

To sell, you must understand the product, the customer's industry, pricing and competitors. That's why so many leaders started in sales.

5 · Human at its core

Even as AI automates outreach, the decisive moments (trust, negotiation, reading a room) stay deeply human.

6 · Transferable everywhere

Every organisation needs revenue, so sales skills move across industries, including back into finance.

Your most distinctive Sales reason is #1 plus #4: a role where your impact is measurable, and where you have to understand a client's business deeply in order to help them. It also links naturally to what you saw in asset management distribution, without making the answer about finance.

Ready answers to "why not…?"

…marketing?
"Marketing creates interest across many people. I'm drawn to the one-to-one part: understanding a specific client and earning their commitment."
…consulting?
"I like diagnosing problems, but I want to be accountable for the outcome too, both winning the client's yes and being measured on it."
…customer service?
"I enjoy helping customers, but I want to proactively create value: finding new clients and growing relationships, with a clear target."
…finance, given your AM background?
"Asset management showed me that winning and keeping clients is a skill in itself. I want to build that skill rigorously, wherever it's practised best."
What's needed to make the Sales reason believable: (1) a real example of persuading, selling or fundraising, with a number if possible; (2) evidence of resilience (rejection handled); (3) one correct mechanic (pipeline, conversion, retention, discovery); (4) the AM sales cycle described in sales language; (5) curiosity about customers, not just about selling.
Watch out: the Sales workstream reader could be a software firm, a consumer brand or a media company. If your answer only works for financial services, rewrite the industry-specific parts in general sales language.

3A · PR vs its neighbours, and what makes it unique

FieldWhat it doesAudienceHow PR is different
Public relationsBuilds and protects reputation through earned media and stakeholder communicationAll stakeholders—
MarketingDrives demand for productsCustomersMarketing sells products to customers. PR manages trust with everyone: investors, staff, regulators, media, communities.
AdvertisingBuys space to deliver controlled messagesCustomersAdvertising is paid and controlled. PR must earn coverage by persuading independent gatekeepers, which makes it more credible.

Six things only (or mostly) true of PR

1 · It handles the bad news

Marketing goes quiet in a crisis. PR is the function that steps forward: results misses, job cuts, failures, scandals.

2 · Earned, not bought

Success depends on persuading independent people (journalists, creators) that something is worth their audience's attention.

3 · Every stakeholder

One week: customers, employees, investors, government, communities. PR thinks about how one message lands with all of them.

4 · Close to leadership

Senior PR people advise CEOs at the most sensitive moments. Reputation is a board-level concern.

5 · Live to the news cycle

The work reacts to the world in real time, so news sense and speed are core skills.

6 · Writing as the core craft

Statements, releases, messages, speeches. Precise words under time pressure are the product.

Your most distinctive PR reason is #1 plus #2: the discipline that earns trust it can't buy, and protects it when things go wrong. Linked to your observation that in finance "trust is the product", it's a reason no marketing-focused candidate can match.
