Start here

The six things that matter most

  1. Your 250-word answer is your cover letter. The employer FAQ says candidates apply to up to three workstreams, "each with a CV and a cover letter of 250 words maximum". Each workstream answer is a cover letter, so write it like one.
  2. Nobody at 10K ranks you. After an eligibility check, an algorithm matches you to employers at random within your chosen workstream and location. The employer then does all of the selecting.
  3. One allocation goes to one firm. Your CV and that workstream's answer go to a single organisation, which sees you alongside roughly 25–50 other candidates for the same role. You might get several allocations, one, or none.
  4. You can't choose the firm, so you can't name the firm. Write for the whole workstream: show that you understand how any firm in that industry works and makes money. That is the specificity that wins.
  5. Each answer is read by a different kind of reader. A PR agency reading your PR answer does not care that Investment Management was your first choice. Every answer has to stand on its own.
  6. Your CV is locked at submission. Finalise it before 1 November. You can only send an updated version to a firm that contacts you afterwards.

The programme from zero

What it is

10,000 Black Interns is the flagship programme of the 10,000 Interns Foundation, a UK registered charity (no. 1199061). It does not employ you and it is not a recruitment agency. It is a matchmaker plus training provider. Employers pledge paid internships, Black students and graduates apply once, and the Foundation's system routes applications to those employers. It also runs training, mentoring and an alumni network around that process.

What employers must commit to

  • Paid at least the Real Living Wage for the location, for the whole internship
  • At least 6 weeks, usually in summer (internships run May–September 2027)
  • UK-based (remote or hybrid allowed)
  • A dedicated line manager and a mentor from inside the organisation

What you get beyond the internship

  • Pre-application CV and cover-letter workshops and sector deep dives
  • Interview training, then pre-internship training (April–June 2027)
  • 1:1 mentoring and networking
  • The 10,000 Alumni Programme (events, jobs board, mentoring)
  • Wellbeing support (Black Minds Matter, Turn2Us)

The rules, straight from the FAQs

  • Matching uses only workstream + location. Employers are told candidates are "not filtered by other factors".
  • Each allocation = one organisation. You may get several, one, or none.
  • An employer receives 25–50 candidates per role on average, and can request more.
  • If your first-choice workstream is full, you may be placed in an adjacent workstream.
  • You can't swap allocations or ask for extra ones. A rejection is not reallocated.
  • New allocations can arrive up to June as new roles are added.
  • Firms see only their own slate. Everyone else should apply direct.
  • Emails come from Pinpoint or the firm itself. They are genuine, so check your spam folder.

What this means for you

  • All three answers count equally. Your third choice could be the one that gets allocated. Don't write it lazily.
  • You compete inside a slate of ~25–50, not against thousands. Being clearly the best-informed person in that pile is very achievable.
  • Some firms decide on paper alone. The FAQ warns that some reject from the CV and cover letter without an interview, so the writing has to carry you.
  • Location is a lever. More roles in a location means more chances of an allocation. Pick somewhere you can genuinely get to.
  • Speed matters. First contact is 24 Nov–4 Dec. Missing a firm's deadline can lose the allocation.

Part 3

Who actually reads your 250 words

Picture the reader. Late November: a hiring manager or early-careers recruiter at one firm opens Pinpoint and sees a slate of about 40 people. For each candidate they see a CV and a 250-word answer. They might spend one to two minutes per person. They are asking four questions:

1. Motivation
Is this a real choice or a box ticked?
2. Understanding
Do they know what we actually do?
3. Evidence
Have they done anything that proves it?
4. Fit
Could I put them in front of a colleague or client in week one?

The "random firm" problem, solved

You can't tailor to one company because you don't know which one you'll get. "Investment Management" might mean a £1 trillion global manager, a 30-person boutique, a wealth manager or an insurer's in-house investment arm. The answer is to tailor to the workstream's common core, meaning the things that are true at every firm in that pool:

WorkstreamCommon core every host sharesPhrases that show understanding
Investment ManagementThey manage other people's money for a fee based on assets. Growth comes from performance plus winning and keeping clients."assets under management", "net flows", "client retention", "explaining the investment process"
SalesRevenue depends on finding, converting and keeping customers. Results are measured in pipeline, conversion and targets."understanding the client's problem", "pipeline", "account management", "repeat business"
Public RelationsThey protect and build reputation through earned media and messaging. Agencies sell expertise by the hour or on retainer."key messages", "media relations", "coverage", "issues and crisis", "stakeholders"

The golden rule

Specific about the industry, specific about you, neutral about the firm. Never write a firm's name, "your firm's values" or "your graduate scheme". You don't know who will read it, and a generic flattering line wastes words.

Should you tailor Investment Management to the client-facing side?

Yes, as your spine, but leave the door open. The allocation is random within the workstream, so the role you're matched to could be in an investment team, operations or distribution. If your answer says "I only want sales" you may put off an investment-team reader. If it says "I want to work on the client-facing side, and to do that well I want to understand how portfolios are built", every reader in the pool can say yes. Your previous asset-management experience makes that line believable.

A note on coherence: your three choices don't need to be connected. Each is a separate lottery ticket read by a different reader. But there is a natural thread you can use in your head: IM distribution is a form of B2B sales, and asset managers rely heavily on financial PR. Use that thread for your own understanding. Don't make the Sales or PR answers about finance only, because that reader might be a software company or a consumer PR agency.
Part 4 · First choice

Investment Management

An asset manager pools money from savers (through pensions, ISAs and insurance policies) and invests it in shares, bonds, property and private assets on their behalf, for a fee. The UK industry manages around £9 trillion, second only to the US.

Pre-knowledge: where it sits in the money system

AUM
Assets under management: the total money a firm manages. The single most important number, because fees are a percentage of it.
bps (basis points)
1 bp = 0.01%. A fee of 50 bps = 0.50% a year. Fees are always discussed in bps.
Active vs passive
Active: managers pick investments to beat a benchmark. Passive: track an index cheaply (e.g. an ETF tracking the FTSE 100).
Benchmark / alpha
The index a fund is measured against. Alpha is return above that benchmark, which is what active managers sell.
Net flows
New client money in minus money withdrawn. Positive flows mean the business is winning.
Fund vs mandate
Fund: a pooled product many investors buy into (OEIC, unit trust, ETF, investment trust). Mandate: a bespoke portfolio run for one large client.
DB vs DC pensions
Defined benefit: guaranteed pension, so the scheme carries the risk. Defined contribution: your pot depends on investment returns. DC is where the growth is.
Private markets
Investments not on a stock exchange: private equity, private credit, infrastructure, real estate. Higher fees and a fast-growing area.
RFP
Request for proposal: a formal questionnaire a client or consultant sends when choosing a manager.

Types of firm you could be allocated to

Global "traditional" managers
Large active and passive ranges: e.g. LGIM, Schroders, M&G, abrdn, BlackRock, Fidelity.
Boutiques
Small, specialist, often owned by their partners. You'll see a bit of everything.
Alternatives
Hedge funds (e.g. Capstone), private equity (Permira, Livingbridge), private credit (Ares).
Wealth managers
Manage money for individuals and families: St James's Place, Canaccord, Rathbones.
Insurers / pension investors
In-house teams at insurers and big pension schemes, i.e. asset owners.
Consultants / platforms
Advise on or distribute managers. Close cousins that sometimes sit in this workstream.

Part 4 · Investment Management

Close-up: the client-facing side

In asset management, the client side is usually called distribution. It splits by who the client is:

Institutional

Clients: DB and DC pension schemes, local government pension pools (LGPS), insurers, sovereign wealth funds, charities, university endowments.

How they buy: slowly and formally. Usually an investment consultant shortlists managers, then RFPs, then a pitch to the trustees. Sales cycles take months, and mandates are worth hundreds of millions.

Wholesale / intermediary

Clients: wealth managers, discretionary fund managers (DFMs), independent financial advisers (IFAs), private banks, and platforms such as Hargreaves Lansdown.

How they buy: fund selectors research funds and add them to "buy lists". More meetings, smaller tickets, and a lot of regional travel.

Retail / direct

Clients: individual investors buying funds or ISAs directly.

How they buy: through marketing, digital channels and brand. They are driven by FCA Consumer Duty, which requires firms to show good outcomes and fair value.

What's happening in the industry right now (use one, in a single sentence)

Fee compression and passive growth. Cheap index funds keep taking share, so active managers must justify their fees. Clear explanation of value becomes the job of distribution.
Private markets go mainstream. Managers are launching private-market products for pensions and wealthy individuals (e.g. LTAFs). There is a lot of client education involved.
UK pension reform. DC schemes and LGPS pools are consolidating into fewer, larger "megafunds", and the government is pushing more investment into UK assets. Fewer, bigger, more demanding clients.
Consolidation and cost pressure. Mergers, job cuts and AI-driven automation. Firms want people who add value quickly.
Sustainability and stewardship. Clients ask how managers vote and engage with companies. FCA sustainability labels (SDR) shape how funds are marketed.
Part 5 · Second choice

Sales

Sales connects a product or service with the customer who needs it, and turns that need into revenue. The Foundation notes that about 1 in 6 UK employees work in sales-related roles. The Sales workstream can include technology and software, consumer goods, business services, media, telecoms and financial services.

Pre-knowledge

B2B vs B2C
Business-to-business (selling to companies: longer cycles, several decision-makers) vs business-to-consumer (selling to individuals: faster, higher volume).
Pipeline
All the potential deals in progress, at each stage, with an estimated value.
Conversion rate
The share of prospects that move to the next stage, or that eventually buy.
Quota / target
The revenue a salesperson is expected to bring in. Pay is often base salary plus commission ("OTE", on-target earnings).
ARR / recurring revenue
Revenue that repeats (subscriptions, contracts). It is highly valued, because keeping customers is cheaper than finding new ones.
Churn
Customers lost over a period. Low churn means healthy account management.
CAC and LTV
Cost to acquire a customer vs their lifetime value. LTV should be well above CAC. Every deal is logged in a CRM (e.g. Salesforce).

The four parts of a sales organisation

1. Lead generation

  • SDR / BDR roles
  • Research prospects
  • Cold outreach, calls, emails
  • Book first meetings
Goal: fill the top of the pipeline

2. New business

  • Account executives
  • Discovery meetings
  • Proposals and demos
  • Negotiation and closing
Goal: convert prospects into paying customers

3. Account management

  • Key account managers
  • Customer success
  • Renewals and upselling
  • Relationship building
Goal: keep and grow existing customers

4. Sales ops & strategy

  • Pipeline data and CRM
  • Forecasting
  • Pricing and territories

Your bridge into Sales

Asset-management distribution is consultative B2B sales: long cycles, several decision-makers (trustees, consultants), formal proposals (RFPs), and revenue that depends on retention. If you saw any of that in your previous role, you have watched high-value sales up close. In the Sales answer, describe it in sales language (prospect, pipeline, relationship, follow-up, objection) so a software or consumer-goods reader recognises it. Then add one non-finance example of persuading or serving people (a part-time job, a society, fundraising) to show range. For interviews, know consultative selling and SPIN questioning. If asked to "sell me this pen", ask about the buyer before you pitch.

Part 6 · Public Relations

How PR makes money, and your bridge

Agency side (most PR internships)

An agency sells people's time and judgement to clients:

  • Retainers: a monthly fee for ongoing work (e.g. £10k–£50k+ a month for a large corporate client)
  • Projects: one-off fees for a launch, campaign or deal
  • Crisis work: billed at high hourly rates when something goes wrong

Profit = fees − staff costs, so agencies track billable hours and win new clients through pitches, which is sales again. An intern who saves a senior consultant time is directly valuable.

In-house side

A communications team inside a company, such as a bank, retailer, charity or asset manager. It is a cost centre: it doesn't bill anyone, so its value is shown through:

  • quality and quantity of coverage, and share of voice
  • reputation and trust scores
  • crises avoided or contained
  • support for sales, recruitment and investor confidence

Your bridge into PR: financial communications

Asset managers spend heavily on communications. Fund managers are quoted in the FT, Bloomberg, Citywire, Investment Week, Money Marketing and Professional Pensions. Firms publish market outlooks, and a single bad story can trigger client withdrawals. The 2019 suspension of the Woodford Equity Income fund is the classic example of reputation turning into lost assets almost overnight. Financial and corporate PR firms in the UK include Brunswick, FGS Global, Teneo, Headland, Lansons, Instinctif, Citigate Dewe Rogerson and Montfort (which supports 10K itself). Consumer and broad agencies include Edelman, Weber Shandwick, MHP and Freuds.

How to use this: say you've seen first-hand that in finance trust is the product, and that clear communication protects it. Then show you can write, research and work to deadlines. Keep it broad enough that a consumer agency reader still nods along. Mention the financial angle once, not throughout.

What PR readers look for that other workstreams don't: writing quality (your 250 words are the writing sample, so any typo is fatal), news sense (follow the news and be able to name a recent campaign or crisis you found interesting and why), organisation under pressure, and confidence on the phone.

Writing the answers

The form asks for 100–250 words and says to "maximise the word count". Aim for 230–250. Use this five-part structure for every answer:

Hook

PartWhat it doesTest
HookOne sharp sentence on why this industry, based on an insight, not a feelingCould another applicant have written it? If yes, rewrite.

The specificity ladder

LevelExample line (Investment Management)
Weak"I am passionate about finance and want to develop my skills in a fast-paced environment."
OK"My internship in asset management showed me how investment teams work and sparked my interest in the industry."
Strong"At [firm], helping prepare quarterly reports for pension clients showed me that assets are kept through trust as much as performance: a £500m mandate at 40 basis points is £2m a year that depends on every meeting going well."

Do

  • One main story per answer, told properly
  • Use different stories across the three answers where you can
  • Use industry vocabulary correctly, a few terms, not a glossary
  • British spelling, short sentences, active voice
  • Read it aloud, then get one other person to proofread it
  • Write it in a doc first and count the words there

Don't

  • Name a firm, or write "your company"
  • Say "this is my second / third choice"
  • Copy and paste a paragraph across answers
  • List adjectives ("hard-working, passionate, driven")
  • Over-claim (e.g. "I managed a portfolio" when you supported one)
  • Paste in AI text unchanged. Readers spot it, and it sounds like everyone else.

Part 8

Strategy beyond the form

1. Pick your location deliberately

Matching is by workstream and location. London has the most host roles in all three of your workstreams, especially IM and PR. If you can do London, choose it. If you can't, choose the nearest major hub where you could genuinely live for 6+ weeks (Edinburgh, Manchester, Leeds, Birmingham and Bristol all have asset managers).

2. Lock the CV before 1 November

It can't be edited later. Make your asset-management bullets results-focused ("prepared X for Y clients", "reduced Z"), and put it first. It is your biggest differentiator on a slate of 40.

3. Treat the cover letter field seriously

The FAQ says your CV and cover letter go to each allocated organisation. Keep the general cover letter firm-neutral, one page, and consistent with your three answers.

4. Respond within 24 hours, 24 Nov – 4 Dec

That is when firms make first contact. Set a Gmail filter for "Pinpoint" and "10,000", check spam daily, and diarise each firm's deadline. Missing one can lose the allocation, and 10K cannot reopen it.

5. Prepare for the firm's own process

Expect any of: online tests or psychometrics, a HireVue video, a written exercise, interviews. For IM, be ready for: "Pitch me a fund or stock", "How does an asset manager make money?", "What's happening in markets?", "Why the client side?"

6. Don't rely on 10K alone

Allocations aren't guaranteed and are random. Apply directly to the asset managers you most want (the 10K employer FAQ itself tells unallocated candidates to do this), and use the 10K training sessions. They count towards applications outside the programme too.
