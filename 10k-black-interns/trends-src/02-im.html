B0 · The big picture in five numbers

$147tnglobal AUM in 2025, up 11% (BCG 2026)

~30%industry operating margin, flat for ~15 years; costs grew 5.4% a year vs revenue 5.1% (BCG)

Why "80% from markets" is the most important number in the industry: an asset manager's revenue is a percentage of the assets it manages (Guide B10). If markets rise 10%, revenue rises about 10% without winning a single new client. So a record year can hide a weak business. If markets fall, revenue falls with them, and costs don't. BCG co-author Johannes Burkhardt's summary: "Distribution now defines who wins." His colleague Renaud Fages: "Performance alone is no longer enough."

B1 · Private markets for everyone: evergreen and semi-liquid funds

From zero: private equity, private credit and infrastructure were traditionally sold only to institutions through closed-ended "drawdown" funds. The investor commits money, the manager "calls" it over 3–5 years, it's locked up for 10+ years, and cash comes back only when assets are sold. "Democratisation" means repackaging these assets for wealthy individuals, retail investors and pension savers through "evergreen" (no end date) or "semi-liquid" funds. Why? Private markets earn managers far higher fees: PwC estimates about 4× more profit per $bn than traditional funds.

How an evergreen fund works, technically

SubscribeMonthly, at NAV. Money is invested immediately, not "called".
›
ValueNAV set by the manager's own valuations of unlisted assets, often checked by third parties
›
Request exitUsually quarterly
›
The gatePays out up to ~5% of NAV a quarter. If requests exceed that, everyone gets a pro-rata share.
›
Liquidity toolsA 10–20% liquid "sleeve", credit lines, loan repayments, secondaries sales

The crucial detail: in US non-traded BDCs (business development companies, the main retail private credit vehicle), the 5% is a convention, not a law. Managers can restrict redemptions indefinitely. US interval funds must offer periodic repurchases. Unfilled requests generally aren't carried forward and must be resubmitted, which inflates headline "request" numbers.

Bull caseWealth is a huge, under-penetrated pool of "sticky" money. Evergreen funds solve the J-curve and cash-drag problems of drawdown funds. Gates are "a feature, not a bug" (Goldman's private credit chief, 2026). BCG sizes a $3tn private-markets opportunity in wealth.
Bear caseA structural liquidity mismatch: quarterly exits promised on assets that don't trade. Valuations are set by the manager, and fees are high. Morningstar: these funds "suit only investors who can go years without needing their money back."
1–3 years: the final DOL rule, and the first private-markets sleeves inside US target-date funds. More LTAFs inside UK DC defaults. The redemption-request ratio becomes the key health metric. 5+ years: evergreen about $5tn; secondaries the standard liquidity backstop; standardised liquidity and valuation disclosure.
Learn the liquidity terms cold: dealing frequency, notice period, gate %, liquidity sleeve, and what happens to unfilled requests. A pension consultant's first questions are: "How is NAV set, who values it, how often, and what's your redemption history?"

B2 · Private credit's stress test: the 2026 gating wave

From zero: private credit means funds, rather than banks, lending directly to mid-sized companies, often ones owned by private equity. Loans are usually floating-rate. Retail investors access it through vehicles that value loans at "fair value" and offer limited quarterly exits (B1). In 2026 the theory met reality: many investors asked for their money back at once, and the gates came down.
Fund (2026)Requests (% of NAV)Paid
BlackRock HPS Corporate Lending ($26bn), March9.3%Capped at 5% (~$620m of ~$1.2bn)
Apollo Debt Solutions~11%, later ~15%Capped at 5%, several quarters running
Blackstone BCRED, Q2~10%Capped at 5%
Cliffwater lending fund~17%Capped at 5%
Blue Owl OBDC IIRedemptions stopped Sept 2025. A merger with a listed fund at ~20% below NAV was announced, then cancelled. Redemptions halted permanently on 18 Feb 2026 and the fund became a wind-down. An investor lawsuit followed.

Bloomberg tallied about $13bn of requests across more than a dozen funds in one quarter, with only about two-thirds paid out.

Why it happened: the "SaaSpocalypse"

The Bank for International Settlements (March 2026) found loans to software (SaaS) companies exceeded $500bn, about 19% of direct lending. Investors feared AI would disrupt those software businesses, and software stocks fell almost 30% between October 2025 and February 2026. Yet BDCs held software loans at an average of about 97.8% of face value (Octus), which raised the question of whether the valuations ("marks") were stale. On the other side, Blue Owl sold $1.4bn of loans at 99.7% of par, evidence the marks were broadly fair.

Technical concept: the listed "second opinion". Some BDCs are listed on an exchange, so the market prices them every day. When listed BDCs trade at a discount to their reported NAV, the market is saying it thinks the loans are worth less than the manager's valuation. Non-traded funds don't get that daily check, which is exactly the valuation-trust problem.
Bull caseThe gates worked as designed: no fire sales. Loans sold near par support the valuations. Floating-rate yields remain attractive, and some funds (e.g. KKR's K-FITS, Blackstone's BREIT) had inflows exceeding outflows.
Bear caseRetail investors didn't understand that 5% is a ceiling, not a promise. Marks near par on AI-threatened software loans look stale. Unfilled requests compound quarter after quarter, and a "semi-liquid" fund became a multi-year wind-down.
1–3 years: more transparency on redemption queues; product redesign (longer notice periods, bigger liquid sleeves, interval funds over BDCs); likely regulatory scrutiny of retail suitability. 5+ years: private credit keeps growing (Preqin forecasts $4.5tn by 2030), but retail wrappers are priced and regulated more like listed closed-end funds.
Quarterly redemption requests vs the 5% cap · listed BDC discounts to NAV · software loan "non-accruals" (loans that stopped paying) · share of income paid in kind (PIK) rather than cash · secondary prices for credit portfolios
Was this a product failure or a communication failure? The structure behaved exactly as the documents said. Investors were surprised anyway. That gap is precisely the client-facing job.
"What's your redemption-request history?" and "What's your software exposure?" are now standard due-diligence questions. Be ready to answer "what happened at Blue Owl, and could it happen here?" with calm facts about structure, not marketing.

B3 · Public meets private: the convergence deals

From zero: "traditional" managers (shares and bonds) have distribution, relationships with advisers, wealth managers and pension schemes, but face falling fees. "Alternative" managers (private markets) have high-fee products but historically lacked access to individual investors. Convergence means combining the two, by buying each other or partnering, to sell blended "public + private" products such as target-date funds or model portfolios with a private-markets sleeve.

Asset management deal value rose 116% in 2025 versus 2024, and deal count rose 14% (Ropes & Gray). Before the Nuveen bid, Schroders had already set a £150m annual cost-savings target and cut about 7% of roles in one quarter of 2025, a sign of the squeeze on mid-sized active managers.

Bear caseCulture clashes and key-person risk. Buyers pay private-markets prices for retail flows that 2026 proved are cyclical. Blended products can combine the worst of both: liquidity promises sitting on illiquid assets.
1–3 years: more mid-sized traditional managers sell, merge or partner; model portfolios and target-date funds with 5–15% private sleeves become standard. 5+ years: a barbell of giant platforms at one end and specialist boutiques at the other, with the "squeezed middle" shrinking.
Deal announcements (who's next after Schroders?) · private markets as a share of listed managers' revenue · launches of blended public-private products · how Nuveen integrates Schroders
A UK icon founded in 1804 was bought by a US group. Is that a sign of UK weakness, or of London's strength as a global hub (Schroders keeps its brand and London becomes the non-US HQ)? Insiders disagree, and both views are defensible.
Learn your firm's private-markets shelf and its partners. Clients increasingly want one conversation across public and private assets. Corporate deals also create client anxiety, which is when relationship managers matter most.

B4 · Fee squeeze, active outflows and consolidation

From zero: fees are an annual percentage of assets. Index (passive) funds charge a fraction of what active funds charge. As money moves to passive and to cheaper share classes, the industry earns less per £ managed. That's fee compression. Costs (technology, data, regulation) keep rising, so margins get squeezed, and smaller firms merge or get bought.
0.32%average US fund fee (asset-weighted), from 0.80% in 2006. Passive 0.10%, active 0.57% (Morningstar)
~25%of UK fund assets now in trackers, up from 18% five years earlier (IA)
−£18.9bnUK active equity fund outflows in 2025, while passive equity took in £12.2bn (Calastone)
£54bnoutflows from UK equity funds over a decade (Calastone)

In 2025, passive global equity funds outsold active for the first time since 2015 (Calastone), and £5.8bn went into money market funds as savers sought safety. February 2026 marked the ninth consecutive month of equity fund outflows. Meanwhile, fees on newly launched US mutual funds topped 1% for the first time since 2015. The new launches are specialist products, where pricing power still exists.

1–3 years: more UK takeovers, likely by US or European buyers or private equity; more fund mergers and closures; fee cuts on old share classes driven by "Assessment of Value" reviews. 5+ years: passive could plausibly reach a third or more of UK fund assets (an extrapolation of current trends, not a published forecast).
IA monthly tracker share · Calastone Fund Flow Index · valuations of listed UK managers (cheap ones are bid targets) · fee cuts in annual Assessment of Value reports
My take: "active vs passive" is the wrong frame. The real question is where active can still earn its fee: less-covered markets, private assets, and outcomes such as income or retirement solutions. A strong candidate can say where they think active adds value, and why.
"Why should I pay for active?" is the default client question. Know your funds' fees versus passive and active peers, and be able to explain what the client gets for the difference.

B5 · The ETF takeover: active ETFs and ETF share classes

From zero: an ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund listed on a stock exchange and traded during the day like a share. An active ETF uses the same wrapper, but a portfolio manager picks the investments rather than tracking an index. An ETF share class lets a traditional mutual fund add an ETF version of the same portfolio, so a manager can offer ETF access without launching a separate clone fund.

How an ETF stays close to its true value: creation and redemption

$23.1tnglobal ETF assets, end-July 2026, a record (ETFGI)
$2.59tnglobal active ETF assets; $590bn inflows in 7 months vs $323bn a year earlier
$3.97tnEuropean ETF assets, end-Aug 2026, up ~23% this year
39%iShares' share of the European ETF market; J.P. Morgan dominates European active ETFs

The US share-class breakthrough: the SEC granted Dimensional relief on 17 Nov 2025 to add ETF share classes to mutual funds, signalled approval for about 30 more firms in December 2025, and the remaining trading and listing approvals followed by April 2026. Law firm K&L Gates said share-class ETFs were "ready to go live". In Europe, 36 active ETF launches and zero closures in Q1 2026 show US managers piling in.

Bull caseThe active ETF is the "rescue wrapper" for active management: cheaper, transparent, and easy to use in platforms and model portfolios. Share classes let incumbents defend their assets.
Bear caseIn Europe the big US tax advantage of ETFs largely doesn't apply. Active ETFs cannibalise higher-fee funds. The market is concentrated (the top 3 hold ~30% of active ETF assets), and many launches won't reach scale.
1–3 years: European active ETF launches accelerate; US share-class conversions begin, with pressure for UK/European equivalents; model portfolios built increasingly from ETFs. 5+ years: the ETF becomes the default retail wrapper in Europe as platforms and app-based brokers grow.
Active share of European ETF flows · iShares vs J.P. Morgan market share · any FCA statement on ETF share classes (none found yet)
Expect wealth managers and model-portfolio builders to ask "is there an ETF version?" Understand primary vs secondary market liquidity and bid-offer spreads.

B6 · UK pensions: megafunds, the Mansion House Accord and LGPS pools

From zero: most UK workers save in a DC workplace pension, usually invested in the provider's "default fund". The government believes the market has too many small schemes, focused too much on low cost, and that the 86 council pension funds (the LGPS) are too small. It wants bigger schemes investing more in UK private assets (infrastructure, housing, growing companies). For asset managers, this changes who their clients are.
PolicyWhat it does
Pension Schemes Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 Apr 2026)Multi-employer DC schemes need £25bn in a single main default fund by 2030 (to 2035 if £10bn+ with a credible plan). Small pots are consolidated automatically. A reserve "mandation" power could require up to 10% in private markets (5% UK), usable no earlier than 1 Jan 2028.
Mansion House Accord (13 May 2025)17 providers (including Aviva, L&G, Nest, Royal London, Phoenix) pledged at least 10% of DC defaults in private markets by 2030, with at least 5% in the UK, subject to fiduciary duty.
LGPS "Fit for the Future"Eight pools cut to six. ACCESS and Brunel were rejected and their funds moved. Pools had to be FCA-authorised and take over asset management by 31 Mar 2026.
Value for Money framework (consultation Jan 2026)Public red / amber / light green / dark green ratings of DC schemes on cost, performance and service. First assessments targeted for 2028.
3.3%UK private-market allocation at end-2025 (£11.1bn) vs the 5% target (first Accord report, 21 Oct 2026)
£25bnL&G's Lifetime Advantage Funds hit the megafund size and passed 10% private markets (May 2026)
−15%occupational DC schemes fell to 790 while assets rose 22% to £249bn (TPR, May 2026)

Fidelity's FutureWise also passed £25bn (from £6.5bn in 2022). Of the UK private assets in the first Accord report, most is property (£8bn), with infrastructure £1.3bn, private credit £0.9bn and private equity only £0.5bn. Venture and growth capital, the government's real aim, is the smallest piece.

Bull caseScale lowers costs and lets schemes build in-house teams and access private markets, as in Australia and Canada. L&G and Fidelity reached £25bn quickly.
Bear caseMandation threatens fiduciary duty. The UK may lack enough good investable deals. A handful of giant buyers creates a "massive concentration of power" and brutal fee negotiations. LGPS transition costs exceed £100m by one estimate.
1–3 years: master trust mergers; Value for Money rules finalised before 2028; UK allocation creeping from 3.3% towards 5%; LGPS pools cutting external managers. A possible use of mandation after 1 Jan 2028 if progress stalls. 5+ years: perhaps 10–20 DC megafunds, with a standard 5%+ UK private-assets sleeve.
The annual Accord progress report · TPR scheme counts · LGPS pool manager-selection news · final VFM rules · any government move towards mandation
Should government be able to direct where your pension is invested, even partly? This is one of the most contested questions in UK finance. Have a reasoned view: e.g. "voluntary targets backed by better deal supply are better than mandation, because fiduciary duty must come first."
Institutional distribution is now a "few big doors" game. Each mandate won or lost is huge. Learn the six LGPS pools, the ~10 leading master trusts and the consultants (Mercer, Aon, LCP, Isio, Hymans Robertson).

B7 · Wealth, model portfolios and the UK retail-investing push

From zero: UK individuals increasingly invest through (a) financial advisers who outsource to model portfolio services (MPS), ready-made portfolios run centrally on platforms; (b) investment platforms such as Hargreaves Lansdown and AJ Bell; and (c) app-based brokers. The government wants more people to move savings from cash into investments, and 2025–27 brings a wave of reforms to make that happen.

25%of MPS assets held by just two firms, Quilter WealthSelect and Tatton
61%of global AUM growth 2020–25 came from individual investors (BCG)
18mpeople could be offered "targeted support" within a decade (FCA estimate)

If two model portfolio firms control a quarter of the market, who has the power: the fund manager or the gatekeeper? What should a mid-sized fund manager do: fight for shelf space, or build its own models?
"Gatekeepers" now means DFM/MPS research teams and platform shortlist teams, not just individual advisers. Understand the Consumer Duty value narrative: wholesale clients need evidence that your fund is fair value.

B8 · Tokenisation and the new fund plumbing

From zero: tokenisation means recording ownership of fund units (or bonds, or cash) on a blockchain-style shared ledger, as a digital "token". The promise: near-instant settlement, 24/7 transfers, lower admin costs, and "programmable" features, e.g. using a tokenised money market fund as collateral. Tokenised money market funds (MMFs) are the first real use case, because they act like interest-earning digital cash.
Traditional fund dealing: investor → platform → transfer agent → fund register, with each step reconciled separately and settlement taking days.

Tokenised / "Direct-to-Fund": the investor's holding is a token on a shared ledger, and the register is the ledger. Fewer intermediaries and faster settlement.
Why it matters: settlement speed, collateral efficiency (a tokenised MMF can be pledged instantly) and cheaper registers. Why it's hard: legal ownership, custody rules, interoperability between different blockchains, and liquidity split across platforms.

Notice the gap between forecasts ($715bn–$14tn by 2030) and reality (~$12bn today). How would you judge whether tokenisation is a revolution or a slow plumbing upgrade? Hint: watch whether money market tokens get accepted as collateral by major clearing houses.
Know whether your firm has tokenised share classes. Treasury and institutional cash clients may ask. Be ready to explain what T+1 means for dealing and settlement.

B9 · AI in asset management, specifically

From zero: "AI" is too vague to be useful, so be precise. Generative AI drafts, summarises and codes. Agentic AI plans and carries out multi-step tasks using tools, e.g. "generate a trading signal, code it, test it on historical data, write it up". In asset management, most of the money from AI is in operations, distribution and reporting, not in picking winning stocks.
Where AI is being appliedEvidence / example
Investment research (quant)Man Group's "AlphaGPT": an agentic system that generates, codes and backtests quant signals. "Several dozen" signals approved for live trading with human checkpoints (Bloomberg, Jul 2025). Man itself flags hallucination and "drift" risks.
OperationsBCG: 55–65% of operations capacity could be freed by agentic AI. Reconciliations, data quality, KYC and onboarding are prime targets.
Distribution & reportingRFP and DDQ drafting, client reporting, fund commentary. Secondary reports of BCG analysis suggest 35–50% of distribution capacity could be freed (unverified).
Whole firmBCG: 25–35% cost reduction within 3–5 years, serving 3–5× more clients per person. McKinsey: 25–40% savings for an illustrative $500bn manager, but only with end-to-end workflow redesign, not scattered experiments.
73%of firms use AI for operational efficiency; 68% in the investment process (Mercer, May 2026)
−9%Man Group headcount (excluding acquisitions) while building AI
26×some firms pay 26 times more than peers for comparable index data (Substantive Research)
80%of buy-side firms expect market-data spending to rise (Coalition Greenwich)

The hidden constraint is data cost. AI needs clean, licensed data, and the big index providers (MSCI, FTSE Russell, S&P) have pricing power. The second-order effect is that AI disrupting software companies became a credit risk in private credit (B2). AI shows up in asset management both as a tool and as an investment risk.

Bull caseA once-in-a-generation margin reset: 25–40% of costs. Personalisation at scale, and faster RFP and reporting turnaround, so smaller teams serve more clients.
Bear casePast tech spending didn't raise productivity (McKinsey). Savings get competed away into lower fees. Hallucinations in client-facing material breach Consumer Duty and marketing rules. Junior roles hollow out, damaging the talent pipeline.
1–3 years: agentic tools in RFPs, client reporting, fund commentary, onboarding and reconciliations; flat or falling headcount in operations, client service and junior research. 5+ years: operating models rebuilt around a smaller core; distribution roles shift to relationships plus judgement.
Cost/income ratios · headcount disclosures · AI mentioned alongside specific savings in results · FCA statements on AI in advice and marketing
My take: the juniors most exposed are those whose job is "assemble the pitch book, fill in the DDQ, write the factsheet commentary". The ones who thrive will use the tools, check their output and own the client relationship. Ask yourself which side of that line your skills sit on.
In interviews, avoid saying "AI will change everything". Say instead: "AI is cutting the cost of producing client materials, so the value moves to accuracy, judgement and the relationship itself."

B10 · Trust: valuations, ESG and stewardship

From zero: trust is the product in investment management (Guide B5). Right now trust is being tested in four places: Are private-asset prices real? Are fees fair? Are "sustainable" funds what they claim? And should asset managers take positions on climate at all, given the political backlash, especially in the US?
IssueWhat happened
Private-market valuationsThe FCA reviewed 36 firms (March 2025). It found conflicts of interest not fully identified, especially where fees depend on valuations (higher valuations mean higher fees), weak record-keeping and poor ad hoc revaluation processes. A follow-up review of private-markets conflicts is planned.
Fair valueConsumer Duty and annual Assessment of Value reports force firms to justify fees. The FCA is now simplifying the reporting ("Consumer Duty 2.0").
Sustainability labels (SDR)About 150 labelled funds by October 2025, short of the 200+ expected. All are active funds.
ESG flowsGlobal sustainable funds had $84bn net outflows in 2025, the first annual outflow since tracking began (Morningstar). Europe returned to inflows in Q1 2026, but the US saw its 14th straight quarter of outflows.
Net Zero Asset ManagersSuspended January 2025 after BlackRock left. Relaunched February 2026 with 250 signatories (down from ~330), dropping references to 2050 and 1.5°C. None of the nine largest managers are members.
US anti-ESG litigationTexas v. BlackRock: antitrust claims that big managers used climate commitments to restrict coal output. Vanguard settled (Feb 2026); BlackRock and State Street continue.
UK Stewardship Code 2026In force 1 Jan 2026. Principles cut from 12 to 6, with less reporting and a refocus on "value creation". About 300 signatories representing ~£50tn.
Bull caseThe UK and EU are keeping their sustainability regimes, and labels add credibility. Europe has returned to ESG inflows. Valuation scrutiny will make private markets more trustworthy, and that will help them grow.
Bear caseUS legal risk makes collective climate action an antitrust exposure. Labels are under-used, and ESG is becoming a niche product. Fee-linked valuation conflicts and stale marks (B2) undermine confidence in private markets.
1–3 years: FCA private-markets conflicts review; simpler Assessment of Value; DC Value for Money ratings from 2028; continued US litigation. ESG rebranded as "transition", "resilience" or "stewardship". 5+ years: standardised valuation disclosure for retail private funds; stewardship judged on outcomes rather than pledges.
Insiders now argue about whether ESG was ever a product category or simply a risk-management discipline that every fund should use. Where do you stand? Either answer is fine if you can defend it.
Never overclaim on sustainability or liquidity. The FCA's anti-greenwashing rule and Consumer Duty apply to what you say in meetings and emails. Know your fund's SDR label, or why it doesn't have one.

B11 · Markets: concentration, the dollar, oil and gilts

From zero: because ~80% of recent revenue growth came from markets (B0), what happens in markets is the business outlook. Four market stories dominate client conversations in 2026. Each connects to a concept from Guide Part A.

1 · US concentration

The top 10 stocks make up roughly 37–41% of the S&P 500, a record above the ~27% dot-com peak. A "global tracker" fund is roughly 70% US and heavily exposed to about 10 technology companies. Concept: diversification (Guide A3). A passive fund can be far less diversified than it looks.

2 · The dollar

The US dollar index fell 10.8% in the first half of 2025, its worst first half since 1973, after the tariff shock ("Sell America"). For a UK investor, a falling dollar shrinks US returns in pounds. Concept: currency risk, and why "hedged share classes" exist.

3 · The 2026 oil shock

The Middle East conflict and Strait of Hormuz disruption cut flows through the strait from ~21.6m to ~4.9m barrels a day (EIA). Brent crude peaked at about $126 in April 2026 and was back above $100 in September. Concept: inflation (Guide A2). Energy shocks raise prices and interest-rate expectations.

4 · UK gilts

The 10-year gilt yield touched 5.295% in September 2026, the highest since 2007. The 30-year passed 6% (single source), the highest since 1998. The Bank of England held rates at 3.75% with CPI at 3.1%. Concept: bond prices fall when yields rise (Guide A2).
Why gilts matter to pension clients: DB pension schemes hold lots of gilts to match future payments (Guide B4, LDI). Higher yields reduce the value placed on their future liabilities, improving funding, but sudden spikes can trigger collateral calls, as in the 2022 LDI crisis. Schemes hold more collateral now, but rapid long-end moves revive that comparison. Fewer DB schemes buying long gilts also means structurally weaker demand for them.

What managers are doing about it

  • Pushing diversification away from the US: international and European equity funds, "equal-weight" products, currency-hedged share classes.
  • Selling "income is back": locking in ~5% gilt yields; money market and short-duration bond funds (£5.8bn into UK MMFs in 2025).
  • Pitching real assets and infrastructure as inflation hedges.
OpportunityHigh yields make bonds attractive for the first time in a decade. Volatility rewards active managers and good advice. Diversified, global and real-asset products have a strong story.
RiskA stagflationary energy shock plus a fiscal risk premium on gilts. Passive trackers concentrated in a few US stocks. Industry revenue is highly exposed to a market fall.
The Bank of England's next rate decisions · Brent crude · top-10 weight of the S&P 500 · the dollar index · gilt auction demand ("cover ratios") · the market's reaction to the UK Budget
Be able to explain in plain English: why a global tracker is a concentrated US tech bet; what a 6% 30-year gilt means for a pension client; and the case for diversifiers. That's real client conversation material.

B12 · Operating models, outsourcing and investment trust activism

From zero: historically, each asset manager ran its own patchwork of systems for trading, risk, accounting and reporting (Guide B8). Now firms buy "front-to-back" platforms, one integrated system from portfolio management to accounting, or outsource middle and back office to custodians. The aim is lower cost and a single source of clean data, which AI depends on.
Platform / moveDetail

Bear caseVendor lock-in (relying on a competitor's technology), migration projects that overrun, loss of in-house know-how, and rising data costs.

UK investment trusts: the activist battle

From zero: an investment trust is a listed company that invests in other assets (Guide B9). Its shares can trade below the value of its assets: a discount. In early 2025, US hedge fund Saba Capital (Boaz Weinstein) requisitioned meetings at seven UK trusts, arguing that boards weren't fixing wide discounts. Shareholders voted the proposals down. At Edinburgh Worldwide, 98.4% (2025) and 92.7% (2026) of non-Saba votes went against. A second wave pushed for conversion to open-ended funds, and a July 2026 requisition targeted Gore Street Energy Storage.

Activists are rightThey force boards to tackle discounts through buybacks, tenders and mergers, which ultimately helps all shareholders.
Activists are wrongShort-term arbitrage that destroys permanent capital, the ideal structure for illiquid assets, and a natural home for UK private-markets exposure.
Connect the dots: Woodford (2019) showed open-ended funds holding illiquid assets can break. Activists now push closed-ended trusts to become open-ended. What's the risk? Insiders who make this link sound genuinely expert.
Due diligence questionnaires ask about operational resilience, outsourcing and data governance, so understand your firm's technology stack at a high level. If your firm runs trusts, discount control and board engagement are client-facing topics.

Six predictions to test (and argue with)

Distribution beats performance. With ~80% of growth coming from markets, the managers that win net new money will be those that own or partner with distribution: models, platforms and megafund relationships.
Private markets grow, but get "listed-fund-style" rules. 2026's gating wave leads to standardised liquidity and valuation disclosure for retail products.
The squeezed middle disappears. Mid-sized UK active houses get bought, merge or specialise. Expect more Schroders-style deals.
Active survives in new wrappers. Active ETFs and model-portfolio-friendly share classes become the main growth route for active strategies.
Client-facing work becomes more consultative. Fewer, larger, more sophisticated clients (megafunds, LGPS pools, MPS gatekeepers) demand expertise, not just relationships.
AI hollows out production, not judgement. Pitch books, DDQs and commentary get automated, and value shifts to accuracy, trust and the relationship.
