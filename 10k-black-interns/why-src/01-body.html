What a great "why" actually is

Most applicants say what they want ("I want to work in investment management"). Good applicants say why in general terms ("because I'm interested in markets"). The best applicants show a personal reason that connects to how the industry actually works, and prove it with something they did.

The "motivation chain". A value with no industry truth is just a nice story. Industry knowledge with no value is a textbook. You need both, joined.

What "outside the box" means here

It does not mean gimmicky, dramatic or exaggerated. It means an angle that's true but unexpected, a link the reader hasn't seen 30 times that day. The surprise usually comes from connecting something ordinary from your life to something specific about the industry:

Expected
"I've always been interested in finance and the stock market."
Unexpected but true
"The most important financial decision in my family was never about the stock market. It was about whether to keep money in a savings account that was quietly losing to inflation."
Why it works
Personal, specific and humble, and it leads straight into the real purpose of investment management (Guide A2, B1).

Three rules for writing about values and upbringing

1 · Lesson, not life story. One moment, then what it taught you. Spend at most 30–40 words of 250 on the memory, and the rest on what it means now.
2 · Show, don't label. "I'm resilient" is a claim. "I sent 40 emails before I got my first reply" is proof.
3 · Only what you're happy to expand on. Interviewers often say "tell me more about that". Share only what you'd be comfortable talking about for two minutes. You never have to disclose anything personal you'd rather keep private.

1A · Your money story

Everyone grows up with messages about money: spoken or unspoken, positive or difficult. These are some of the most powerful raw materials for an investment management answer, because the industry exists to answer one question: how do ordinary people's savings grow and stay safe over a lifetime?

1. What's your earliest memory involving money, whether saving, spending, worrying or celebrating?
2. Growing up, what did the adults around you say or believe about money? (e.g. "save for a rainy day", "don't trust banks", "property is the only safe investment", "never borrow")
3. Who in your family or community was "good with money"? What did they do differently?
4. Did you ever see a money decision go well, or badly, for someone close to you? What made the difference?
5. When did you first realise that money could grow (interest, investing, a business), and not just be earned and spent?
6. Have you noticed prices rising, at home, at the shops or on rent, and what it meant for people's choices?
7. Did anyone in your life save into a pension, an ISA or a property, or send money to family elsewhere? What did that teach you about responsibility and the long term?
What to look for in your answers: phrases about patience, security, responsibility for others, trust or distrust of institutions, the long term, small habits adding up, or knowledge gaps (people not knowing their options). Each of these maps directly onto a real truth about investment management (see Part 2).

1C · Moments, people and frustrations

Moments of pride

A time you persuaded someone to change their mind. How did you do it?
A time you kept going after being rejected or told no
A time you explained something complicated so someone finally understood it
A time you stayed calm when others didn't
Something you sold, organised, raised money for or built

People and frustrations

Who do you trust most, and why? What do they do that earns trust?
Who's the best communicator you know? What do they do differently?
What annoys you about how things are explained, sold or reported, to you or your family?
When did you see someone or something misrepresented (in the news, online, at school), and how did it feel?
What do you lose track of time doing?
Why frustrations are gold: a strong "why" often starts with something you noticed was wrong or missing. For example: people not understanding their pension, a salesperson who didn't listen, a story told unfairly. Industries exist to fix problems, and a frustration shows you've noticed one. Keep the tone constructive: "I saw a gap, and I want to be part of closing it."

1D · Mining your asset management experience

Your previous asset management role is your biggest asset. But "I did an internship" is a fact, not a motivation. The motivation lies in what you noticed and felt while you were there. Answer these as specifically as you can.

What was the moment you thought "this is interesting"? Where were you and what was happening?
What surprised you about how the firm actually worked, compared with what you expected?
Who impressed you most, and what exactly did they do? (e.g. how they handled a client, explained a fund, ran a meeting)
What was the hardest task you did, and how did you approach it?
What did you produce or contribute that someone actually used?
Did you see anything client-facing: a pitch, a report, an RFP, a client meeting? What struck you?
Did you see how the firm communicated externally (press, commentary, social media)? What did you notice?
What do you want to learn next that you didn't get to?

Part 3 · Investment Management↑ Contents

3A · Investment Management angles

1 · Inflation at the kitchen table

TruthCash loses real value when interest is below inflation. That's the whole reason investing exists (Guide A2).
NeedsA memory of prices rising, saving in cash, or a family money conversation
Line"Watching [who] save carefully into an account that paid less than inflation taught me that being responsible with money isn't enough on its own. It has to be invested well."
Avoid ifYou'd rather not discuss family finances in an interview

2 · Compounding as a life philosophy

TruthSmall, consistent gains snowball, and so do small fee differences (Guide A3)
NeedsA habit you built over time: training, study, saving, practising a skill
Line"[Your habit] taught me that small, consistent effort compounds, which is exactly what drew me to an industry where decades of patient decisions shape whether millions retire comfortably."
Avoid ifThe habit is trivial or you can't describe the payoff

3 · The invisible custodian

TruthAlmost everyone is an asset manager's client through their pension, yet few know it (Guide B1)
NeedsNoticing that you or people around you didn't know where your pension or savings were invested
Line"Until my internship, I didn't know that my own workplace pension [or a family member's] was managed by firms like the one I was sitting in. Most people never find out."
Avoid ifYou can't link it to wanting to make the client side clearer

4 · The translator

TruthClient-facing roles translate complex investment processes into confidence (Guide B5)
NeedsA time you translated between worlds: generations, languages, cultures, school and home, experts and beginners
Line"I grew up [translating letters for / explaining forms to / tutoring] [who], so I've always noticed the gap between how experts speak and what people need to hear."
Avoid ifIt isn't genuinely part of your life

5 · Ownership and a voice

TruthInvestors own companies and vote on how they're run. This is stewardship (Guide B7).
NeedsAn interest in how companies behave, or the realisation that "a share is ownership"
Line"What changed my view of the industry was realising that a fund doesn't just hold shares. It holds a voice in how hundreds of companies are run."
Avoid ifYou can't talk about stewardship or voting in an interview

6 · Keeping trust is harder than earning it

TruthRetention protects recurring fee revenue; one bad meeting can lose a mandate (Guide B5, B10)
NeedsA part-time job, team or role where you kept a customer's or a group's trust over time
Line"At [job] I learned that regulars came back because of how we treated them, not one great day. It's the same reason a client stays with an asset manager through a bad year."
Avoid ifThe example has no element of long-term relationships

7 · Widening the circle of investors

TruthDC pensions and ISAs are pushing millions more people towards investing, and many lack confidence (Guide B13)
NeedsA genuine observation: friends who don't invest, family who distrust it, your own learning curve
Line"Many people I grew up around [kept savings in cash / didn't trust investing]. Not because they were careless, but because nobody explained it to them in a way that made sense."
Avoid ifIt becomes a speech. Keep it to one observation, then the industry.

8 · Patience in a world of instant results

TruthInstitutions invest over decades, while markets and social media reward short-termism (Guide B4)
NeedsA value of patience, faith, delayed gratification, or a long project you saw through
Line"In a world built around instant results, I'm drawn to an industry whose best decisions are judged over ten or twenty years."
Avoid ifYou can't back it with something you did patiently

3B · Sales angles

1 · The first thing I ever sold

TruthEvery business runs on someone finding a customer and earning their yes (Guide C1)
NeedsSelling something at school, reselling clothes or trainers, a family business, a market stall, a fundraiser
Line"The first thing I ever sold was [what]. I learned more from the [number] people who said no than from the ones who said yes."
Avoid ifIt can't lead into a real lesson about customers

2 · Listening is the product

TruthDiscovery and consultative selling win; buyers arrive informed (Guide C3, C4)
NeedsSomeone who earned your trust by listening, or a time listening changed an outcome
Line"The best salesperson I ever met barely talked about the product. [What they did instead]."
Avoid ifYou can't describe the person or moment specifically

3 · Rejection as training

TruthThe funnel means most attempts fail, so resilience is structural (Guide C5)
NeedsSport, auditions, applications or cold outreach where "no" was normal
Line"[Sport / activity] taught me that losing is information. Sales is the only job I know where that mindset is literally built into the numbers."
Avoid ifYou'd be making the resilience sound bigger than it was

4 · Sales is service

TruthEthical, value-based selling builds repeat business; pushy selling destroys it (Guide C4)
NeedsA value of service or faith, or a bad experience of being "sold to"
Line"I was raised to believe [the value / saying], which is why the sales I admire start with what the customer needs, not what the seller wants to hit."
Avoid ifYou can't show you also respect targets and results

5 · The numbers behind persuasion

TruthModern sales is data-driven: pipeline, conversion, churn (Guide C5)
NeedsLiking data, a time you tracked results and improved them
Line"What surprised me about sales is how measurable persuasion is. Every stage of a pipeline tells you where trust was won or lost."
Avoid ifYou'd struggle to explain a sales funnel in an interview

6 · Relationships compound

TruthAccount management and retention drive most revenue in recurring businesses (Guide C2)
NeedsLong relationships you've kept: regular customers, a mentoring role, a community
Line"In [community / job], the people who did best were the ones everyone trusted after years, not the loudest in the room. That's the kind of salesperson I want to become."
Avoid ifIt reads as avoiding the "closing" side; mention both

7 · I watched a £multi-million sale up close

TruthAsset management distribution is consultative B2B sales (Guide C6)
NeedsSomething client-facing you saw in your AM role
Line"Before my internship, I didn't think of fund management as sales. Then I watched [pitch / RFP / client meeting] and saw a months-long sale with five decision-makers."
Avoid ifThe reader might be a consumer brand. Keep it in sales language.

8 · Translator (sales version)

TruthValue selling means expressing a product in the buyer's terms (Guide C4)
NeedsA time you adapted how you spoke to different audiences
Line"Switching between [two worlds] taught me that the same idea needs different words for different people, which is the heart of good selling."
Avoid ifYou used this angle for IM. Pick a different one here.

8 · Owning mistakes well

TruthHonest, human responses can rebuild trust (KFC "FCK", Guide D5)
NeedsA value of humility or integrity, or a time you owned a mistake and it improved things

Part 4 · Cliché detector

Recruiters read the same phrases hundreds of times. If a sentence in your draft matches the left column, replace it using the method on the right.

ClichéWhy it failsUpgrade method
"I have always been passionate about…"Unprovable, and everyone says itReplace with the moment the interest started
"…a fast-paced, dynamic environment"Describes every jobName one specific thing about the industry's pace (e.g. a 7am results announcement)
"I want to develop my skills"About you, not themSay what you'll contribute, then what you'll learn
"I am a hard-working team player"Adjectives without evidenceGive an action plus a result that shows it
"Since a young age…"Vague and overusedGive the actual age, place or event
"I want to make a difference"Means nothing on its ownName the problem and who it affects
"I'm a people person"UnspecificDescribe how you build trust with people
"Finance has always fascinated me"GenericName the mechanism that fascinates you (compounding, fees on AUM, stewardship)
"PR appeals to my creative side"PR is also strategy, risk and researchPair creativity with judgement or news sense
"Sales suits my outgoing personality"Great sales is mostly listeningTalk about questions and listening
"I believe I would be a great asset"A claim, not a demonstrationDelete it. Your evidence should already show it.
Values list: "integrity, resilience, ambition"Labels with no storyPick one value, give its origin and one action
The "anyone test": read each sentence and ask, "Could any other applicant have written this?" If yes, it's either a cliché or a necessary fact. Keep necessary facts short and cut the clichés. Your aim is that at least half your sentences could only have been written by you.

Part 6 · The authenticity test and interview follow-ups

Six questions to ask of every answer

Is every sentence true? No inflated roles, invented moments or borrowed stories. Interviewers probe.
Could anyone else have written it? At least half the sentences should be uniquely yours.
Does the value connect to how the industry actually works? Not just "I like it", but a mechanism (AUM fees, discovery, earned media).
Is the value shown, not labelled? An origin and an action, not a list of adjectives.
Would I happily talk about this for two minutes? If not, swap it for a story you're comfortable expanding on.
Does it end looking forward? What you'll contribute and learn, not a summary of the past.

Prepare for "tell me more"

Whatever you write becomes interview material. For each answer, prepare a 60–90 second spoken version of the story, and answers to these likely follow-ups:

If you wrote about…Expect to be asked…
A family value or money memory"How has that shaped decisions you've made?" "How would you explain investing to that person today?"
Your AM internship"What did the firm do well or badly?" "What would you change about how they served clients?"
An industry trend"What's your view on it?" "How should a firm like ours respond?"
Selling or persuading"Sell me something." "Tell me about a time you failed to persuade someone."
Reputation or communication"What's a recent PR campaign or crisis you found interesting, and why?"
