A1 · How any business works

Every company, whether an asset manager, a software firm or a PR agency, does the same thing at heart: it sells something for more than it costs to provide.

Revenue (also "turnover", "sales" or "the top line") is the money a business earns from customers before any costs.

Profit ("the bottom line") is what remains after all costs.

Margin is profit as a percentage of revenue. £100 revenue and £30 profit is a 30% margin. A high margin means each pound of revenue is very profitable.

Fixed costs stay the same however much you sell (e.g. office rent, core staff). Variable costs rise with each sale (e.g. materials, commission).

Scale is the idea that once fixed costs are covered, each extra sale is mostly profit. That's why big firms in all three industries chase growth.

Four ways any business can grow revenue

1. New customers
Win clients you didn't have
2. Sell more
To existing customers (upsell, cross-sell)
3. Charge more
Raise prices or sell premium products
4. Keep customers
Every client lost must be replaced

Keep these four in mind. Sales exists to do 1 and 2. Account management and client service exist to do 2 and 4. PR protects 3 and 4, because reputation lets you charge more and keeps customers loyal. Investment management is a special case: its revenue also grows when markets rise (see B10).

Front office, middle office, back office

You'll hear these phrases constantly in finance. They describe how close a team is to the client and to revenue:

Front office

  • Generates revenue directly
  • e.g. fund managers, salespeople, client relationship managers

Middle office

  • Controls risk and checks the front office
  • e.g. risk, compliance, performance measurement

Back office

  • Processes and records everything
  • e.g. operations, settlement, accounting, IT

Cost centre vs profit centre

A profit centre brings in revenue (e.g. a sales team or an investment team). A cost centre supports the business but doesn't bill anyone directly (e.g. HR, in-house PR, compliance). Cost centres must constantly show their value. That's why in-house PR teams measure coverage and reputation so carefully (D7).

A2 · Shares, bonds, interest rates and inflation

Companies and governments need money. They raise it in two basic ways: by selling a piece of ownership (shares) or by borrowing (bonds). Investors buy these to grow their savings.

Shares (equities, stocks)

A share is a tiny slice of ownership in a company. If you own 1 share out of 1 million, you own one-millionth of the company.

  • You make money if the share price rises (capital growth) or the company pays you a slice of its profit (a dividend).
  • Higher potential return, but higher risk: if the company fails, shareholders are paid last and can lose everything.
  • Bought and sold on a stock exchange (e.g. the London Stock Exchange). The FTSE 100 is an index of the 100 largest UK-listed companies.

Bonds (fixed income, debt)

A bond is a loan you make to a government or company. They promise to pay you interest (the coupon) and return your money on a set date (maturity).

  • UK government bonds are called gilts. Company bonds are corporate bonds.
  • Lower risk than shares, because bondholders are paid before shareholders. Returns are more predictable, hence "fixed income".
  • Credit rating (AAA down to junk) shows how likely the borrower is to repay.

Interest rates

The interest rate is the price of borrowing money. In the UK, the Bank of England sets the "base rate", which influences every other rate in the economy. Remember four things:

  • Rates up → borrowing costs more, saving pays more, and economic growth tends to slow.
  • Bond prices move opposite to interest rates. If new bonds pay 5%, an old bond paying 2% is worth less, so its price falls. This is the single most-tested fixed income idea in interviews.
  • Higher rates usually put pressure on share prices, because future profits are worth less today and companies pay more on their debt.
  • The yield is the return you'd earn on a bond at today's price. When prices fall, yields rise.

Inflation

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise. Measured in the UK by CPI, it means that £100 today buys less next year. Central banks aim for about 2%. It matters because cash sitting in a bank loses real value whenever interest is below inflation. That is the core reason people invest at all, and it's a powerful opening idea for an investment management answer.

A3 · Risk, return, diversification and compounding

Risk and return go together

The most basic rule of investing is that to earn a higher return, you must accept more risk. Risk here means uncertainty: how much the value can swing, and the chance of losing money. A professional investor's job is not to avoid risk but to take the right risks, and be paid properly for them.

The risk–return ladder (illustrative). Higher bars mean more expected return over the long run, and more volatility and risk of loss along the way.

Volatility is how much an investment's price swings up and down. It's measured by standard deviation. A fund with 20% volatility swings much more than one with 5%.

Liquidity is how quickly you can sell something for cash without moving the price. Shares in big companies are very liquid. Property and private companies are illiquid, which can take months or years.

Time horizon is how long until you need the money. A 25-year-old saving for retirement can take more risk than a 64-year-old, because they have time to recover from falls.

Risk appetite (or tolerance) is how much loss a client can emotionally and financially accept. Matching investments to a client's horizon and appetite is the heart of client-facing work.

Diversification: "don't put all your eggs in one basket"

If you own one company and it fails, you lose everything. If you own 500 companies across countries and industries, one failure barely matters. Diversification reduces risk without necessarily reducing expected return. It's often called "the only free lunch in investing". It is why most people invest through funds (pooled portfolios of many investments) rather than buying individual shares.

Asset allocation means deciding how much to put in each asset type (e.g. 60% shares / 40% bonds). It is the biggest driver of a portfolio's long-run result, more than picking individual shares.

Compounding: why time matters so much

Compounding means earning returns on your previous returns. Growth snowballs:

£10,000 invested at 6% a yearAfter 10 yearsAfter 20 yearsAfter 40 years
Value≈ £17,900≈ £32,100≈ £102,900
Small fee differences compound too. On a pension pot over 40 years, paying 1.0% a year instead of 0.2% can reduce the final value by roughly a quarter or more. That's why fees are so fiercely debated in investment management, and why regulators push firms to prove "value for money" (B12).

Active vs passive in one paragraph

Passive investing buys every company in an index (e.g. all of the FTSE 100) to match the market's return at very low cost. Active investing pays professionals to pick investments, aiming to beat the market, which is called generating alpha, for a higher fee. Much of the modern industry is shaped by the battle between the two (B13).

A4 · Customers, B2B vs B2C and regulation

B2B: business-to-business

  • Selling to organisations (e.g. an asset manager selling to a pension fund, or software sold to a bank)
  • Few customers, but each is worth a lot
  • Many decision-makers, formal processes, long sales cycles (months)
  • Relationships and trust are everything

B2C: business-to-consumer

  • Selling to individuals (e.g. a fund sold to you via an app, or a supermarket product)
  • Many customers, each worth relatively little
  • Quick decisions, often emotional, driven by brand and marketing
  • Scale and reputation are everything

There's also B2B2C, where you sell through an intermediary to the end customer. Asset managers selling funds through financial advisers and investment platforms (who then serve the public) is a classic example. That's called the wholesale or intermediary channel (B4).

Stakeholders

A stakeholder is anyone affected by, or able to affect, an organisation: customers, employees, shareholders/investors, regulators, government, suppliers, media and local communities. PR is essentially the management of relationships with stakeholders (D1).

Regulation: why finance is different

Because financial firms look after other people's money, they're tightly regulated. In the UK:

BodyWhat it does
FCA (Financial Conduct Authority)Regulates how financial firms behave, including asset managers, advisers and banks' conduct. Protects consumers and market integrity. It can fine firms and ban individuals.
PRA (Prudential Regulation Authority)Part of the Bank of England. Makes sure banks and insurers are financially safe (hold enough capital).
Bank of EnglandSets interest rates and looks after overall financial stability.
The Pensions Regulator (TPR)Oversees workplace pension schemes and their trustees.
CMA (Competition and Markets Authority)Competition and consumer protection across all industries, including misleading "green" claims in marketing.
ASA (Advertising Standards Authority)Rules on advertising and marketing honesty. Relevant to PR and sales.

Fiduciary duty

A fiduciary is someone legally obliged to act in another person's best interest. Asset managers and pension trustees have fiduciary-style duties to their clients. This idea, "we look after other people's money and must put them first", sits underneath everything in investment management, and it is a strong theme to show you understand.

B1 · What investment management is

An asset manager takes money from many savers, pools it, and invests it in shares, bonds, property and other assets, aiming to grow it in line with each client's goals. In return it charges a small yearly percentage of the money it looks after.

Analogy: imagine 10,000 people each have £1,000 to save. On their own, none can afford a professional or buy enough different investments to be safe. Pooled together they have £10 million. They can now hire a team of experts, own hundreds of companies, and pay each expert a tiny fraction of the pot. That pooled pot is a fund, and the firm running it is an asset manager.

Where the money actually comes from

Most people never deal with an asset manager directly, but almost everyone in the UK is a client indirectly:

Why it matters (your "why" material)

For savers: it's how ordinary people beat inflation and afford retirement. The quality of investment decisions directly affects how millions of people live in old age.
For the economy: it channels savings into companies, infrastructure and governments that need capital to grow. This is called capital allocation.
For companies: as shareholders, asset managers vote and engage with company boards on strategy, pay and climate. This is called stewardship.

Scale

The UK is the world's second-largest asset management centre after the US. The Investment Association reports roughly £9 trillion managed in the UK. Globally, the industry manages well over $100 trillion. The largest firm, BlackRock, manages more than $10 trillion on its own.

Buy-side vs sell-side

Buy-side means firms that buy investments with clients' money: asset managers, hedge funds, private equity, pension funds and insurers. You are buy-side.
Sell-side means investment banks and brokers that sell services to the buy-side, such as research, trading, and new shares or bonds. Their clients are the buy-side.
Asset managers and wealth managers are related but different. An asset manager builds funds and portfolios, usually for institutions or for sale through intermediaries. A wealth manager works with individual (often wealthy) clients on their whole financial picture and often buys asset managers' funds for those clients. In other words, wealth managers are often an asset manager's client.

Public vs private markets

Public markets: traded on exchanges, with prices every second, high transparency and easy buying and selling. This is where traditional asset managers grew up.
Private markets: not exchange-traded and valued only occasionally. Money is locked up for years, but there is potential for higher returns and fees. This is the fastest-growing area of the industry, and traditional managers are racing to build or buy capabilities (B13).

Investment styles

Active: picks investments to beat a benchmark, for higher fees
Passive / index: tracks a benchmark at very low cost
Growth: companies expected to grow fast (e.g. tech), often expensive
Value: companies that look cheap relative to their worth
Income: companies or bonds paying high, steady dividends or coupons
Quantitative ("quant"): investment decisions driven by data and models
Fundamental: deep research into individual companies' businesses
Top-down vs bottom-up: start from the economy vs start from individual companies
Sustainable / ESG: considers environmental, social and governance factors
Smart beta / factor: rules-based funds tilted to factors like value or quality, a hybrid of active and passive
Ask any portfolio manager "what's your process?" and you'll hear a version of: philosophy (what we believe drives returns) → process (how we find and choose investments) → portfolio construction (how we size positions and control risk). Consultants judge managers on whether these three are clear, consistent and repeatable. Using that language shows real understanding.

The investment process, step by step

›

›

The investment team roles

Portfolio manager (PM) / fund manager
Makes the final investment decisions and is accountable for the fund's performance. Often meets major clients.
Research analyst
Covers a sector (e.g. banks, healthcare) or region and recommends buys and sells. "Buy-side analyst" is a common entry route.
Dealer / trader
Executes trades efficiently and quietly, so big orders don't push the price against the fund.
Economist / strategist
Views on the economy, interest rates and asset allocation.
Stewardship / responsible investment
Votes at company meetings and engages boards on governance, climate and pay.
Investment risk
Measures and challenges the risks the PM takes. Independent from the PM.

How performance is judged

Benchmark: the index a fund is compared with (e.g. MSCI World, FTSE All-Share)
Absolute return: how much the fund made (e.g. +8%)
Relative return / excess return: versus the benchmark (fund +8%, benchmark +6% = +2%)
Alpha: return from the manager's skill, above what the market delivered
Beta: how much a fund moves with the market. Beta 1 = moves in line.
Tracking error: how far a fund's returns drift from its benchmark (a risk measure for active funds)
Sharpe ratio: return per unit of risk taken. Higher is better.
Drawdown: the fall from a peak to a trough. Clients remember big drawdowns.
Peer group / quartile: how the fund ranks against similar funds. "Top quartile" means the best 25%.
Track record: performance history. Consultants usually want 3–5+ years.

A worked example

A global equity fund returns +11% this year. Its benchmark, the MSCI World, returned +9%. The fund charges 0.75%. Net of fees, the client got roughly +10.25%, an excess return of about +1.25%. The client relationship manager's job at the review meeting is to explain why (which decisions added value, and which detracted) and whether the process should keep working. If the fund had returned +11% while the benchmark did +14%, the conversation would be about underperformance, even though the client made money.

Performance attribution breaks down where excess return came from: asset allocation (being in the right markets) vs stock selection (picking the right companies). Client-facing teams use attribution reports in every review meeting. Knowing the term signals that you understand what client conversations actually involve.

B9 · Products and fund vehicles

The strategy is what the manager does (e.g. "global equity income"). The vehicle is the legal wrapper clients buy it through. One strategy can be sold in several vehicles to different clients.

Pooled funds (many investors share one portfolio)

  • OEIC / unit trust: UK open-ended funds. Investors buy and sell at the daily NAV, and the fund grows or shrinks with demand.
  • UCITS: the EU standard for retail funds, sellable across Europe (often Dublin or Luxembourg "SICAVs"). A strong investor-protection badge.
  • ETF (exchange-traded fund): a fund that trades on a stock exchange like a share. Usually passive and cheap. A fast-growing vehicle.
  • Investment trust: a UK company listed on the stock exchange that invests in other assets. Its shares can trade above (premium) or below (discount) the value of its assets.
  • LTAF (Long-Term Asset Fund): a UK vehicle (since 2021) designed to let DC pensions access illiquid private assets.

Segregated mandates (one client, own portfolio)

A large client (e.g. a £1bn pension scheme) has a portfolio run just for them, with custom restrictions (e.g. no tobacco) and negotiated fees. Governed by an investment management agreement (IMA).

Private market funds

Closed-ended limited partnerships. Investors commit money that's "called" over time, locked up for 7–12 years, with fees on committed capital plus carried interest. The terms are GP (general partner, the manager) and LP (limited partner, the investor).

Model portfolios

Ready-made portfolios that wealth managers and advisers use for their clients. A big growth area in wholesale.

Open-ended vs closed-ended

Open-ended (OEIC, unit trust, most UCITS, ETFs)Closed-ended (investment trusts, private equity funds)

The 2019 Woodford case is the classic lesson. The Woodford Equity Income Fund was a daily-dealing open-ended fund that held hard-to-sell unlisted companies. When performance fell, investors rushed to withdraw, and the fund had to suspend dealing in June 2019, trapping investors. It then wound down. It's cited constantly as an example of liquidity mismatch, of reputational collapse, and of why the FCA scrutinises fund structures. It's useful in IM and PR conversations.

Share classes and charges

One fund can have several share classes with different fees (e.g. a retail class, a cheaper "clean" class for platforms, and an institutional class for big investors). Retail funds show an OCF (ongoing charges figure): the annual cost as a percentage, including the management fee and running costs. Under the PRIIPs and UK consumer rules, investors must also receive standardised documents showing costs and risks (e.g. a KID / KIID).

B13 · What's happening now: key trends

Pick one of these for your answer and understand it deeply. The others are interview ammunition.

1 · The passive shift and fee compression

Cheap index funds and ETFs keep winning money from active funds, especially in equities. Average fees are falling. Active managers must prove they're worth paying for, through performance, specialist strategies or better client solutions.

So what for the client side: explaining value clearly has never mattered more.

2 · Private markets go mainstream

Private equity, private credit and infrastructure are growing much faster than traditional funds and carry higher fees. Traditional managers are buying private-markets firms or partnering with them. New vehicles (LTAFs, ELTIFs, semi-liquid "evergreen" funds) open them to DC pensions and wealthy individuals.

So what: lots of client education, because these products are complex and illiquid.

3 · UK pension reform

Government reforms (including the Pension Schemes Bill) push DC schemes to consolidate into fewer "megafunds" with large minimum sizes by around 2030, and LGPS assets into fewer, bigger pools. Under the 2025 Mansion House Accord, major DC providers pledged to put 10% of default funds into private markets by 2030, with at least half of that in the UK.

So what: fewer, larger, more sophisticated clients. Distribution becomes more consultative and the competition fiercer.

4 · Consolidation and cost pressure

Squeezed margins drive mergers, partnerships and job cuts. Firms outsource operations and invest in technology. Mid-sized active managers feel the most pressure, caught between giant passive firms and specialist boutiques.

So what: firms want people who add value quickly, can use technology and understand clients.

5 · Wealth and DC as the growth engines

Money is shifting from DB schemes to individuals: DC pots, ISAs and wealth managers. Model portfolios, platforms and digital investing are growing. The government also wants more people to invest rather than hold cash (e.g. ISA reforms announced in 2025).

So what: retail and wholesale distribution, plus Consumer Duty, matter more each year.

6 · Sustainability: still important, now more nuanced

ESG went through a boom, then a backlash (especially in the US) and greater scrutiny of "greenwashing". UK SDR labels now set standards. Clients still ask detailed questions about stewardship, climate and voting.

So what: you need precise, evidence-based communication, not slogans.

7 · AI and data

AI is used to summarise research, draft RFPs, personalise client reporting, analyse data and automate operations. Firms are investing heavily but carefully, because of accuracy and compliance concerns.

So what: entry-level work is changing. Judgement, relationships and checking AI output become more valuable.

8 · ETFs and tokenisation

Active ETFs (active strategies in an ETF wrapper) are growing fast, and Europe is catching up with the US. Some firms are experimenting with tokenised funds recorded on blockchain to speed up settlement.

So what: product and distribution teams are rethinking how funds reach investors.

A one-sentence synthesis you can adapt: "As fee pressure, the growth of passive investing and pension consolidation create fewer, larger and more demanding clients, the firms that win will be those that can explain their value clearly and build long-term trust." That sentence links three trends to why client-facing work matters.

B15 · Expert check: can you answer these out loud?

If you can answer each in two or three sentences without notes, you understand investment management better than most applicants. A short model answer is given for each.

How does an asset manager make money?
A percentage fee on assets under management, sometimes plus performance fees. Revenue grows through market performance and net flows, and costs are mainly people and technology.
What's the difference between institutional and wholesale clients?
Institutional clients are organisations such as pension schemes and insurers, investing large sums, usually via consultants and RFPs. Wholesale clients are intermediaries such as wealth managers, IFAs and platforms that select funds for individuals.
Why do investment consultants matter?
They advise pension trustees and shortlist managers, acting as gatekeepers. Without a positive consultant rating, winning institutional business is very difficult.
What's the difference between DB and DC pensions, and why does it matter?
DB guarantees an income and the scheme bears the risk. Most are closed and de-risking. In DC, members bear the risk. DC is growing fast and consolidating, so it's a key growth market.
Active vs passive: what's the debate?
Passive is cheaper and many active funds fail to beat their benchmark after fees, so money has moved to passive. Active managers argue for skill, risk management and access to areas passive can't reach, such as private markets.
Why might interest rates rising hurt bond prices?
Existing bonds pay fixed coupons. When new bonds offer higher rates, old ones become less attractive, so their prices fall until their yield matches the market.
What does a client relationship manager actually do?
Owns existing client relationships: runs review meetings explaining performance and positioning, handles requests, anticipates needs, and protects and grows the firm's revenue by retaining and expanding mandates.
Name a current industry trend and why it matters.
E.g. UK pension consolidation: fewer, bigger schemes with more bargaining power and interest in private markets, which raises the bar for distribution teams.
What is Consumer Duty?
An FCA rule since 2023 requiring firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers, including fair value, clear communication and suitable products.
Why is retention as important as winning new business?
Fees recur every year a client stays, and new mandates take months to win. Each lost client must be replaced just to stand still, so servicing protects the revenue base.

How salespeople are paid

Usually base salary + commission, adding up to "OTE" (on-target earnings). A typical split is 50/50 or 60/40 base/variable. C9 · Expert check: can you answer these out loud?

What's the difference between sales and marketing?
Marketing creates awareness and interest across many people at once. Sales converts that interest into customers through one-to-one conversations, then helps retain and grow them.
Walk me through a B2B sales process.
Prospect → outreach → qualify (e.g. BANT) → discovery → tailored solution → proposal → handle objections → negotiate → close → onboard → renew and expand.
Why is discovery so important?
Because you can't sell value until you understand the buyer's problem and its impact. Good discovery lets the buyer articulate the need, which makes the solution compelling and the price justified.
What does an SDR do vs an AE?
SDRs generate and qualify opportunities and book meetings. AEs run the sales process from discovery to close and own a revenue quota.
What is churn, and why does it matter?
The rate at which customers or revenue are lost. High churn means you must keep replacing customers just to stand still, so retention and account management protect growth.
What's pipeline coverage, and why aim for about 3×?
Pipeline value divided by target. Since only a fraction of deals close, you need several times your target in pipeline to hit it reliably.
How would you handle "it's too expensive"?
Acknowledge it, explore what's behind it, reconnect the price to the value or the cost of the problem, consider options (scope, terms), and check that the concern is resolved.
What is SPIN selling?
A questioning approach (Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff) that helps buyers recognise the impact of their problem and the value of solving it themselves.
How is AI changing sales?
It automates research, outreach and call analysis, so human value shifts towards complex conversations, judgement and long-term relationships.
How does asset management distribution resemble B2B sales?
Long cycles, many decision-makers (trustees, consultants), formal proposals (RFPs), pitches, and recurring fee revenue that depends on retention, all handled by sales and relationship teams.

D1 · What PR is (and isn't)

PR is the strategic management of communication between an organisation and the people who matter to it (its stakeholders), to build understanding, trust and reputation.

The simplest distinction: advertising is what you say about yourself in space you paid for. PR is getting others, such as journalists, experts, customers and influencers, to say it for you. Because it's third-party, it's more credible. Because you don't control it, it's harder.

GoalDrive sales or awarenessDrive demand for productsBuild reputation, trust and understanding
AudienceCustomersCustomersAll stakeholders: media, investors, staff, government, communities, customers
ControlFull: you buy the spaceHighLow: journalists decide what to write
CredibilityLower ("they would say that")MediumHigher (third-party endorsement)
Includes bad news?NoRarelyYes: crisis, issues, results, job cuts

The PESO model: the four types of media

Paid
Adverts, sponsored content, paid social, advertorials
Earned
Press coverage, interviews, reviews, awards. The core of PR.
Shared
Social media, influencers, word of mouth, reposts
Owned
Website, blog, reports, newsletters, the company's own channels

Modern PR works across all four ("integrated communications"). A research report (owned) can generate a news story (earned), be amplified on LinkedIn (shared) and be promoted with a paid boost (paid).

Why reputation is worth money

Customers pay more and stay loyal to brands they trust
Investors value companies they understand and trust more highly, while scandals can wipe billions off share prices in a day
Employees, regulators and government: a good reputation attracts talent, eases regulation and earns a "licence to operate"
Warren Buffett's line is quoted constantly in PR: "It takes 20 years to build a reputation and five minutes to ruin it." Reputation is an intangible asset: you can't see it on a balance sheet, but it shows up in sales, share prices and hiring. PR people see themselves as its guardians. Framing PR as protecting and growing a valuable asset, not just "getting press", is the expert view.

The relationship is give-and-take. Journalists are under enormous time pressure, with fewer of them covering more topics. PR people who reliably provide accurate information, good spokespeople, data and fast responses become trusted sources. Those who spam irrelevant press releases get ignored. "Thinking like a journalist" is the core PR skill.

D5 · Crisis communications

A crisis is an event that threatens an organisation's reputation, operations or finances and demands urgent communication. An issue is a slower-burning problem that could become a crisis if ignored.

1 · PrepareCrisis plan, scenarios, holding statements, a trained team, a "dark site"
›
2 · Respond fastFirst statement within the "golden hour": acknowledge, show concern, commit to action
›
3 · ManageRegular updates, one voice, all stakeholders (staff, customers, regulators, media)
›
4 · RecoverFix the cause, show change, rebuild trust over time
›
5 · LearnA review: what worked, what to change

The principles

Speed: a silence gets filled by others, often with speculation
Empathy first: show you care about the people affected before defending the organisation
Honesty: never lie or speculate. Say what you know, what you don't and when you'll update.
Responsibility: take ownership where appropriate; deflecting blame backfires
Consistency: one agreed message across every channel and spokesperson
Action: words must be backed by visible actions
Holding statement: a short, pre-prepared first response. "We are aware of [situation]. Our immediate priority is [the safety/wellbeing of those affected]. We are working urgently to understand what happened and will provide a further update by [time]."

Case studies every PR candidate should know

CaseWhat happenedLesson
KFC "FCK" (UK, 2018)A delivery-supplier change left hundreds of restaurants without chicken. KFC ran a full-page apology ad with its bucket spelling "FCK", humorous and humble.Owning a mistake with honesty and personality can improve reputation. Now a widely awarded campaign.
BP Deepwater Horizon (2010)A huge Gulf of Mexico oil spill. The CEO's remark "I'd like my life back" caused outrage.Empathy for victims must come first. One careless quote can define a crisis.
Woodford fund suspension (2019)A star UK fund manager's flagship fund froze withdrawals, trapping investors. Then the business collapsed.In finance, reputation is the product. Loss of trust becomes loss of assets almost immediately.

In the social media era the "golden hour" is often minutes, and employees, customers and creators may break the story before the press does. Modern crisis teams monitor social channels 24/7 and coordinate legal, HR, operations and comms together. If you mention crisis work in your answer, emphasise calm judgement under pressure and clear, honest writing at speed.
