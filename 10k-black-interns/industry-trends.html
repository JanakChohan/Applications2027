A1 · Six habits of industry thinking

Industry professionals don't know the future either. What they have is a set of habits for turning news into a view. Learn these and every page that follows becomes easier, and you'll sound like an insider in interviews.

1 · Follow the money

For any trend, ask: who earns more, and who earns less, if this happens? Private markets grow because managers earn ~4× more profit per $bn there (B1). Outcome pricing spreads because AI shrinks seat revenue (C5). The money usually explains the motive.

2 · Think in second-order effects

First order: "AI writes emails faster." Second order: "everyone sends more emails, so inboxes flood, so providers tighten spam rules, so volume stops working, so human relevance becomes scarce" (C1). The interesting insights are usually two or three steps out.

3 · Separate structural from cyclical

Cyclical trends reverse when conditions change (e.g. a bad year for markets). Structural ones persist (e.g. passive's share rising for 20 years). The key question is always: "will this still be true in five years if conditions normalise?"

4 · Steelman both sides

Every theme here has a bull and a bear case. Before forming a view, be able to state the strongest version of the side you disagree with. Interviewers love "the bull case is X, but I lean towards Y because…"

5 · Watch leading indicators

Headlines are lagging. Insiders watch the numbers that move first: redemption requests before gates (B2), reply rates before pipeline (C1), click-through before newsroom cuts (D2). Each theme lists what to watch.

6 · Hold views loosely

A strong opinion is a position + evidence + what would change your mind. "I think X because of A and B; I'd change my mind if C happened." That's how professionals talk, and it sounds far more credible than certainty.

Recurring concepts you'll meet in all three industries

Disintermediation: cutting out the middleman (e.g. buyers skipping salespeople; AI answers replacing clicks to news sites)
Concentration: a market dominated by a few players (megafunds, model portfolio gatekeepers, holding companies)
Barbell: a market splitting into giants and specialists, with a squeezed middle
Commoditisation: a product becoming interchangeable, so price becomes the only differentiator (passive funds, basic outreach, press releases)
Unit economics: the revenue and cost of one unit (one client, one deal, one hour) that determines profitability
Operating leverage: when costs are mostly fixed, extra revenue is mostly profit, and lost revenue hurts badly
Regulatory arbitrage: structuring a product to fall under lighter rules (e.g. a 5% gate as convention rather than law)
Value capture: who in the chain keeps the profit: the manufacturer, the distributor or the platform

B1 · Private markets for everyone: evergreen and semi-liquid funds

From zero: private equity, private credit and infrastructure were traditionally sold only to institutions through closed-ended "drawdown" funds. The investor commits money, the manager "calls" it over 3–5 years, it's locked up for 10+ years, and cash comes back only when assets are sold. "Democratisation" means repackaging these assets for wealthy individuals, retail investors and pension savers through "evergreen" (no end date) or "semi-liquid" funds. Why? Private markets earn managers far higher fees: PwC estimates about 4× more profit per $bn than traditional funds.

How an evergreen fund works, technically

The crucial detail: in US non-traded BDCs (business development companies, the main retail private credit vehicle), the 5% is a convention, not a law. Managers can restrict redemptions indefinitely. US interval funds must offer periodic repurchases. Unfilled requests generally aren't carried forward and must be resubmitted, which inflates headline "request" numbers.

B2 · Private credit's stress test: the 2026 gating wave

From zero: private credit means funds, rather than banks, lending directly to mid-sized companies, often ones owned by private equity. Loans are usually floating-rate. Retail investors access it through vehicles that value loans at "fair value" and offer limited quarterly exits (B1). In 2026 the theory met reality: many investors asked for their money back at once, and the gates came down.

Blue Owl OBDC IIRedemptions stopped Sept 2025. A merger with a listed fund at ~20% below NAV was announced, then cancelled. Redemptions halted permanently on 18 Feb 2026 and the fund became a wind-down. An investor lawsuit followed.

Bloomberg tallied about $13bn of requests across more than a dozen funds in one quarter, with only about two-thirds paid out.

Why it happened: the "SaaSpocalypse"

The Bank for International Settlements (March 2026) found loans to software (SaaS) companies exceeded $500bn, about 19% of direct lending. Investors feared AI would disrupt those software businesses, and software stocks fell almost 30% between October 2025 and February 2026. Yet BDCs held software loans at an average of about 97.8% of face value (Octus), which raised the question of whether the valuations ("marks") were stale. On the other side, Blue Owl sold $1.4bn of loans at 99.7% of par, evidence the marks were broadly fair.

Technical concept: the listed "second opinion". Some BDCs are listed on an exchange, so the market prices them every day. When listed BDCs trade at a discount to their reported NAV, the market is saying it thinks the loans are worth less than the manager's valuation. Non-traded funds don't get that daily check, which is exactly the valuation-trust problem.
Bull caseThe gates worked as designed: no fire sales. Loans sold near par support the valuations. Floating-rate yields remain attractive, and some funds (e.g. KKR's K-FITS, Blackstone's BREIT) had inflows exceeding outflows.
Bear caseRetail investors didn't understand that 5% is a ceiling, not a promise. Marks near par on AI-threatened software loans look stale. Unfilled requests compound quarter after quarter, and a "semi-liquid" fund became a multi-year wind-down.
1–3 years: more transparency on redemption queues; product redesign (longer notice periods, bigger liquid sleeves, interval funds over BDCs); likely regulatory scrutiny of retail suitability. 5+ years: private credit keeps growing (Preqin forecasts $4.5tn by 2030), but retail wrappers are priced and regulated more like listed closed-end funds.
Quarterly redemption requests vs the 5% cap · listed BDC discounts to NAV · software loan "non-accruals" (loans that stopped paying) · share of income paid in kind (PIK) rather than cash · secondary prices for credit portfolios
Was this a product failure or a communication failure? The structure behaved exactly as the documents said. Investors were surprised anyway. That gap is precisely the client-facing job.
"What's your redemption-request history?" and "What's your software exposure?" are now standard due-diligence questions. Be ready to answer "what happened at Blue Owl, and could it happen here?" with calm facts about structure, not marketing.

B3 · Public meets private: the convergence deals

From zero: "traditional" managers (shares and bonds) have distribution, relationships with advisers, wealth managers and pension schemes, but face falling fees. "Alternative" managers (private markets) have high-fee products but historically lacked access to individual investors. Convergence means combining the two, by buying each other or partnering, to sell blended "public + private" products such as target-date funds or model portfolios with a private-markets sleeve.

B4 · Fee squeeze, active outflows and consolidation

From zero: fees are an annual percentage of assets. Index (passive) funds charge a fraction of what active funds charge. As money moves to passive and to cheaper share classes, the industry earns less per £ managed. That's fee compression. Costs (technology, data, regulation) keep rising, so margins get squeezed, and smaller firms merge or get bought.
0.32%average US fund fee (asset-weighted), from 0.80% in 2006. Passive 0.10%, active 0.57% (Morningstar)
~25%of UK fund assets now in trackers, up from 18% five years earlier (IA)
−£18.9bnUK active equity fund outflows in 2025, while passive equity took in £12.2bn (Calastone)
£54bnoutflows from UK equity funds over a decade (Calastone)

In 2025, passive global equity funds outsold active for the first time since 2015 (Calastone), and £5.8bn went into money market funds as savers sought safety. February 2026 marked the ninth consecutive month of equity fund outflows. Meanwhile, fees on newly launched US mutual funds topped 1% for the first time since 2015. The new launches are specialist products, where pricing power still exists.

B5 · The ETF takeover: active ETFs and ETF share classes

From zero: an ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund listed on a stock exchange and traded during the day like a share. An active ETF uses the same wrapper, but a portfolio manager picks the investments rather than tracking an index. An ETF share class lets a traditional mutual fund add an ETF version of the same portfolio, so a manager can offer ETF access without launching a separate clone fund.

How an ETF stays close to its true value: creation and redemption

$23.1tnglobal ETF assets, end-July 2026, a record (ETFGI)
$2.59tnglobal active ETF assets; $590bn inflows in 7 months vs $323bn a year earlier
$3.97tnEuropean ETF assets, end-Aug 2026, up ~23% this year
39%iShares' share of the European ETF market; J.P. Morgan dominates European active ETFs

The US share-class breakthrough: the SEC granted Dimensional relief on 17 Nov 2025 to add ETF share classes to mutual funds, signalled approval for about 30 more firms in December 2025, and the remaining trading and listing approvals followed by April 2026. Law firm K&L Gates said share-class ETFs were "ready to go live". In Europe, 36 active ETF launches and zero closures in Q1 2026 show US managers piling in.

Bull caseThe active ETF is the "rescue wrapper" for active management: cheaper, transparent, and easy to use in platforms and model portfolios. Share classes let incumbents defend their assets.
Bear caseIn Europe the big US tax advantage of ETFs largely doesn't apply. Active ETFs cannibalise higher-fee funds. The market is concentrated (the top 3 hold ~30% of active ETF assets), and many launches won't reach scale.
1–3 years: European active ETF launches accelerate; US share-class conversions begin, with pressure for UK/European equivalents; model portfolios built increasingly from ETFs. 5+ years: the ETF becomes the default retail wrapper in Europe as platforms and app-based brokers grow.

Expect wealth managers and model-portfolio builders to ask "is there an ETF version?" Understand primary vs secondary market liquidity and bid-offer spreads.

B6 · UK pensions: megafunds, the Mansion House Accord and LGPS pools

From zero: most UK workers save in a DC workplace pension, usually invested in the provider's "default fund". The government believes the market has too many small schemes, focused too much on low cost, and that the 86 council pension funds (the LGPS) are too small. It wants bigger schemes investing more in UK private assets (infrastructure, housing, growing companies). For asset managers, this changes who their clients are.

B8 · Tokenisation and the new fund plumbing

From zero: tokenisation means recording ownership of fund units (or bonds, or cash) on a blockchain-style shared ledger, as a digital "token". The promise: near-instant settlement, 24/7 transfers, lower admin costs, and "programmable" features, e.g. using a tokenised money market fund as collateral. Tokenised money market funds (MMFs) are the first real use case, because they act like interest-earning digital cash.
Traditional fund dealing: investor → platform → transfer agent → fund register, with each step reconciled separately and settlement taking days.

Tokenised / "Direct-to-Fund": the investor's holding is a token on a shared ledger, and the register is the ledger. Fewer intermediaries and faster settlement.
Why it matters: settlement speed, collateral efficiency (a tokenised MMF can be pledged instantly) and cheaper registers. Why it's hard: legal ownership, custody rules, interoperability between different blockchains, and liquidity split across platforms.

B11 · Markets: concentration, the dollar, oil and gilts

From zero: because ~80% of recent revenue growth came from markets (B0), what happens in markets is the business outlook. Four market stories dominate client conversations in 2026. Each connects to a concept from Guide Part A.

1 · US concentration

The top 10 stocks make up roughly 37–41% of the S&P 500, a record above the ~27% dot-com peak. A "global tracker" fund is roughly 70% US and heavily exposed to about 10 technology companies. Concept: diversification (Guide A3). A passive fund can be far less diversified than it looks.

2 · The dollar

The US dollar index fell 10.8% in the first half of 2025, its worst first half since 1973, after the tariff shock ("Sell America"). For a UK investor, a falling dollar shrinks US returns in pounds. Concept: currency risk, and why "hedged share classes" exist.

3 · The 2026 oil shock

The Middle East conflict and Strait of Hormuz disruption cut flows through the strait from ~21.6m to ~4.9m barrels a day (EIA). Brent crude peaked at about $126 in April 2026 and was back above $100 in September. Concept: inflation (Guide A2). Energy shocks raise prices and interest-rate expectations.

4 · UK gilts

The 10-year gilt yield touched 5.295% in September 2026, the highest since 2007. The 30-year passed 6% (single source), the highest since 1998. The Bank of England held rates at 3.75% with CPI at 3.1%. Concept: bond prices fall when yields rise (Guide A2).
Why gilts matter to pension clients: DB pension schemes hold lots of gilts to match future payments (Guide B4, LDI). Higher yields reduce the value placed on their future liabilities, improving funding, but sudden spikes can trigger collateral calls, as in the 2022 LDI crisis. Schemes hold more collateral now, but rapid long-end moves revive that comparison. Fewer DB schemes buying long gilts also means structurally weaker demand for them.

What managers are doing about it

  • Pushing diversification away from the US: international and European equity funds, "equal-weight" products, currency-hedged share classes.
  • Selling "income is back": locking in ~5% gilt yields; money market and short-duration bond funds (£5.8bn into UK MMFs in 2025).
  • Pitching real assets and infrastructure as inflation hedges.
OpportunityHigh yields make bonds attractive for the first time in a decade. Volatility rewards active managers and good advice. Diversified, global and real-asset products have a strong story.
RiskA stagflationary energy shock plus a fiscal risk premium on gilts. Passive trackers concentrated in a few US stocks. Industry revenue is highly exposed to a market fall.
The Bank of England's next rate decisions · Brent crude · top-10 weight of the S&P 500 · the dollar index · gilt auction demand ("cover ratios") · the market's reaction to the UK Budget
Be able to explain in plain English: why a global tracker is a concentrated US tech bet; what a 6% 30-year gilt means for a pension client; and the case for diversifiers. That's real client conversation material.

C1 · AI SDRs and the deliverability squeeze

From zero: an SDR (sales development rep) is the entry-level seller who finds prospects, contacts them cold and books meetings for senior sellers (AEs, account executives). An "AI SDR" is software built on a large language model that tries to do that whole loop itself: pull a target list, research each company, write personalised emails, send follow-ups, read replies and book meetings. "Agentic" means it plans and carries out several steps on its own, rather than just drafting text for a human.

The catch is deliverability. AI SDR economics depend on cheap volume, and the email providers have shut that door. My take: the evidence points to augmentation beating automation in outbound sales. The firms getting value use AI to make a human far better prepared, not to replace the human with volume. Ask yourself: if every competitor can send 10,000 "personalised" emails, what becomes scarce? (Genuine relevance and trust.)
The cold-email volume game is closing. Your edge is judgement: choosing which accounts and signals matter, writing like a human, and checking what the agents produce. Learn SPF/DKIM/DMARC and CRM hygiene, and you become the person who runs the tools.

The mechanics that change a seller's job

D1 · AI answer engines: earned media gets a second audience

From zero: people increasingly ask an AI (ChatGPT, Google's AI Overviews, Perplexity, Claude, Gemini) instead of clicking through search results. The AI writes one answer, sometimes with small source links. Whatever the AI "reads" shapes what it says about your company. It turns out that PR's traditional product, coverage in credible outlets, is one of the main things these systems read. Earned media now informs machines as well as people.

How AI answers are built: retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

1 · Fan-outThe model rewrites your question into several search queries
›
2 · RetrievePulls candidate pages from a web index or licensed content
›
3 · RankScores them on relevance, authority and freshness
›
4 · GenerateWrites an answer from those passages, citing some sources

There are two routes into an AI's answer. Training memory (what the model learned) is slow to change. Live retrieval (what it fetches now) can be influenced. Retrieval favours well-structured, quotable, recent pages on trusted domains. GEO (generative engine optimisation) comes from a 2023 academic paper, which found content changes such as adding citations, quotations and statistics could boost visibility in AI answers by up to 40%. A new metric, "share of model", measures how often a brand appears across a fixed set of AI prompts compared with competitors.

~82–85%of AI citations come from earned media (Muck Rack "What Is AI Reading?", 2025)
5×growth in press-release citations by AI, July–Dec 2025 (Muck Rack)
8% vs 15%click-through when Google shows an AI summary vs when it doesn't (Pew, 2025)
900mChatGPT weekly users (Feb 2026)

Market signals: Adobe agreed to buy Semrush for $1.9bn (Nov 2025); "AI visibility" start-ups raised large rounds; Bing added GEO to its guidelines (Feb 2026). The UK competition regulator (CMA) gave Google Search "strategic market status" (Oct 2025), with AI Overviews in scope. Note the caveats: Muck Rack sells PR software, and its "earned" category is broad. Different AI models also favour different outlets: The Guardian is cited most by ChatGPT, Yahoo Finance by Claude.

Bear case"GEO" is just SEO rebadged (Google says so). AI results are unstable: platforms disagree on 61.9% of recommendations. And if AI answers mean nobody clicks, the outlets that supply citations lose money and shrink, sawing off the branch PR sits on.
1–3 years: "AI visibility audits" become a standard agency deliverable; press releases rewritten as structured, fact-dense, machine-readable pages; "share of model" added beside share of voice. 5+ years: AI agents research and buy on people's behalf, and reputation becomes partly a data-supply problem.

D4 · Deepfakes, synthetic media and proving what's real

From zero: AI can now convincingly fake a CEO's face and voice. Criminals use this to steal money, and bad actors use it to start crises. And when anything could be fake, real evidence can be dismissed as fake too: the "liar's dividend". Companies need ways to prove what's genuine, and crisis plans for when a fake goes viral.

The reference case: Arup (2024)

An employee at the UK engineering firm's Hong Kong office joined a video call where the "CFO" and several colleagues were all deepfakes. They made 15 transfers totalling about $25.6m. It's still the case every crisis team cites.

The scale (Resemble AI)

More than $1.28bn in deepfake fraud losses in 2025. In the corporate section: 41 incidents, $74.9m documented, the largest single loss $35m. Over 80% of incidents didn't disclose losses at all.

How content provenance works: C2PA "Content Credentials"

1 · CaptureCamera or app creates a "manifest": who made it, with what tool, what edits, whether AI was used
›
2 · HashA cryptographic fingerprint of the content is recorded
›
3 · SignThe manifest is signed with a private key, often in secure hardware
›
4 · VerifyAnyone can recompute the fingerprint. Any change breaks the match ("tamper-evident").

Limits: many platforms strip this metadata on upload; credentials prove origin, not truth; and adoption is patchy. Google's Pixel 10 reportedly signs all photos by default, while Apple's iOS 26 can view credentials but doesn't create them at capture. The EU AI Act's transparency duties apply from August 2026.

UK regulation in action: Ofcom opened a formal Online Safety Act investigation into X (January 2026) over its Grok AI being used to create sexualised "undressed" images. Potential fines reach 10% of global revenue.

Bull caseProvenance standards, verified owned channels and pre-registered "authentic" spokesperson content will make debunking fast. Technology plus speed can solve these crises.
Bear caseDetection is an arms race, and most people never check credentials. The faster threat may be AI systems confidently repeating falsehoods (D1) rather than fake videos.
1–3 years: crisis plans add deepfake scenarios, verification protocols (call-back codes, finance controls) and "pre-bunking" (warning audiences before fakes appear). Content credentials appear on official corporate imagery. 5+ years: corporate communications authenticated by default.
Ofcom's decision on X · EU AI Act enforcement · whether iPhones create Content Credentials at capture · annual deepfake loss data
Add verification to your crisis basics (holding statements, escalation trees, dark sites): reverse image search, checking Content Credentials, open-source intelligence. Know the Online Safety Act and Ofcom's role.

From zero: agencies traditionally sold hours, many of them junior hours spent on media lists, monitoring, coverage reports and first drafts (Guide D7). AI now does much of that in minutes. Meanwhile the big advertising holding companies that own famous PR brands are merging, cutting costs or selling. Clients ask: why pay for hours AI saves?

Holding-company shake-up

GroupWhat happened
Omnicom + IPGMerger completed November 2025, creating the world's largest ad holding company. PR networks combined into Omnicom Public Relations; Golin and Ketchum merged (Feb 2026); Porter Novelli folded into FleishmanHillard. Famous brands disappear.
WPPA £500m savings plan to 2028; ~11,000 jobs lost since early 2025, and up to 1,000 more (FT, Sep 2026). Reportedly hired Goldman Sachs to explore selling Burson (not confirmed). Precedent: KKR bought a majority of FGS Global from WPP (~$1.7bn, 2024).
The ironyBurson won the 2026 Cannes PR Lions Grand Prix ("The KitKat Heist") and PR Agency of the Year while its owner considered selling it.

The pricing problem, in one calculation

The average US agency billing rate is about $278/hour (PR Council 2025). If AI compresses a 40-hour task into 4 hours and the client price stays the same, the implied rate exceeds $2,700/hour on the remaining time, and one commentator calculates $7,000+ on certain tasks. Either prices fall, or agencies switch to value-based or outcome pricing. This is the same shift happening in software sales (Part C5).

The junior squeeze: one UK dataset showed graduate vacancies down ~46% year on year (Adzuna, July 2026, unverified), and the Big Four cut graduate intakes. A CIPR Influence article (June 2026) warned "the bottom rungs of the career ladder have been kicked away". No PR-specific junior data was found.

Bull caseFees are still growing, and revenue per head is a record. Strategic advisory firms (Brunswick, FGS, Teneo) are thriving. AI frees people for strategy, and private equity money is circling the sector.
Bear caseUK firms "run to stand still". Holding companies treat PR as non-core and sell it. Storied brands are erased, the billable hour is deflating, and the junior pyramid that trained the profession is collapsing.
1–3 years: Burson's fate; agencies "productise" AI services (GEO audits, AI monitoring) and shift to retainers or outcome fees; fewer, more technical junior hires. 5+ years: a barbell of top-end strategic and financial advisory firms plus tech-enabled platforms, with a squeezed generalist middle. That's the same shape predicted for asset management (B3).
Entry roles will expect AI fluency from day one. Your value is judgement, writing, relationships and data, not list-building. Target firms by direction of travel: advisory boutiques, financial PR, or tech-enabled agencies.

D8 · Financial and corporate PR: IPOs, activists and legal claims

From zero: financial PR (IPOs, results, takeovers, activist fights) is the best-paid and most regulated corner of PR (Guide D6). Three big stories: London's listings drought and tentative revival, record shareholder activism (with the UK the most-targeted market in Europe), and new UK rules that turn marketing and green claims into legal risk, not just reputational risk.

London IPOs

2025 was the weakest year for London listings in more than 35 years: only £184m raised in nine months, against ~£17bn in 2021. CRH, Flutter and Wise moved their main listings to New York, and delistings have outnumbered listings every year since 2022. Signs of a thaw: H1 2026 saw 7 IPOs raising £577m, up 215% (EY), helped by a stamp-duty holiday on new listings and simpler FCA listing rules. The Iran war paused plans earlier in the year.

Record activism

255 global activist campaigns in 2025, a record (Barclays). Elliott was the most active, with 18 campaigns and ~$20bn deployed, including BP. 61% of Q4 campaigns pushed for M&A. The UK was Europe's most-targeted market, with record US activist pressure. See also Saba vs UK investment trusts (B12).

Claims become legal exposure

RuleWhat it means for communications
DMCC Act (CMA powers live April 2025)The CMA can now fine directly for misleading consumer practices, up to 10% of global turnover. First substantive fine: the AA, £4.2m for "drip pricing" (April 2026), plus refunds to 80,000+ customers. StubHub was fined £889k, and 100 firms received advisory letters.
Green claimsThe CMA's Green Claims Code and, for financial firms, the FCA anti-greenwashing rule. Every sustainability claim must be evidenced.
UK Sustainability Reporting StandardsFinal UK SRS (based on the global ISSB standards) published February 2026. The FCA consulted on requiring them for listed companies from 2027.
Market abuse (UK MAR)Inside information must be released properly via RNS. Leaks or selective briefing are illegal.

Tariff communications: "tariff" appeared on 238 S&P 500 earnings calls in Q3 2025, a third fewer than the previous quarter, as executives hedged their language to avoid political reaction. Some firms withdrew financial guidance altogether. Choosing how much to say about political risk is now a core financial-PR judgement.

Bull caseListing reforms and the 2026 pickup build an IPO pipeline. Record activism and M&A keep financial PR busy, and sustainability reporting creates new work.
Bear caseWeak post-IPO performance (2025's top five IPOs were down ~26% on average), New York's pull, and geopolitics keep closing IPO windows. Greenhushing becomes rational under the threat of fines.
1–3 years: late 2026–27 tests whether the London IPO rebound is real; UK SRS becomes mandatory for listed firms; more CMA fines, possibly the first green-claims fine. 5+ years: combined financial and sustainability narratives become audited territory, and "claims compliance" becomes a core PR skill.
Learn the basics of inside information, how an RNS works, takeover-code timing and how to substantiate a claim. This links directly to your asset management experience: the same discipline of accuracy applies.

Inwards: employees are the most trusting audience (78% trust their employer), but they also leak, post and organise, and AI restructuring, layoffs and return-to-office rules all need careful messaging. "Corporate diplomacy" means communicating through tariffs, sanctions and conflicts where governments respond publicly to corporate decisions (see Amazon, D3). No neutral position exists.

Awards as signals: what the industry rewards

Cannes 2025 PR Grand Prix: "Lucky Yatra" (FCB India, Mumbai railways). Every train ticket became a lottery entry, to reduce fare-dodging. The jury rewarded redesigning a service to solve a business problem, not a stunt.
Cannes 2026 PR Grand Prix: "The KitKat Heist" (Burson London + VML). After a real theft of ~413,793 KitKat bars (about 12 tonnes) between Italy and Poland, Nestlé responded with humour around "have a break", reassured customers there were no safety or supply concerns, and invited the public to trace stolen batches. Fast, witty crisis judgement.
Bull caseInternal comms and public affairs gain a seat at the top table during AI transformation and geopolitical upheaval. Employees are the best ambassadors.
Bear caseTeams are tiny and unfunded, and information overload is rising. AI layoffs strain the "trusted employer" premium. Any geopolitical statement can trigger a government backlash.
1–3 years: AI-transformation change communication becomes the dominant internal brief, and internal and external messages must be identical (assume everything leaks). Scenario-based geopolitical risk communication becomes standard. 5+ years: internal and external comms converge into one "stakeholder communications" function.
Internal comms is an under-supplied, growing specialism and a strong entry route. Public affairs is another. Read the FT daily and learn to trace how a policy shock moves through costs, pricing and messaging. The KitKat Heist is a perfect case to discuss in an interview.
