# 1. FINDINGS

## 1.1 The seat itself: what "client portfolio manager / product specialist / investment director" means in private credit

Across every live posting checked (Blackstone, Barings, Fortress, KKR, Apollo, Goldman Sachs Asset Management, BlackRock), the job is the same shape even when the title differs: sit between the investment team that originates and manages loans and the people who give the firm money (institutions, consultants, private banks, wealth platforms), and translate one language into the other, continuously, in writing and in meetings.

Barings advertises the role literally as "Client Portfolio Manager (Private Credit, Senior Associate / Associate Director)" in London. Its duties, read directly from the posting [P342]:
- "supporting business development liaison for Barings' European Private Credit Capabilities," working across "Global and European Direct Lending capabilities"
- communicating investment strategy to grow assets under management in "regional private debt funds"
- giving "scheduled and ad-hoc updates on all aspects of the Private Credit market," including "investment performance, market levels etc"
- monitoring competitor activity and market trends
- supporting client meetings and building **RFPs and DDQs** (request-for-proposal responses and due-diligence questionnaires)
- creating and overseeing marketing collateral: "fact sheets and pitch books"
- "contribut[ing] to and review[ing] reporting materials for existing clients and respond[ing] to client reporting inquiries on a timely basis"
- contributing to "Thought Leadership materials" with marketing and investment staff

Required background: a finance/economics degree, "3-5 years professional experience," ideally with private credit or direct lending exposure, and "CFA or CAIA, or a willingness to seek the designation desired" [P342].

Blackstone runs the same function at several levels inside "Private Wealth Solutions," a division built to sell Blackstone's institutional strategies down into private banks and wealth platforms. Its Institutional Client Solutions (ICS) Credit Sales Associate posting describes the associate as someone who will "partner with Senior Relationship Managers to help execute on client servicing and business development initiatives": building the new-business pipeline, preparing client and consultant roadshows, running the CRM (Salesforce), coordinating institutional and consultant requests, and tracking indications of interest (IOIs) across asset classes [P343]. It wants "3+ years' experience, preferably within Investment Banking, Investor relations or Alternative Credit Management," Series 7/79/63 licences, and pays "$105,000 - $175,000" base [P343]. A more senior, adjacent Blackstone "Product Specialist, Vice President" posting (Real Assets rather than Credit, but structurally identical to the Credit product-specialist ladder) shows what the job looks like with 7+ years' experience: developing sales strategy with the investment team, giving "real-time updates on products, investments, and markets" to advisors and portfolio managers, writing "monthly commentary, quarterly reviews, and investment activity updates" for private-wealth channels, joining client and prospective-client meetings to discuss "portfolio performance and market commentary," and supervising junior product specialists; base pay "$175,000 - $200,000" [P344].

Fortress Investment Group's "Director, Private Credit Product Specialist, Private Wealth Solutions" posting asks for someone with 8-15 years' experience "in private credit, preferably in a product specialist, investor relations, or portfolio management role," who has "deep understanding of private credit markets and fund structures" and can build "fluency in private credit strategies including direct lending, real estate credit, net lease, asset-based finance, CLOs, and opportunistic credit" — i.e. the job spans several sub-strategies, not just one fund. It explicitly wants someone who can "act as a conduit between portfolio managers and distribution teams" and sit in "investment committee and deal pipeline meetings" [P345].

KKR runs a comparable seat inside its Client and Partner Group (CPG) for private wealth: a "Product Specialist / Client Portfolio Manager" whose job includes "conducting portfolio data analyses to provide product insight and investor messaging on deal origination activity, private market/credit trends, portfolio holdings and performance attribution" [P346]. Separately, KKR's Credit Solutions Group hires investment-side associates who "source and underwrite private credit investment opportunities" [P347] — useful as a contrast: that is the investment seat, not the client-facing one.

Goldman Sachs Asset Management runs a "Client Solutions – Private Credit (Hybrid Capital)" seat inside its Alternatives Capital Formation function [P349], and BlackRock is separately hiring a "Senior Product Manager, Private Markets, Portfolio Management" to build out Aladdin's private-credit portfolio tooling for its own PM teams [P350] — a reminder that "product" in private markets covers both client-facing product specialists and internal technology/data product roles; the internship candidate should be clear which one they mean.

The internship this file is written for — Fidelity International's "Investment Directing Summer Internship" — sits on exactly this seat, one rung down. Per the listing: interns "support investment directing and investment specialists by preparing core marketing presentations and reporting materials to support sales activities," work "with internal stakeholders to source key data and obtain the latest internal views," and gain exposure to "fund and market analysis, client reporting," working alongside "investment specialist teams, fund analysts, Portfolio Managers, and local sales and marketing professionals" [P351]. That is the same three activities as every posting above: reporting, marketing/positioning material, and being the fluent go-between for portfolio managers and distribution.

## 1.2 What is actually different about reporting when there is no daily price

A public equities client portfolio manager reports against a closing price that the market sets every day, for free, verifiable by anyone. A private credit product specialist reports against a number the manager itself calculated, usually once a quarter (semi-liquid vehicles do it monthly), because there is no exchange on which a $30m unitranche loan to a mid-market software company trades.

That NAV is built from a "fair value" exercise: model the loan (discounted cash flow off a comparable yield/spread, sometimes cross-checked by a third-party valuation firm), mark it, and that mark becomes the number in the client's statement. This is the single hardest thing to explain to a beginner and the single most important thing a product specialist has to be fluent in, because the honest answer to "is my investment up or down this quarter" is "the manager decided it was."

A quarterly investor letter in this world typically contains, based on the disclosure themes that both the Barings and Blackstone postings describe compiling: NAV per unit/share and the change on the quarter; portfolio composition by seniority (first lien, unitranche, second lien, mezzanine) and by sector/geography; weighted-average yield or spread over the reference rate (SOFR/SONIA); leverage, both at the fund level and inside the underlying portfolio companies; a watch list of underperforming credits and any names on non-accrual (not paying cash interest); how much of the quarter's income was cash versus PIK (payment-in-kind, added to principal rather than paid — already established in this project that PIK ran about 5.9% of BDC income in 2023 and roughly 8% in 2026, a trend line every product specialist in this seat should be able to explain); realized gains/losses versus unrealized marks; and how much capital is still committed-but-undrawn ("dry powder") versus deployed.

Due diligence questions institutional investors ask (the DDQ the Barings posting has the CPM help build) [P342] cluster around: valuation policy and whether an independent third party checks the marks; use of leverage, including subscription lines that draw on a bank facility rather than calling LP capital (which can flatter early IRR — see 1.3); related-party or affiliate transactions; concentration limits (how much of the fund can sit in one borrower or sector); key-person provisions; side letters given to large investors; fee structure and the GP's own co-investment (alignment); and, increasingly since 2026, exactly what the redemption terms are in a semi-liquid vehicle and what happens if requests exceed the cap.

A capital call (also "drawdown notice") is a formal notice from the GP to a limited partner requiring it to wire a specified portion of its already-committed-but-not-yet-invested capital, usually within 5-10 business days, to fund a named investment or pay fees/expenses. A distribution notice is the mirror image: a notice that cash (or, rarely, securities in kind) is being paid out to LPs, and it will specify whether that payment is a return of capital, income, or a capital gain — a distinction that matters both for tax and for how an LP should read their own DPI (see below).

## 1.3 Why this seat is harder, and more important, than the equivalent job in listed equities

A public-markets client portfolio manager reports a time-weighted return: the market marks the security every second the exchange is open, so "up 8% this year" is a fact anyone can check on a screen. None of that machinery exists in private credit, which forces the industry to use a different vocabulary — and forces the product specialist to be the one who can translate it under pressure. Precise, plain definitions, per ILPA's own glossary [P352] and standard industry usage:

- **IRR (Internal Rate of Return)**: the single annualised discount rate that makes the present value of every cash flow in and out of the investment — capital calls (negative), distributions (positive), and the remaining unrealised value (treated as a final positive cash flow) — net to zero. It is a *money-weighted* return: it rewards getting cash back quickly, not just getting a lot of it back eventually.
- **MOIC (Multiple on Invested Capital)**: total value created (cash already distributed plus the current fair value of what is left) divided by the capital actually invested. It is a multiple, not a rate — it ignores how long the money was tied up. A 2.0x MOIC over 2 years and a 2.0x MOIC over 8 years are very different outcomes, and MOIC alone cannot tell you which is which.
- **DPI (Distributions to Paid-In capital)**: cumulative cash (or in-kind value) actually paid out to investors, divided by cumulative capital they have put in. This is the "cash in your pocket" number — the only one of the five that cannot be revised by a valuation committee, because it is realised.
- **RVPI (Residual Value to Paid-In capital)**: the fair value of everything still held in the fund, divided by cumulative paid-in capital. This is the part that *is* a mark, not a fact.
- **TVPI (Total Value to Paid-In capital)**: DPI + RVPI — realised plus unrealised, the "total scorecard." A fund can show an impressive TVPI almost entirely built from RVPI (i.e. still just a mark) in its early years; a seasoned product specialist flags that distinction unprompted rather than letting a client assume TVPI means "cash I could get today."
- **Vintage year**: the year a fund holds its first close and starts investing (sometimes the year of legal formation). It matters because a fund's return is shaped as much by the pricing and competitive environment it invested into as by manager skill — a 2020-vintage direct lending fund and a 2026-vintage one bought loans at very different spreads over the reference rate, so comparing their IRRs at the same point in their life without adjusting for vintage is close to meaningless. This is why every serious performance benchmark (Cliffwater, Burgiss/MSCI, Preqin) is cut by vintage year.
- **J-curve**: the shape of a fund's *net* IRR over its life, per ILPA, "plotting the returns generated by a private equity fund against time (from inception to termination)" [P352] — early on it typically dips because fees and expenses are charged against called capital before any value has been created or realised, then rises as investments mature, generate income, and are eventually repaid or sold. In private credit specifically, the dip is shallower and shorter than in private equity because loans throw off cash interest from day one, rather than requiring a multi-year hold before any income or exit exists.

Put together, these are why the seat is genuinely harder than its listed-equity counterpart: there is no external price to fall back on, so the manager's own valuation policy *is* the number a client is trusting; the standard performance vocabulary (IRR/MOIC/DPI/RVPI/TVPI) has no single-figure "return" a client can sanity-check against a benchmark index the way they could with an equity fund's total return; commitments, calls and distributions require the product specialist to keep straight what a client has promised, what has actually moved, and what tax character that movement has; and, since 2026, semi-liquid vehicles add a further layer — a client can hold something the statement calls "monthly liquidity" and still be told their redemption is only partially honoured this quarter, a genuinely counter-intuitive outcome that only a confident explainer prevents from becoming a complaint or a redemption panic (see 1.2 and the beginner section below for the 2026 gating episodes already established in this project).

## 1.4 Skills, qualifications and typical backgrounds

Reading straight across the postings gathered for this file, three things are consistent. First, the entry point into a *junior* client-facing private-credit seat (Barings' Senior Associate/Associate Director CPM, Blackstone's ICS Sales Associate) asks for only 3-5 years of prior experience, a finance-adjacent degree, and treats the CFA/CAIA as desirable rather than mandatory: Barings wants "CFA or CAIA, or a willingness to seek the designation" [P342]; Blackstone's associate-level posting wants "3+ years' experience, preferably within Investment Banking, Investor relations or Alternative Credit Management" plus Series 7/79/63 licences, with no charter required at all [P343]. Second, at the senior end (Fortress's Director-level product specialist, Blackstone's VP-level product specialist) the bar rises to 7-15 years and the CFA becomes "a plus" rather than a requirement even there: Fortress asks for "8-15 years of experience in private credit, preferably in a product specialist, investor relations, or portfolio management role... Bachelor's degree required with MBA or CFA a plus" [P345]; Blackstone's VP posting wants "7+ years" and says an "MBA preferred but not required; CFA also valued" [P344]. Third, prior banking, investor-relations, or credit-analysis experience is treated as at least as valuable as a charter — none of the postings read this session required a CFA outright, all of them wanted direct exposure to credit markets or institutional client relationships first.

The publicly reported career paths of people who now hold senior versions of this seat back this up. Robert Rdzak spent eight years at Barings in consultant relations, rising to Managing Director, before moving across to Nuveen Private Capital as MD, Consultant Relations [P359] — a lateral move between two large credit managers' distribution functions rather than a move up from a junior analyst role inside one firm. Public hiring announcements around Schroders Capital's private-debt build-out (see Gap note on P360, read only at snippet level) describe new senior hires arriving from a "Strategy Specialist" role in another manager's Client Partnership Group, from a credit-team MD role at another asset manager, and from a long (almost two-decade) run at a large fixed-income shop — again, moves *within* the client-facing/product-specialist track across firms, not moves in from research or trading desks. The pattern that emerges: this is a track with its own ladder (analyst/associate CPM → product specialist → director/MD in consultant relations or client solutions), most easily entered either straight out of a finance degree at the junior end, or laterally from investment banking, investor relations, or a credit-analysis seat, with the CFA (or CAIA) functioning as a credibility signal and, per Barings, sometimes something the employer is happy to sponsor "a willingness to seek" rather than already hold [P342].

Two professional bodies now offer purpose-built, self-paced credentials rather than requiring the full multi-year charter route. CFA Institute's Private Markets and Alternative Investments Certificate is a five-course, "fully online and self-paced" program (roughly 70-90 hours total) whose second course is explicitly "Private Equity and Private Credit Investments: Fund structures, value creation, risks, and core strategies," assessed by a single unproctored 60-question, 90-minute exam with two attempts allowed [P353] — aimed, per the FAQ, at "early-career professionals aspiring to enter private markets" and "investment and client-facing professionals needing industry understanding." CAIA's Private Debt microcredential is shorter still — "entirely online," five hours across four modules (Fundamentals; Corporate Lending covering secured lending, mezzanine and venture debt; Asset-Based Lending; Portfolio Implementation), costing $395 individually and carrying continuing-education credit for CFP/CIRO/Investments & Wealth Institute members [P354]. Neither is a substitute for the full CFA charter or the CAIA designation, but both are exactly the kind of concrete, nameable credential a penultimate-year student could realistically start or complete before an internship interview, and naming one specifically is a stronger signal than saying "I've read about private credit."

## 1.5 What to read and follow

The trade body to know by name is the **Alternative Credit Council (ACC)**, AIMA's dedicated arm for "the private credit and direct lending space" [P355]. Its flagship annual publication, **Financing the Economy**, is described on the ACC's own hub as having "become a key industry reference point, providing investors and policymakers with data and insight on the trends underpinning the growth of private credit into a globally recognised asset class," with the most recent edition dated 2025 on that page [P356]. The same hub lists a running **ACC Private Credit Quarterly Market Update** (latest referenced: Q1 2026), a **Borrower's Guide to Private Credit: UK Edition**, a first dedicated **Private Credit in Asia 2025** research piece, and a **Financing the Economy podcast** already past its 22nd episode [P356] — all free, all a realistic reading/listening list for a candidate over a few weeks.

On the ratings/data-provider side, **KBRA** runs a dedicated private-credit hub publishing free commentary alongside its rated deal coverage; its own page states it "performed ~4,300 underlying credit assessments on middle market sponsor-backed companies in 1H 2026 LTM" and that its "assessment portfolio of European Union (EU)- and UK-based middle market (MM) companies has grown more than fivefold since 2022" [P357] — concrete evidence of how fast third-party credit assessment of private, unrated loans has scaled. This project could not, this session, successfully reload live pages for **Cliffwater** (its Direct Lending Index is the most widely cited free benchmark for US middle-market direct lending returns), **Lincoln International** (publishes a quarterly Senior Debt Index), **Proskauer** (publishes the quarterly Private Credit Default Index already used elsewhere in this project), or **PitchBook LCD** — each returned a 403/404 this session and the search budget ran out before a corrected URL could be found; see Gaps. The same applies to the subscription trade press most practitioners read daily — **Private Debt Investor**, **9fin**, **Creditflux**, **Octus** (formerly Reorg) and **Debtwire** — none of which were successfully opened this session. A candidate preparing for interview should still be able to name all of these as "the trade press and data providers people in this seat read," because that list itself (which outlets exist, and that most of the deep data sits behind paywalls a junior would only get through a Bloomberg/desk terminal) is a legitimate, checkable claim, even where this file could not fetch and quote their current content.

On the standard-setting side, **ILPA** (Institutional Limited Partners Association) is the reference every product specialist implicitly works against: its glossary is where the plain-English definitions of IRR, MOIC, DPI, RVPI, TVPI, capital call, distribution, commitment and the J-curve used throughout this file come from [P352], and ILPA's broader reporting-template guidelines are what many institutional LPs now expect a fund's quarterly reporting to follow.

On a single introductory book: general professional knowledge (not verified by a fresh fetch this session — see Gaps) points to Stephen L. Nesbitt's *Private Debt: Opportunities in Corporate Direct Lending* (Wiley Finance) as the most commonly recommended accessible introduction, written by the founder of Cliffwater. This should be confirmed against a bookseller or publisher listing before being quoted as settled fact in the final deliverable.

## 1.6 Compensation and career path

Public, disclosed numbers at this seat are thin, and mostly exist only because of US state pay-transparency laws that force employers to publish a range on senior New York postings — not because the industry publishes them voluntarily. What is solid: Blackstone's ICS Credit Sales Associate role (3+ years' experience) discloses "$105,000 - $175,000" [P343]; Blackstone's Product Specialist VP role (7+ years, adjacent Real Assets strategy on the same ladder as Credit) discloses "$175,000 - $200,000" [P344]. Neither of the UK postings read this session (Barings' CPM, Legal & General's private-credit portfolio-monitoring role) disclosed any figure at all — the UK has no equivalent transparency mandate, so a UK-based version of this seat is simply less visible from outside. One vendor careers blog gives broader, non-product-specialist-specific bands across the wider private-credit function — roughly $120k-$200k at analyst level rising to $700k-$2m+ at MD/partner level, explicitly flagging that "CFA and CAIA designations" are "valued in private credit hiring" [P361] — but this is a single marketing blog, not a compensation survey, and should be treated as directional at best, not quoted as fact.

The career path itself, so far as it can be reconstructed from postings and hiring announcements, runs: graduate/junior analyst or associate in a client-facing or investor-relations seat (3-5 years, degree required, CFA/CAIA optional) → product specialist or senior CPM (5-10 years, often a lateral hire from investor relations, banking, or another manager's equivalent seat) → director/MD in consultant relations, client solutions, or product specialism (8-15+ years, credential increasingly "valued" but still not universally required). The lateral movement observed in the two hiring stories read this session (Barings → Nuveen; various firms → Schroders Capital) suggests firms recruit *within* this specialism as much as promote up through it — reputation and existing institutional/consultant relationships appear to travel with the person.

## 1.7 How to talk about wanting this specialism, and how an interviewer tests it

A graduate can credibly say something like: "I'm drawn to the client-facing side of private credit because it sits at the point where a manager's investment view has to be translated into something a client can actually act on and trust — and because there's no daily price to hide behind, that translation has to be more rigorous, not less." What a graduate cannot credibly claim is direct experience valuing an illiquid loan, having sat in an LP due-diligence meeting, or having a settled view on whether current private-credit spreads are attractive — those require the job itself.

The following are the kind of questions an interviewer would actually use to separate "read one article" from "understands the mechanics." These are research notes on what a good answer contains, not scripted answers:

1. **"Walk me through the difference between IRR and MOIC using a simple example."** Good answer: uses two contrasting hypothetical funds (one fast/small multiple, one slow/big multiple) to show IRR rewards speed and MOIC rewards size; explicitly says IRR is money-weighted and time-value-sensitive, MOIC is not.
2. **"What's a J-curve, and why is it typically shallower in private credit than in private equity?"** Good answer: explains fees drag early net IRR down before value is created; then contrasts credit's day-one cash coupon income against private equity's income-free hold period.
3. **"A fund reports a great quarter. How do you tell whether that's realised or just a valuation mark?"** Good answer: reaches for DPI (realised, cash) versus RVPI (unrealised mark) and notes TVPI blends both, so a strong TVPI early in a fund's life can be almost entirely RVPI.
4. **"A private wealth client calls panicking because a semi-liquid fund gated redemptions. What do you tell them?"** Good answer: explains gates/caps are a pre-disclosed, contractual liquidity-management mechanism (references 2026 precedents), distinguishes a liquidity queue from a loss or a default, and stresses this should have been explained *before* the client invested.
5. **"What is a capital call, and what happens if an LP fails to fund one?"** Good answer: defines the drawdown-notice mechanic and notes real consequences (default interest, forfeiture/dilution of the LP's interest, GP remedies), and can mention that subscription credit lines are sometimes used to delay calls.
6. **"What's the difference between a distribution that's return of capital and one that's income?"** Good answer: connects the distinction to tax treatment and to how DPI should be interpreted going forward.
7. **"How do you value a loan that never trades?"** Good answer: describes a fair-value/discounted cash flow approach benchmarked to comparable market yields/spreads, mentions third-party valuation agents and quarterly valuation committees, and is comfortable saying "it's a modelled estimate, not a market price."
8. **"What would you expect to see in an institutional investor's due-diligence questionnaire before they commit to a private credit fund?"** Good answer: names valuation policy/independent checks, leverage use including subscription lines, key-person provisions, related-party transactions, concentration limits, and non-accrual/PIK disclosure.
9. **"Explain the difference between a unitranche loan, a first-lien loan, a second-lien loan, and mezzanine debt."** Good answer: orders them correctly by seniority/priority of claim and connects seniority to expected yield.
10. **"Why would a company choose a private credit loan over a broadly syndicated leveraged loan or a high-yield bond?"** Good answer: speed and certainty of execution, confidentiality, covenant flexibility, avoiding public market pricing volatility and ratings requirements.
11. **"What is PIK income, and why do investors watch it closely?"** Good answer: defines payment-in-kind interest (added to principal rather than paid in cash) and explains rising PIK as a possible early signal of borrower stress, ideally citing the trend already established in this project (PIK roughly 5.9% of BDC income in 2023, rising to about 8% by 2026).
12. **"What's the 'denominator effect,' and why did it matter for private credit allocators?"** Good answer: when public markets fall and private marks lag, private holdings become a mechanically larger share of a fixed portfolio, forcing institutions to pause new commitments even if they still like the asset class.
13. **"How would you explain 'dry powder' to a client, and why can too much of it be a problem?"** Good answer: distinguishes AUM/committed capital from capital actually deployed, and explains that a large uninvested pile can pressure managers into looser underwriting or tighter spreads to get money out the door.
14. **"Why does vintage year matter when comparing two funds' performance?"** Good answer: ties entry pricing/competitive conditions at the time of investing to eventual returns, and explains why benchmarks are cut by vintage rather than compared in absolute terms across different start years.
15. **"If you were drafting a quarterly investor letter for a private credit fund, what would it need to contain?"** Good answer: NAV per unit and its change; portfolio composition by seniority/sector/geography; weighted-average yield/spread and leverage; a watch list and non-accrual names; cash versus PIK income; realised versus unrealised activity; and dry powder/pipeline commentary — i.e., recreates the structure described in 1.2 above from first principles.

# 6. WHAT A BEGINNER MUST UNDERSTAND FROM THIS

**1. In private credit, the report you get every quarter is not a photograph of a market price — it is the manager's own opinion of value, checked but not overruled by an outside referee.** Best worked example: a public-equities CPM says "your Apple shares closed at $X yesterday, verifiable by anyone." A private credit product specialist says "our team's fair-value model puts your loan to Company Y at $Z this quarter" — a Level 3 mark. That single sentence is the reason this seat exists: someone credible has to stand behind that number to a client who cannot check it themselves, and explain the process (DCF off comparable yields, sometimes a third-party valuation agent) behind it.

**2. IRR and MOIC answer different questions, and mixing them up is the most common beginner mistake.** Worked example: Fund A returns your $1 as $1.30 after 6 months (fast); Fund B returns your $1 as $2.00 after 5 years (slow but bigger). Fund A's IRR is roughly 90%+ annualized; Fund B's is closer to 15%. But Fund B made you twice your money (2.0x MOIC) while Fund A only made you 1.3x. Neither number is "the return" on its own — IRR captures speed, MOIC captures size, and a good product specialist quotes both, plus DPI (how much of that has actually been paid out in cash) versus RVPI (how much is still just a mark on paper). TVPI = DPI + RVPI is the "total scorecard," realized plus unrealized.

**3. Capital calls and distributions are not deposits and withdrawals — they are a drip-feed with legal teeth.** Worked example: an investor "commits" $10m to a fund at close but might only ever have $6-7m actually drawn ("called") at any one time; the rest sits as an unfunded, contingent obligation the investor must be ready to fund on 5-10 business days' notice whenever the GP sends a capital call for a new loan or for fees. When the GP later gets repaid by a borrower, it sends a distribution notice — and whether that cash is "return of your capital" or "income earned" changes both the investor's tax bill and their DPI going forward. Confusing "committed" with "invested," or "distributed" with "still-invested-but-marked-up," is the single fastest way to sound like you have not actually worked with private funds.

**4. The J-curve is real in private credit, but it is a much gentler curve than in private equity, and explaining why is a genuine differentiator in an interview.** Worked example: in a buyout fund, the GP pays fees and makes zero-income investments for years before any exit, so net IRR often runs negative in years 1-2 — a deep dip before the curve turns up. A direct-lending fund's loans throw off cash interest from the day they are funded, so while fees still create a small early drag, there is no multi-year income gap: the curve is shallow and short. Being able to draw that contrast, unprompted, is exactly the kind of test an interviewer is running for.

**5. "Semi-liquid" is a legal structure with a cap built in, not a promise — and 2026 proved it.** Worked example (already established in this project and safe to cite as background): several large evergreen vehicles received redemption requests well above their monthly/quarterly cap in 2026 (Blue Owl's OCIC at 21.9% of NAV and OTIC at 40.7%, both capped at 5%; Partners Group's $8.6bn Global Value fund gated on 9.8% of NAV requests) and simply paid out the capped amount, queuing the rest. The product specialist's job is to have explained this mechanism to the client *before* it happens — gates are a designed liquidity-management feature of the vehicle, not a sign the fund has failed — so that when it does happen, the client is not hearing the concept for the first time in a panic.
