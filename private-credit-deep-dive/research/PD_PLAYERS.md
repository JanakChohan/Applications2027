## 1.2 The insurance convergence

This is the single biggest structural change in who owns private credit risk. Managers have
bought life and annuity insurers, or struck long-term asset-management deals with them, because an
insurer's liabilities (annuities, life policies) are long-dated, contractually fixed and
predictable, which is exactly the funding profile that matches an illiquid, long-maturity loan
book. Owning or managing the insurer converts a fee-earning asset manager into something closer to
a "spread" business: it borrows cheaply (policyholder premiums) and lends at a private-credit
yield, pocketing the difference, in addition to earning management fees on the same assets twice
over (once from outside investors, once from its own captive insurance balance sheet).

- **Apollo and Athene.** Announced 8 March 2021 as an all-stock merger; Athene's equity was valued
  at approximately $11 billion, exchanged at 1.149 Apollo shares per Athene Class A share, with
  Apollo shareholders ending up owning about 76% of the combined company and Athene shareholders
  about 24% [Confirmed, fetched] [P173]. The merger completed on 1 January 2022. Marc Rowan
  (incoming Apollo CEO) called it "all about alignment between Apollo and Athene... to optimize
  our strategy and allocate capital efficiently"; Athene CEO Jim Belardi said a fully aligned
  business would be "significantly greater than a sum-of-the-parts" [Confirmed, fetched] [P173].
  Athene is now the retirement-services subsidiary sitting inside Apollo's $849 billion Credit
  segment (section 1.1), which is why Apollo's "Credit AUM" dwarfs pure-play managers like Ares.
- **KKR and Global Atlantic.** KKR first acquired a majority economic interest (about 61.1%) in
  Global Atlantic on 1 February 2021. On 29 November 2023 KKR agreed to buy the remaining 37% it
  did not already own for approximately $2.7 billion in cash (1.0x Global Atlantic's book value,
  with adjustments); the deal closed on 2 January 2024, taking KKR to 100% ownership [Confirmed,
  fetched] [P174]. Global Atlantic's AUM grew from $72 billion in 2020 to $158 billion by the time

- **Brookfield and American Equity Life (AEL).** Brookfield Reinsurance completed its acquisition

**Why regulators are worried.** The concern is not that insurers hold illiquid assets — that is
traditionally appropriate given their long-dated liabilities — but two more specific things: (1)
opacity and conflicts of interest when an asset manager both originates the loan and owns/manages
the insurer buying it (an "affiliated" transaction, where the manager is on both sides), and (2)
regulatory arbitrage through offshore reinsurance. The NAIC's April 2026 issue brief on CLOs and
private credit records that US insurers held $276.8 billion in CLOs at year-end 2024 (up about 2%
on 2023, more than doubled since 2018), representing about 5.1% of total bonds and 3.1% of total
cash and invested assets; life insurers held 82% of that exposure and insurers with more than $10
billion in assets accounted for 82% of the total [Confirmed, fetched] [P180]. About 80% of insurer
CLO holdings are investment grade (39% AAA), which the NAIC uses to argue the issue is not that
these assets are inherently inappropriate, but whether "transparency, valuation, capital
treatment and supervisory tools are keeping pace" [Confirmed, fetched, verbatim below] [P180]. The
NAIC has already responded with a principles-based bond definition (effective 1 January 2025,
classifying assets by economic substance rather than legal form), a 45% risk-based-capital charge
on CLO residual tranches, tighter asset-adequacy testing for long-duration liabilities (AG 53), and
new disclosure and testing requirements for asset-intensive offshore reinsurance (AG 55), plus
enhanced scrutiny of Bermuda and Cayman Islands reinsurers [Confirmed, fetched] [P180]. Separately,
Barclays research (cited in trade press) found private credit assets held by US life insurers grew
more than 20% in 2025 to about 10% of total assets, and over 15% for PE-affiliated insurers such as
Apollo-backed Athene and KKR-backed Global Atlantic [Reported] [P182]. The IMF has separately
warned that insurers holding complex, leveraged, often investment-grade-rated private credit
instruments could face larger-than-expected losses in a stress scenario because reduced capital
buffers assume the ratings are reliable [Reported] [P182]. On Bermuda specifically: the Bermuda
Monetary Authority has tightened oversight of private-equity-owned (re)insurers (requiring prior
approval of long-term block reinsurance deals, more data collection and on-site exams), following
concerns crystallised by the 777 Re case (a PE-owned Bermudian reinsurer that ran into trouble after
concentrated, related-party exposure); the US Treasury has also joined the scrutiny, with Treasury
Secretary Scott Bessent meeting state insurance regulators to discuss the industry's growing
exposure to private credit routed through the roughly $1.52 trillion Bermuda life reinsurance
market [Reported] [P183].

## 1.3 The bank relationship: from competitor to partner (and lender)

Banks have responded to losing leveraged-lending market share to private credit funds in two ways
simultaneously: forming origination partnerships with the same managers that are "competing" with
them, and lending TO those managers' funds (which is a bigger, less visible source of
interconnectedness).

**Named partnerships:**
- **JPMorgan**: increased its direct-lending balance-sheet commitment to $50 billion in February
  2025 (from an earlier commitment), plus nearly $15 billion more from co-lending partners
  including FS Investments, Cliffwater and Shenkman Capital Management. Since 2021, JPMorgan says
  it has deployed over $10 billion across more than 100 private credit transactions [Reported]
  [P184].
- **Citi and Apollo**: announced 26 September 2024, a $25 billion private credit and direct
  lending program initially focused on North America, with the potential to expand geographically
  and beyond the initial size. Mubadala Investment Company and Apollo's own insurance arm, Athene,
  can co-invest alongside the program. Citi's rationale, in its own words: "Combining the strength
  of Citi's Banking and Capital Markets franchise with Apollo's deep capital resources will provide
  clients with a range of options." Apollo's Jim Zelter: "Our collaboration will allow Citi to
  enhance its client offerings and bring more private solutions to bear, while enabling Apollo to
  increase origination flow and tap into Citi's extensive client relationships" [Confirmed,
  fetched, verbatim below] [P185].
- **Wells Fargo and Centerbridge**: announced September 2023, a strategic relationship in which
  Centerbridge launched a new BDC platform (Overland Advisors / Overland Advantage) targeting at
  least $5 billion (including $2.5 billion of equity commitments) for direct lending to
  non-sponsor-backed North American middle-market companies with $25–125 million-plus of EBITDA.
  Wells Fargo provides client sourcing from its middle-market customer base and makes a minority
  investment in the fund; it does not commit senior balance-sheet capital the way JPMorgan does
  [Reported] [P186].
- **Barclays and AGL Credit Management**: launched 2 April 2024, an exclusive cooperation
  agreement giving AGL first look at every deal with a private-credit option that Barclays
  originates through its leveraged finance and investment-banking franchise, backed by a $1 billion
  anchor commitment from the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA). Notably, Barclays itself
  commits no balance-sheet capital to the platform, unlike JPMorgan's model [Reported] [P187].
- **Societe Generale and Brookfield**: announced September 2023, a partnership targeting a €10
  billion (about $10.7 billion) private investment-grade debt fund over four years, launching
  initially with €2.5 billion and focused on power, renewables, data, midstream and transportation
  credit, plus a fund-finance strategy — explicitly designed to produce investment-grade,
  duration-matched product for insurance-company buyers [Reported] [P188].
- **Goldman Sachs**: created a Capital Solutions Group in January 2025 to combine its financing,
  origination and structuring activities, explicitly to scale private credit; Goldman Sachs Asset
  Management aims to grow its private credit portfolio to $300 billion by 2029 and was in talks in
  2026 to raise at least $10 billion for a new direct-lending fund (West Street Loan Partners VI),
  whose predecessor raised over $13 billion in 2024 [Reported] [P189].

**Banks lending TO private credit funds — the less visible, more systemically-watched channel.**
The Financial Stability Board's 6 May 2026 "Report on Vulnerabilities in Private Credit" is the
single best primary source here [Confirmed, fetched] [P181]. Key findings, read directly from the
report:
- Private credit (narrowly defined as nonbank direct lending to medium-sized companies negotiated
  bilaterally) is estimated at $1.5–2 trillion globally as of end-2024, similar in size to the
  institutional leveraged loan market (~$1.5–1.7 trillion) and the high-yield public bond market
  (~$2 trillion); the US has the largest market at around $1 trillion [Confirmed, fetched] [P181].
- Direct bank lending to private credit funds is "relatively small" in aggregate: FSB member data
  captures around $220 billion of drawn-plus-undrawn credit lines from banks to private credit
  funds, while commercial-data estimates range from $270 billion to $500 billion, representing "a
  small share" of bank total assets and CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1) capital — in aggregate, member
  data puts it at under 0.5% of total bank assets, though some individual banking groups reach up
  to 4.4% of total assets [Confirmed, fetched] [P181].
- The most common facility type is the subscription line — a short-term bridge loan secured
  against a fund's uncalled capital commitments from its own investors (limited partners), priced
  around 200bps over benchmark rates. Other facility types include fund portfolio financing (the
  largest segment in the euro area, structured like a securitisation "borrowing base"), NAV
  facilities (loan-to-value typically capped near 30%), asset-based lending (loan-to-value up to
  65%), repo financing, bank investment in private credit CLO tranches, and revolving credit lines
  to BDCs (up to 90% of bank lending to BDCs takes this form) [Confirmed, fetched] [P181].
- The FSB explicitly flags "strategic partnerships" between banks and asset managers — including
  forward-flow agreements where asset managers continuously buy newly originated loans off a bank's
  platform — as a growing but poorly understood source of interconnectedness, where "the extent to
  which incentives are aligned between originators or sponsors and investors... remains uncertain"
  [Confirmed, fetched, verbatim below] [P181].
- Separately, roughly half of companies that borrow from private credit funds also maintain bank
  financing relationships (typically revolving credit lines), meaning banks and private credit
  funds often have overlapping exposure to the same borrowers [Confirmed, fetched] [P181].
- The private-credit CLO ("middle market CLO") market had reached about $155 billion outstanding
  as of October 2025, around 16% of the $977 billion broader US CLO market; these structures tend
  to hold a higher share of CCC-rated and non-covenant-lite loans than traditional broadly
  syndicated loan CLOs, but issuers typically retain the entire (first-loss) equity tranche
  themselves, which the FSB says suggests "limited contagion of losses to other market
  participants" [Confirmed, fetched] [P181].
- On the wider NBFI (non-bank financial institution) picture: separate research cited in trade
  press put the largest US banks' total credit commitments to the NBFI sector at $2.3 trillion by
  2024, up from $1.3 trillion in 2018, with around $1.4 trillion in actual drawn exposure at
  year-end 2025 [Reported] [P199]. The Bank of England announced in December 2025 that its second
  System-Wide Exploratory Scenario (SWES) stress-test exercise, launching in early 2026 with
  results expected in early 2027, will focus specifically on the private markets ecosystem and
  whether bank–NBFI interactions can amplify systemic stress [Reported] [P199].

## 1.4 Consolidation and M&A: traditional managers buying private credit capability

Traditional (mostly public-markets) asset managers facing fee compression have been buying private
credit managers outright, rather than building the capability from scratch, because origination
relationships and underwriting teams take years to build organically.

- **BlackRock buys HPS Investment Partners.** Announced 3 December 2024; BlackRock agreed to pay
  approximately $12 billion, with 100% of consideration in BlackRock equity (one third at closing,
  two thirds over five years, contingent on performance, per deal reporting). The deal completed on
  1 July 2025. HPS managed approximately $157 billion of client assets as of 31 March 2025. The
  combined "Private Financing Solutions" platform is expected to hold about $190 billion in client
  assets, and BlackRock projected the deal would grow private-markets fee-paying AUM by about 40%
  and management fees by about 35% [Confirmed, fetched] [P153].
- **Nuveen acquires Arcmont.** Announced 27 October 2022, completed 1 March 2023; Nuveen (TIAA's
  asset manager) acquired a controlling interest in Arcmont, a European private debt manager with
  $21 billion of committed capital, in a deal reportedly worth over $1 billion. The combination
  with Nuveen's existing Churchill Asset Management created a private-debt platform with more than
  $60 billion of combined committed capital at the time, and took Nuveen's firmwide alternative
  credit AUM to $178 billion [Reported] [P190]. By 2026 Churchill and Arcmont together made up
  Nuveen Private Capital, a $97 billion platform (section 1.1) [Reported] [P164].
- **Janus Henderson acquires Victory Park Capital (VPC).** Announced October 2024 (majority stake,
  cash and stock consideration). In September 2025, insurer CNO Financial Group bought a minority
  stake in VPC for $600 million of capital commitments. In 2026 Janus Henderson also agreed to buy
  Rantum Capital, a Frankfurt-based private debt and equity manager focused on German-speaking
  mid-sized companies, expected to close Q3 2026 [Reported] [P191].
- **T. Rowe Price acquires Oak Hill Advisors.** Completed 29 December 2021 for $4.2 billion ($3.3
  billion at closing — about 74% cash, 26% stock — plus up to $900 million more contingent on
  performance milestones from 2025). This was T. Rowe Price's first major push into private markets
  [Reported] [P192].
- **Manulife acquires CQS.** Deal completed 3 April 2024 (financial terms undisclosed); Manulife
  Investment Management took on CQS's multi-sector alternative credit platform, co-branding it
  "Manulife | CQS Investment Management" and keeping CQS's existing leadership in place [Reported]
  [P193].
- **Blue Owl's acquisition run.** Kuvare Asset Management (insurance-focused asset manager),
  completed 1 July 2024 for $750 million, expected to add up to $20 billion of AUM; Atalaya Capital
  Management, completed 30 September 2024 (asset-based/specialty finance manager); IPI Partners
  (digital infrastructure fund manager), completed 3 January 2025 [Reported] [P194]. These moves
  extended Blue Owl beyond direct lending into insurance asset management and digital
  infrastructure credit.
- **Franklin Templeton / Benefit Street Partners / Alcentra.** Franklin Templeton bought Benefit
  Street Partners (BSP) from Providence Equity Partners, completing 1 February 2019, for a firm
  then managing about $26 billion of alternative credit. It layered on Alcentra in November 2022
  (section 1.1), and in January 2026 folded Alcentra into BSP entirely, creating a combined
  transatlantic credit manager overseeing roughly $86 billion [Reported] [P195].
- **Brookfield and Oaktree** (background/context, not new for 2024-26 but foundational to the
  wider consolidation trend): Brookfield announced on 13 March 2019 that it would acquire 62% of
  Oaktree Capital Management for approximately $4.7 billion, completing 30 September 2019. Oaktree
  — a distressed-debt and credit specialist — had grown to $223 billion of AUM by 31 December 2025
  under Brookfield's ownership [Reported] [P198].

**The strategic logic, in plain terms**: public-markets fee rates have been compressed for two
decades by index funds and fee competition; private credit fees (management fee plus often a
performance fee) are structurally higher, and private credit AUM is stickier (longer lock-ups,
harder to redeem — see the already-established BDC gating episodes). Buying an established private
credit manager gets a traditional manager instant origination relationships, underwriting talent
and a track record — all of which take years to build from zero — in exchange for a large
upfront/earnout price tag, which is why so many of these deals are structured with big
performance-contingent deferred payments (BlackRock/HPS, T. Rowe/Oak Hill).

## 1.5 The Fidelity International angle

Fidelity International's own strategy, confirmed in this research round with two independent trade
press sources reporting on the same underlying event:

- Fidelity International's **FutureWise** default fund for UK workplace pensions passed £25
  billion of AUM in May 2026, having grown from £6.5 billion since it moved to a target-date-fund
  (TDF) structure in 2022; Fidelity projects it will exceed £40 billion by 2030 [Confirmed,
  fetched, two sources] [P196] [P197].
- FutureWise became, in early 2025, the inaugural investor in Fidelity's **Diversified Private
  Assets Long-Term Asset Fund (LTAF)**, with gradual exposure increases over three years targeting
  a 15% allocation to private markets during the fund's growth phase [Confirmed, fetched] [P196]
  [P197].
- On 21–22 May 2026, Fidelity announced the **Private Markets Partner Network** sitting inside that
  LTAF: an initial group of **10 general partners** spanning private equity, private credit,
  infrastructure and real estate. The 10 named were **Ares Management, Bridges Fund Management,
  Crescent Capital, Churchill Asset Management, CVC Capital Partners, DigitalBridge, Fidelity
  International (its own private credit strategies), Haveli Investments, Hg and Stonepeak**
  [Confirmed, fetched, two sources] [P196] [P197]. This directly confirms the previously-reported
  detail that Ares, Crescent and Churchill sit in the network's private-credit sleeve, and adds the
  other seven names and the exact date. Fidelity said "additional partnerships" are expected as
  allocations scale toward the long-term target [Confirmed, fetched] [P196].
- Vivian Liu, Fidelity International's Head of Private Markets Portfolio Management, on the
  network's purpose: "We have carefully selected a group of specialist GPs to provide diversified
  exposure across private markets" [Confirmed, fetched, verbatim] [P196] [P197].
- James Monk, FutureWise's Investment Director, on the wider structural shift: "Moving to a TDF
  structure has enabled us to accelerate innovation, enhance governance and deliver a clearer
  investment journey for members" [Confirmed, fetched, verbatim] [P197].

**How this positions Fidelity versus a firm like Blackstone.** Fidelity International is not
trying to become a private credit *manufacturer* competing with Ares or Blackstone for origination
and underwriting talent. Per the already-established UK-filing language, Fidelity decided to "cease
manufacturing of non-core private asset classes in order to pivot resources to areas of unique
strength... active ETFs and the distribution of third-party capabilities for both private assets
and select public market investments." The Private Markets Partner Network is the concrete
expression of that "distribution of third-party capabilities" strategy: Fidelity acts as the
**access and packaging layer** — running the LTAF wrapper, doing manager selection and due
diligence, and providing the pension-scheme distribution relationship (FutureWise itself) — while
outsourcing actual loan origination and underwriting to specialist GPs it has selected. This is the
opposite model to Blackstone/Apollo/Ares/KKR, which each run their own origination platforms,
underwrite loans directly, and increasingly own the insurance balance sheet that buys the loans
too (section 1.2). In career terms for a client portfolio manager role: Fidelity's private credit
proposition is built on manager research, portfolio construction and access, not underwriting.

## 1.6 How firms differentiate from each other

Search results in trade press repeatedly return the same handful of axes on which managers claim
to differentiate, though full-text access to the underlying commentary pieces was partly blocked
(see confidence notes):
- **Origination and sponsor relationships**: whether a manager sees deal flow directly from
  private equity sponsors (a "sponsor-backed" or "sponsor-finance" model, e.g. Ares, Antares,
  Golub) or has built a book of "non-sponsored"/independent middle-market relationships (the model
  Centerbridge's Overland Advantage and some BDCs pursue) is treated in trade press as a real point
  of differentiation — trade press in 2026 was specifically discussing "the unsponsored deal
  opportunity in private credit" as a distinct strategy [Reported, snippet only] [P199].
- **Scale — the ability to underwrite a large cheque alone.** Only a handful of platforms (Ares,
  Blackstone, Apollo, Blue Owl, HPS/BlackRock) can write a $1 billion-plus unitranche loan without
  syndicating it, which lets them win the largest, most competitive sponsor-backed deals; smaller
  managers compete on sector expertise or flexibility instead. A Macfarlanes commentary piece
  titled "Platform extension in private credit: growth, granularity and governance" (June 2026)
  was found to argue that institutional investors increasingly "want deeper relationships with
  fewer managers," which is part of what is driving both scale-seeking and the M&A wave in section
  1.4, though the full body text of that piece could not be retrieved (404 on direct fetch) —
  treat this specific framing as PAYWALLED-NOT-READ / SNIPPET only [P199].
- **Sector specialisation**: trade press specifically discussed Ares building "industry
  specialisation within direct lending" and separately, mid-market infrastructure credit as a
  distinct, growing niche in 2026 — both signal managers competing on vertical expertise rather
  than pure balance-sheet size [Reported, snippet only] [P199].
- **Workout and restructuring capability**: not directly evidenced with a fetched primary source in
  this round (GAP — flagged in section 5), but logically follows from the already-established rise
  in PIK income and the Proskauer default index numbers: a manager with an in-house restructuring
  team can manage a troubled loan itself rather than selling it at a discount, which matters more
  as defaults tick up.
- **Permanent capital and distribution reach**: managers with permanent or evergreen capital
  (insurance balance sheets under section 1.2, non-traded BDCs, LTAFs) do not face the redemption
  risk that open-ended interval funds do (see already-established BDC gating episodes), and having
  a captive insurance balance sheet also guarantees a captive buyer for newly originated loans —
  itself a form of "distribution." Firms that lack their own insurer (Ares before Aspida, HPS
  before BlackRock/AIG-type deals) have had to go out and strike bank or sovereign-wealth-fund
  partnerships instead (section 1.3) to source the same kind of "permanent-ish" capital.

# 6. WHAT A BEGINNER MUST UNDERSTAND FROM THIS

1. **"Private credit AUM" is not one number, and different managers count different things inside
   it — so never compare two firms' AUM figures without checking what's included.** Best worked
   example: Apollo's $849 billion "Credit" segment AUM (P154) is so large partly because it
   includes Athene, its own insurance subsidiary's entire investment portfolio — money Apollo
   effectively also "owns" the liability side of — whereas Ares's $440.5 billion Credit Group AUM
   (P150) is almost entirely third-party fund and account money with no captive insurer inside it.
   A beginner comparing "Apollo vs Ares market share" without knowing this would draw the wrong
   conclusion about who actually has more third-party client money to manage.

2. **The insurance convergence is the single most important structural trend, because it changes
   WHO bears the risk and WHY the manager wants the loan.** Best worked example: Apollo-Athene.
   Before the January 2022 merger, Apollo was a fee-earning manager persuading Athene (a separate
   company) to buy the loans it originated. After the merger, Apollo owns Athene outright, so every
   dollar of spread Athene earns between its (cheap) annuity liabilities and its (higher-yielding)
   private credit assets flows straight to Apollo's bottom line, on top of any management fee.
   This is why "asset manager buys/merges with insurer" has been the dominant M&A pattern
   (Apollo/Athene, KKR/Global Atlantic, Brookfield/AEL, Blackstone/Corebridge & Resolution Life,
   Carlyle/Fortitude Re, Sixth Street/Talcott, Ares/Aspida) — and why regulators (NAIC, IMF,
   Bermuda Monetary Authority, US Treasury) are increasingly uncomfortable: when the same firm
   originates the loan AND owns the insurer buying it, the price and rating attached to that loan
   may not be independently checked the way it would be if a third party did the buying.

3. **Banks did not simply "lose" business to private credit — many of them are now funding private
   credit funds behind the scenes, which is the part regulators worry about most.** Best worked
   example: a subscription line. A bank lends a private credit fund short-term money secured
   against the fund's own uncalled investor commitments (its "dry powder" promises), at around
   200bps over benchmark rates (FSB report, P181). This looks safe individually — it is secured
   against real institutional-investor commitments — but the FSB's own $220–500 billion estimate
   of total bank exposure to private credit funds (drawn plus undrawn), and the fact that around
   half of private-credit borrower companies also have separate bank credit lines, means banks and
   private credit funds are increasingly exposed to the same underlying risk through two different
   doors. This is exactly why the Bank of England chose "the private markets ecosystem" as the
   subject of its next system-wide stress test, starting early 2026.

4. **Traditional asset managers are buying private credit capability rather than building it,
   because origination relationships cannot be built quickly — and the deal structures show
   managers hedging that bet.** Best worked example: BlackRock's ~$12 billion, all-stock purchase
   of HPS (announced December 2024, completed July 2025). BlackRock did not have a scaled direct
   lending platform of its own; buying HPS's $157 billion of client assets and its origination
   relationships instantly gave it one. The fact that a large share of the total deal consideration
   in comparable transactions (e.g. T. Rowe Price/Oak Hill's up-to-$900 million earnout) is paid
   out later and tied to performance milestones shows acquirers are trying to make sure the
   origination talent they are buying actually keeps performing after the deal, since the value is
   almost entirely in people and relationships, not hard assets.

5. **Fidelity International has deliberately chosen not to compete on origination and underwriting
   at all — it competes on manager selection, packaging and distribution instead.** Best worked
   example: the Private Markets Partner Network launched inside Fidelity's Diversified Private
   Assets LTAF in May 2026. Rather than building its own $100 billion-plus direct lending platform
   the way Blackstone or Ares have, Fidelity selected 10 specialist GPs (Ares, Crescent, Churchill
   and others for credit, plus specialists in private equity, infrastructure and real estate) and
   built the LTAF wrapper, due diligence process and pension-scheme distribution (FutureWise, its
   own £25 billion default fund) around them. For a client portfolio manager role at Fidelity, this
   is the single most important fact to internalise: the job is fundamentally about manager
   research, portfolio construction across specialist managers, and explaining access and
   structure to clients — not about underwriting individual loans.
