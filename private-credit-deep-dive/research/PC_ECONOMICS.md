# 1. FINDINGS

## 1.1 How the manager makes money

**Base management fee: the gross-versus-net-assets fight is the whole ballgame.**
Almost every externally managed business development company (BDC — a closed-end fund structure created by Congress in 1980 that lets ordinary investors buy into a portfolio of loans to mid-sized private companies, usually listed or semi-liquid) pays its external manager a base management fee of roughly 1.0%–2.0% per year, and the fee base matters enormously [Confirmed, P104, P106]. Charging the fee on **gross assets** (total assets including everything bought with borrowed money) rather than **net assets** (assets minus fund-level debt, i.e. shareholders' equity/NAV) means the manager earns more, in absolute dollars, the more the fund borrows — even though the extra assets belong partly to lenders, not shareholders. Ares Capital Corporation (ARCC), the largest listed BDC, charges 1.50% per year on average gross assets (cash excluded, borrowed funds included), stepping down to 1.00% on assets funded above 200% of NAV [Confirmed, P101, primary source: investment advisory agreement filed as an exhibit on SEC EDGAR]. FS KKR Capital Corp (FSK) uses the same gross-assets base but stepped its fee down from 1.5% to 1.0% on all assets financed with leverage above 1.0x debt-to-equity, effective June 15, 2019 — a direct, filed acknowledgment that a flat gross-assets fee over-rewards leverage [Confirmed, P102/P103]. One industry-wide estimate found externally managed BDCs' average all-in management-fee-plus-incentive-fee expense at 3.19% of total (gross) assets, which works out to 5.46% of net assets — nearly double, because leverage roughly doubles the base against which some of the fee is calculated while shareholders alone absorb the leverage cost [Reported, P144]. A generalist counter-finding says moderate BDC leverage (capped by law, see §6) means a 1.5%–1.75% gross-asset fee usually is not much more than an equivalent 2% net-asset fee [Reported, P104] — so the two views (fee on gross assets is a real leverage-driven amplifier vs. it is roughly a wash given regulatory leverage caps) coexist in the trade literature; a beginner should hold both.

**Income (performance) incentive fee: 20% over a hurdle, with a catch-up.**
The standard BDC incentive-fee mechanism, taken verbatim from Ares Capital's investment advisory agreement (filed on SEC EDGAR): the adviser gets **0%** of "pre-incentive fee net investment income" until it exceeds a **1.75% quarterly hurdle (7.0% annualized)**; then the adviser receives **100% of the income between 1.75% and 2.1875% quarterly** — this is the "catch-up," designed so that once income clears roughly 2.19% a quarter (8.75% annualized) the manager has caught up to exactly 20% of the whole pre-incentive-fee income; above 2.1875% quarterly the adviser takes a flat **20% of everything** [Confirmed, P101]. FS KKR's structure is similar in shape (hurdle plus catch-up plus a flat share above it) but FSK cut its base incentive-fee rate from 20% to **17.5%** in its June 2021 amended advisory agreement [Confirmed, P103]. Trade summaries put the industry range at **15%–20% incentive fee** over a **6%–8% hurdle (7% is the most common)** [Reported, P104, P106]. Ares also has an income "look-back" deferral: no incentive fee is paid in a quarter if the sum of distributions and NAV change over the trailing four quarters falls below 7% of NAV — a partial shareholder protection [Confirmed, P101].

**Capital gains incentive fee — the second incentive fee most beginners never hear about.**
On top of the income incentive fee, most BDCs also charge a **capital gains incentive fee, typically 20% of cumulative net realized capital gains** (net of cumulative realized losses and unrealized depreciation), calculated annually since the adviser was appointed, minus capital-gains fees already paid in prior years [Confirmed, P101, Ares Capital's agreement dating this stream to October 8, 2004]. FS KKR's agreement contains the same 20% capital-gains incentive-fee mechanic [Confirmed, P102]. This means a BDC manager is paid twice on the same book: once on the running yield (income incentive fee) and again if a workout or equity kicker produces a realized gain.

**Origination, arrangement and OID income — a fee the *borrower* pays that flows straight to the lender/manager.**
Private loans are typically issued **below par** — Original Issue Discount, or OID — which functions as an underwriting/arrangement fee paid by the borrower at closing, with the direct lender acting as its own underwriter [Confirmed, P142]. Roughly **69% of BDC deals with OID amortize it into yield over the loan's life**, while about **15% book the entire discount to income immediately** at closing — a real accounting choice that shifts when the manager's business (and hence base-fee-eligible assets and incentive-fee-eligible income) recognizes that value [Reported, P142]. Immediate recognition inflates the current quarter's "pre-incentive-fee net investment income," which is exactly the number the 20% incentive fee is calculated on — so the accounting choice is not neutral to what the manager collects when.

**Leverage multiplies fee income directly.** Because the base management fee is charged on gross assets in most agreements, every dollar of fund-level debt the manager adds increases the fee base — this is stated as a deliberate policy conflict in the trade literature: "may give a BDC's investment adviser a financial incentive to incur leverage" [Confirmed, P106]. Combined with the fact the incentive fee is levied on net investment income (which leverage also increases, at the cost of increased volatility to the shareholder equity cushion — see §6), fund-level leverage is simultaneously a leverage on manager fee income and on shareholder risk.

## 1.2 How the investor makes money — and why this is not private equity

The single most important idea for a beginner: **a private credit investor's return is overwhelmingly contractual current income, not capital appreciation.** A private loan is a debt instrument. Absent default, the investor cannot make more than principal plus contracted interest and fees; there is no analogue to a private equity fund's multiple-of-money upside from selling an equity stake for many times cost. The building blocks of the **gross return** are:

1. **Reference rate** — most private credit loans are floating rate, priced off SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the US dollar reference rate that replaced LIBOR) [Confirmed, P148].
2. **Credit spread** — the additional yield over SOFR compensating for the borrower's credit risk. Private credit new-issue spreads have run **1.5–3.4 percentage points wider than broadly syndicated loan (BSL) spreads since 2019** [Reported, P148] — this spread premium over a public comparator is the crux of the "illiquidity premium" argument, discussed in §7.
3. **Fees/OID amortization** — the arrangement fee described above, recognized as yield over the life of the loan (or, for the ~15% of deals booked immediately, recognized as an income spike at close) [Reported, P142].
4. **PIK income** — payment-in-kind interest, where interest is added to principal rather than paid in cash. Already established in this project's shared background: PIK income rose from about 5.9% of BDC income in 2023 to about 8% in 2026 — a genuine part of gross yield but non-cash, so it does not put spendable cash in an investor's hand and can also be a symptom of borrower stress (see §4).

From gross yield, an investor's **net return** is gross yield **minus**: realized credit losses (defaults net of recoveries), **minus fund-level fees** (the management and incentive fees in §1.1), **minus fund-level leverage costs** (interest paid on the fund's own borrowings, which is a genuine cash cost distinct from the fee-on-gross-assets issue above), **minus fund expenses**. This is why gross CDLI-style index returns (§3) and net investor experience can diverge meaningfully, and why the brief's rule to "always distinguish gross from net returns" matters concretely here: Cliffwater's Direct Lending Index is a levered-loan-return index net of losses but **before** fund-level manager fees are applied to an end investor's specific vehicle [Inferred from index construction reporting, P108/P110].

## 1.3 Actual returns data

Cliffwater's Direct Lending Index (CDLI — an asset-weighted index of business development company and private BDC loan returns, inception date reported as 2004 with the index itself first published from 2015) returned **9.3% for full-year 2025**, and has delivered a **20-year average of 9.5% per year with only one negative year (2008)** [Confirmed, P108]. Within 2025, interest income alone equaled **10.4%** of return (the main driver), with PIK interest income steady at **0.7%** [Confirmed, P108]. Trailing-12-month return to Q2 2025 was reported at **10.06%**, with a **Q2 2025 quarterly return of 2.34%** [Reported, P110]. Since CDLI's inception, the index has **outperformed public high-yield bonds, leveraged loans and investment-grade credit in 13 of the 20 calendar years ending 2024** [Reported, P130, citing Cliffwater's own memo]. Cliffwater also reports **gross yield spreads averaging 5.95 percentage points over high-yield bonds and 4.38 points over bank loans**, over the ten years to September 30, 2025 [Reported, P110]. Over the same long window, CDLI showed **low correlation to the S&P 500 (0.52) versus high-yield bonds' 0.82 correlation**, and near-zero correlation (0.09) to broad fixed income, which is one reason institutions like it as a diversifier — though low *reported* correlation is itself contested (§7) [Reported, P110].

**Dispersion.** Multiple sources independently describe private credit manager dispersion as structurally much wider than in public credit: "the gap between top- and bottom-quartile managers in private credit can be three times that or more compared to public markets, where top- and bottom-manager performance may vary by one to two percentage points" [Reported, P111]. This is presented as a reason manager selection matters far more in private credit than in, say, an index bond fund — there is no way to buy "the private credit market" the way you can buy a bond index.

**The IRR-vs-TVPI methodology fight.** A named academic critique (Jeff Hooke and Xiaohua Hu of Johns Hopkins Carey Business School, and Michael Imerman of UC Irvine) argues private credit funds "barely outperform or in some cases underperform" public credit benchmarks once returns are measured properly, and that IRR (internal rate of return) overstates performance because it assumes early distributions are reinvested at the same high rate, which is often unavailable; they prefer TVPI (total value to paid-in capital) and found that for 2018-vintage senior debt funds, **79% of TVPI came from unrealized (not yet cashed-out) residual value** — i.e., much of the "return" is still a mark, not cash in hand [Reported, P132]. Cliffwater's counter (as a manager and consultant with commercial interest in the asset class) is that private credit outperformed public credit by "4.82 percent per annum" on a time-weighted basis over 2015–2020, and that high residual values in senior debt funds simply reflect loan principal that is expected to be repaid at maturity, not a valuation trick, while TVPI "was never intended as a performance measure because it does not factor in time" [Reported, P132]. Both named camps are captured with attribution; neither claim is adopted as fact here.

Fitch's US private credit portfolio-tracking data recorded default rates reaching a **record 9.2% for 2025** in one measure and a **record 6.0% in April 2026** in another measure/report, alongside a trailing-twelve-month rate of **5.8% through January 2026, up from 5.6% in December 2025** — the range across Fitch releases reflects different sample definitions and measurement windows, illustrating rule 8's warning that different data providers (and even the same provider's different indices) give different headline numbers [Reported, P115, P130].

## 1.4 Losses: default rates, recovery rates, and the "shadow default" critique

This project's shared background already established the Proskauer Private Credit Default Index moving **1.84% → 2.73% → 2.51%** across four quarters to mid-2026; new material below extends and cross-checks that with other providers, deliberately avoiding re-deriving the same series.

KBRA's Direct Lending Index shows the trailing-12-month **default rate rose to 2.3% of issuers as of June 2026**, with KBRA projecting it will keep rising to **around 3.5% by end-2026** [Reported, from earlier search synthesis, corroborated in fetched detail below]. KBRA's own Middle Market Default Monitor (KMDM), as of its Q4 2025 compendium, counted **81 companies in payment default or likely default (17 active defaults, 64 rated CCC- as "likely")**, giving a **default rate by count of 3.4%** (down slightly from 3.5% in Q3 2025) and **by value of 2.0%** (down from 2.1%) [Confirmed, P112, fetched primary document]. The same report flags stress beneath the headline rate: **25% of borrowers had sub-1.0x interest coverage** (the lowest share since Q3 2024, i.e. still historically elevated), median interest coverage was **1.5x**, and **12% of borrowers by count (7% by notional value)** have debt maturing before end-2026, concentrated in Consumer Retail, Media, and Chemicals/Containers/Metals/Materials [Confirmed, P112].

Lincoln International's Senior Debt Index (a monthly fair-value index covering roughly 1,600 middle-market direct-lending investments across 175+ fund clients, now co-published with S&P Dow Jones Indices as the S&P Lincoln Senior Debt Index) reported **covenant defaults rising for a second straight quarter, from 2.4% to 2.9%**, but still **below the six-year average of 4.5%** as of Q1 2025 [Reported, P114].

Moody's, in an April 28, 2026 note, put the 2025 private credit default rate in a wide **1.6%–4.7% range**, explicitly attributing the spread to how **distressed restructurings** (which accounted for roughly **65% of all 2025 defaults**) are classified — include them and you capture more real stress but may overstate loss severity; exclude them and you understate latent stress [Confirmed, P116]. For comparison, Moody's put public high-yield one-year probability of default at **3.2%** as of March 2026, and all US listed companies' average one-year PD at **7.9%** (down from 9.1% a year earlier) [Confirmed, P116]. Moody's separately noted BDCs' average PD-implied risk premium over Baa-rated public corporates hit its **widest margin since post-pandemic normalization** [Confirmed, P116] — i.e. the market-implied risk in BDC portfolios looks worse than headline BDC default statistics alone would suggest.

**The "shadow default" / visibility-gap argument, with mechanism detail.** Multiple named sources converge on the same three mechanisms by which lenders keep a stressed loan off the formal default tape: (1) **amend-and-extend** — maturity extensions that "merely delay restructuring rather than prevent it" [Reported, P118]; (2) **PIK conversion** — switching cash interest to payment-in-kind so the loan looks current while unpaid interest is added to principal and compounds [Reported, P118, P119]; and (3) **covenant waivers/resets**, sometimes paired with a fee for the waiver [Reported, P118]. Fitch's own trailing-12-month default rate of **5.7% as of November 2025** (the highest since February 2024, per this source) is explicitly flagged by the same commentary as "only part of the picture," since shadow defaults are, by construction, unmeasured by formal default statistics [Reported, P118]. A separate industry piece puts a number on this: so-called "bad PIK" (PIK granted to borrowers who would otherwise be in payment default) could push the true "shadow default rate" to around **6%**, materially above formal default readings [Reported, from earlier search synthesis].

**Recovery rates: the headline advantage over public loans looks smaller or reversed once collateral quality is controlled for.** The Federal Reserve's own FEDS Notes analysis (Fang Cai and Sharjil Haque, published February 23, 2024) found the **post-default recovery value of a direct (private credit) loan averaged around 33%**, versus **52% for broadly syndicated loans and 39% for high-yield bonds** — a materially *worse* recovery outcome for private credit in this Fed sample, explained by the fact that **more than half of private credit loans by value go to sectors with minimal tangible/collateralizable assets** (software, financial services, healthcare) [Confirmed, P122, primary-source Fed research note]. This is in tension with older, more favourable industry data cited elsewhere — e.g., a Morningstar summary of 1995–Q1 2022 data putting **middle-market first-lien recovery at about 69% versus about 65% for broadly syndicated first-lien loans** [Reported, P121] — and PitchBook coverage stating that whatever historical recovery advantage private credit had, "this advantage is eroding," per Citi Research credit strategists [Reported, P123]. A beginner should read this as: the direction of the recovery-rate debate has genuinely reversed over time and by dataset — older, mostly-pre-2022 samples favour private credit; the Fed's 2024 note and 2025–26 trade commentary say the advantage has shrunk or flipped, largely because private credit has grown fastest in asset-light, low-collateral sectors like software.

## 1.5 The valuation problem

Private loans have no public market price. Fair value is estimated under **ASC 820** (the US GAAP fair value standard), which sorts inputs into three "levels": Level 1 (quoted prices in active markets — essentially never applies to a private loan), Level 2 (observable inputs other than quoted prices, e.g. comparable trades) and **Level 3** (unobservable inputs based on the reporting entity's own market-participant assumptions — where almost all direct loans sit) [Confirmed, P124]. The two standard Level 3 techniques for a credit instrument are a **discounted cash flow/yield analysis** (project contractual interest, principal and fees, discount at a market-participant-required yield reflecting current market yields for comparable credit risk/tenor/seniority/collateral — not the loan's own contractual coupon) and an **enterprise value coverage test** (does the company's estimated enterprise value cover the debt) [Confirmed, P124]. In practice this means each manager's board — typically a valuation committee of the BDC's board of directors, often assisted by an outside third-party valuation firm — signs off on a fair value that ultimately rests on assumptions no external market is testing in real time.

**The FCA's private market valuation review (UK) is the most systematic regulatory look at this problem.** Published **5 March 2025**, it surveyed **36 firms in two phases**, covering roughly **£3 trillion of global private-asset AUM and about £1 trillion of UK private AUM**, across private equity, venture capital, **private debt** and infrastructure [Confirmed, P126, primary-source regulator publication]. Findings specific to private debt: firms used both income-approach and market-approach methodologies, "often referred to a coverage test and a yield analysis," and **backtesting is inherently harder for debt because most assets are held to maturity rather than sold**, so there is rarely a realized transaction to check the mark against [Confirmed, P126]. Across all asset classes the FCA flagged: many firms only **"partly identified and documented" material conflicts of interest** (beyond the obvious fee/remuneration conflict) around investor marketing, secured borrowing, asset transfers, and redemptions/subscriptions; **inconsistent independence**, including senior investment professionals sitting as voting members of valuation committees; **only a minority of firms had defined quantitative or qualitative triggers for ad hoc (out-of-cycle) valuations**, and most firms did **not** revalue during significant market events; and valuation-committee minutes often failed to record how a decision was reached [Confirmed, P126]. The review made no finding that private debt is worse-governed than private equity — it applied the same scrutiny across classes — but it did note that judgment plays an outsized role in debt "where the credit quality of the asset had deteriorated" [Confirmed, P126].

**The SEC's 2025–2026 examination priorities name private credit specifically.** Examiners are directed to assess "methods and controls surrounding fair valuation of illiquid assets, especially during market volatility," to scrutinize side-by-side conflicts where an adviser runs both a public-facing BDC and separately managed accounts, to look for "overreliance on internal marks without sufficient independent validation," and to check whether managers fail to adjust valuations in distressed, covenant-breach or underperforming scenarios [Reported, P127]. Separately, remarks attributed to federal prosecutors (per a December 2025 Debevoise client note) signal renewed regulatory and enforcement attention to private-fund valuation and fee practices generally [Reported, P128, PAYWALLED-NOT-READ in full — corroborated by the ACA Group summary of the same SEC exam-priorities theme, P127].

**Documented, named, dated examples of different managers marking the identical loan at materially different prices** — precisely the criticism the brief asks about, and here it is with names attached, sourced to a CreditSights analysis that cross-referenced BDC 10-Q/10-K schedules of investments for identical borrower positions held across multiple funds [Confirmed as a documented dataset, P129]:
- **Pluralsight**: Golub Capital marked its position at **97 cents on the dollar** versus Blue Owl Capital at **83 cents**, a **14-point gap**, as of March 31, 2024; the gap narrowed to 8 points by June 30, 2024.
- **Lithium Technologies**: as of June 30, 2024, Goldman Sachs BDC marked it at **50 cents**, Hercules Capital at **53 cents**, and Sixth Street Specialty Lending at **77 cents** — a **27-point spread** on the same credit; one quarter earlier the same three funds were far closer together (93, 89, 91).
- **Auven Therapeutics (A.T. Holdings II)**: Barings BDC marked it at **78 cents** versus Oaktree Specialty Lending Corp at **par (100 cents)** — a **22-point gap**, as of June 30, 2024.
- **Isagenix International**: CION Investment Corp marked it at **98 cents** (an outlier) versus Crescent Capital BDC and Main Street Capital both at **88 cents** and Barings BDC at **86 cents** — a **12-point spread**, as of June 30, 2024.
- **Allucent (LS Clinical Services)**: Goldman Sachs BDC at **88 cents** versus MidCap Financial Investment Corp at **96 cents** — an **8-point gap**, as of June 30, 2024.

These are dated, sourced, cross-manager, same-quarter discrepancies on the identical underlying credit — the strongest evidence available that "mark-to-model" really does produce materially different answers depending on which manager is doing the modelling, even though every mark is nominally an ASC 820 Level 3 fair value produced under board oversight [Confirmed, P129]. Separately, a November 2025 opinion piece flagged that in practice **only about 25%–35% of BDC positions get revalued with any "material" price change in a given quarter**, i.e. most marks simply roll forward unchanged even though quarterly revaluation is nominally required [Reported, corroborating the same CreditSights dataset, P129].

## 1.6 Leverage at every level

**Leverage inside the borrower**: the underlying private companies themselves typically carry meaningful leverage; KBRA's Q4 2025 compendium found borrower **median interest coverage of just 1.5x** and flagged that **nearly 30% of companies with debt maturing before end-2026 carry leverage above 10x EBITDA or report negative EBITDA** [Confirmed, P112 and earlier search synthesis].

**Leverage at the fund (BDC) level is capped by statute, and the cap itself changed in 2018.** Under Section 61(a) of the Investment Company Act of 1940, BDCs originally had to maintain **200% asset coverage**, equivalent to a maximum **1:1 debt-to-equity ratio**. The Small Business Credit Availability Act, signed **March 23, 2018**, let BDCs reduce minimum asset coverage to **150%** — equivalent to a maximum **2:1 debt-to-equity ratio** — provided the change is approved by a majority of disinterested directors (or shareholders) and publicly disclosed [Confirmed, P139]. This single legal change roughly doubled the leverage capacity of the whole listed BDC sector.

**Beyond on-balance-sheet debt, funds use subscription lines, NAV facilities, and CLO/back-leverage — each a different kind of leverage-on-leverage.** A subscription line (capital-call facility) is secured against **uncalled investor commitments**, not fund assets, and is typically the cheapest, shortest-dated form of fund leverage [Confirmed, P140]. A NAV facility is instead secured against the **value of the fund's existing investment portfolio**, is more common later in a fund's life once commitments are mostly called, and can be collateralized by future distributions — ILPA published specific 2024 guidance for limited partners on how to scrutinize these because they add leverage that is not obvious in headline "committed capital" figures [Confirmed, P141]. **Back-leverage**, often structured as a **total return swap (TRS)** or as a special-purpose-vehicle "drop-down"/CLO-warehouse facility, lets a direct lender post only a fraction of a loan's cost while a bank funds the rest and the lender still receives the full economic return, magnifying gains and losses [Reported, P135, P149]. Moody's estimated **US bank lending to private credit funds reached about $300 billion as of June 2025**, a category growing faster than any other bank lending line since 2016 [Reported, from earlier search synthesis, corroborating P134's back-leverage discussion]. In 2026 banks began pulling back and repricing this exposure: reported spreads on back-leverage facilities rose to **as much as 200 basis points over SOFR, up from roughly 175–180bps**, and JPMorgan was reported to have curbed some lending after marking down certain loans citing AI-related disruption [Reported, P134, PAYWALLED-NOT-READ in full at Bloomberg — corroborated by the independent Mayer Brown (P135) and debtserious (P149) descriptions of the same back-leverage mechanics, though those two do not confirm the specific $300bn/200bps figures]. Private-credit CLOs (also called middle-market CLOs) are a related, securitized form of the same idea — bundling private loans and issuing tranched debt against them — and now make up roughly **11% of the broader $1.3 trillion CLO market**, with the **covenant-lite share of new middle-market CLO collateral rising to about 25% in Q1 2025, up from about 16% in 2021-vintage deals** [Reported, from earlier search synthesis]. The Federal Reserve has also studied private credit and leveraged loans as partial substitutes for each other, relevant to how leverage migrates between bank and non-bank channels [Reported, P136 — title only, not fully fetched].

**Leverage at the investor level** compounds all of the above: an institutional allocator or wealth platform that itself borrows (or uses a fund-of-funds structure with its own subscription line) to increase exposure to a levered BDC that has back-levered its loan book is exposed to three stacked layers of leverage before considering the leverage inside the underlying borrower — the “leverage on leverage” framing used by regulators and commentators in 2026 [Inferred from the leverage layers documented individually above; the explicit three/four-layer framing is this analyst's synthesis, not a single cited source].

## 1.7 The illiquidity premium: real compensation, or an artefact of not marking to market?

This is a live, named, dated debate, not a settled question.

**The critics' case, led by Cliff Asness (AQR co-founder), March 25, 2026.** Asness argues "not marking something doesn't make it low risk" — private assets look less volatile mainly because they are repriced infrequently, not because their underlying economics are actually more stable, a phenomenon he had earlier labelled (in private equity) "volatility laundering." His sharper, newer claim is that the **illiquidity premium has effectively inverted**: historically, investors demanded extra return to accept illiquidity; today, he argues, many investors *want* the illiquidity because it lets them avoid observing (and being forced to react to) interim volatility — treating a bug (you can't sell it) as a feature (you don't have to watch it fall), even though this can come at the cost of lower true long-run performance. On private credit specifically, he argues reported volatility is unrealistically low and that deferring defaults through amend-and-extend-type modifications (rather than forcing a formal default) artificially suppresses the apparent default rate — directly linking the valuation problem (§5) and the shadow-default problem (§4) to the return-premium question [Confirmed, P131]. AQR's own capital market assumptions research, per the same piece, now suggests private equity's *expected* return may be at or below listed equity — i.e., no illiquidity premium going forward — though Asness stresses this is a point estimate with wide uncertainty and is about private equity, not private credit specifically [Confirmed, P131].

**The academic quantification of the smoothing effect.** A named August 2026 paper by Saiee Katarkar, "Volatility Laundering in Private Credit: How Appraisal-Based Valuation Masks Economic Risk in Global Credit Markets," argues Level 3 quarterly appraisal-based marks with limited secondary-market checks create artificially smooth reported returns, and — because a loan's upside is capped at par (unlike private equity, which can appreciate without limit) — the smoothing mostly operates by **deferring bad news**: missed covenants, PIK elections, and stale discount rates that do not move even as public market yields do [Reported, P130]. A separate, independently cited paper (Hoyeon Kang, June 16, 2026) applies a standard academic "unsmoothing" correction (the Geltner method, commonly used to de-smooth appraisal-based real estate indices) to CDLI and finds an autoregressive serial-correlation coefficient of **0.29**, implying quarterly volatility is **understated by about 26%**; the worst drawdown deepens from **-6.68% to -9.00%** once unsmoothed, and the Sharpe ratio falls from **1.39 to 1.03** [Reported, P130]. Even after this adjustment, the same analysis concludes the distortion in CDLI is "much smaller than...other private asset classes" — i.e., the smoothing critique bites, but by the critics' own numbers it bites less hard on senior secured direct lending than on private equity [Reported, P130].

**The industry's defence.** Cliffwater's own materials point to CDLI's daily mark process (a formula using a public-market beta adjustment, according to the same secondary reporting) as different from — and less smoothed than — typical private equity marking, and to CDLI's realized-loss track record (a cumulative **1.01% loss rate versus 1.49% for high-yield bonds over 20 years**, i.e. about a third lower) as evidence the outperformance is not purely a marking artefact [Reported, P130, citing Cliffwater's own comparison]. AllianceBernstein frames illiquidity as "a feature, not a flaw" for long-horizon investors who do not need daily liquidity and can therefore rationally accept a locked-up structure in exchange for a genuine yield pickup, rather than treating illiquidity purely as a cost to be compensated [Reported, P133]. The balanced synthesis found in the Swedroe piece: volatility suppression in private credit is real but "relatively modest" specifically for **senior, secured loans backed by private equity sponsors** — the debate is less about whether the cash flows exist (they do; they are largely contractual interest) and more about **the timing and magnitude of loss recognition**, i.e. whether losses show up in the mark when they happen or years later when they can no longer be deferred [Reported, P130].

# 6. WHAT A BEGINNER MUST UNDERSTAND FROM THIS

1. **The manager gets paid on the size of the balance sheet, not (mainly) on how good the loans turn out to be.** A 1.5% fee on gross assets means a manager who borrows more money to buy more loans earns a bigger fee even before a single dollar of profit is proven out — and Ares Capital's own filed agreement shows this is not a caricature: 1.50% on gross assets, stepping down only above 200% of NAV. **Best worked example:** compare a $1bn-NAV BDC with no leverage (fee base = $1bn, fee = $15m) to the same BDC at the legal maximum 2:1 leverage (fee base = $3bn, fee up to ~$40-45m blended) — same equity, same manager skill required, nearly triple the fee dollars. This is why the brief calls the gross-vs-net-assets distinction one that "matters enormously."

2. **A private credit loan cannot make you rich the way a private equity stake can — its return is capped, so the game is about not losing, not about winning big.** The gross return is reference rate (SOFR) + credit spread + fee/OID income, full stop — there is no "IPO" or "10x exit" waiting at the end of a healthy loan, only par back at maturity. **Best worked example:** SOFR near 4% + a 5-6 point spread + a point or so of amortized OID gets you to roughly a 10% gross yield, which is close to what CDLI has actually delivered (9.3% in 2025, 9.5% over 20 years) — but a private equity fund's headline return is built on capital gains from selling stakes for more than was paid, an entirely different mechanism. This is the one idea that most separates the two asset classes and that every interviewer will expect a candidate to state cleanly.

3. **The headline default rate is not the whole stress picture, because lenders and borrowers have several ways to keep a struggling loan off the default tape.** Amend-and-extend, PIK conversion, and covenant waivers can all let a company avoid a formal default classification while its debt burden actually grows. **Best worked example:** Moody's found distressed restructurings made up about 65% of all 2025 private credit defaults, and its default-rate estimate for the same year ranged all the way from 1.6% to 4.7% depending purely on how those restructurings are counted — the same underlying reality can be labelled a "low" or "high" default year depending on classification choices the industry itself controls.

4. **Nobody outside the manager can independently verify a private loan's price, and when you can compare, the same loan is often priced very differently by different managers in the same quarter.** This is the single best piece of evidence for the "mark-to-model" critique, and it is dated and named, not hypothetical. **Best worked example:** in Q2 2024, Goldman Sachs BDC marked its Lithium Technologies position at 50 cents on the dollar, Hercules Capital at 53 cents, and Sixth Street Specialty Lending at 77 cents — the same loan, the same quarter, a 27-point spread in what three different boards and their valuation processes called "fair value."

5. **The extra yield ("illiquidity premium") private credit offers over public bonds is real in part, but part of what looks like lower risk is really just less frequent, model-based pricing rather than true stability — and even the industry's own defenders now concede this.** **Best worked example:** an independent academic "unsmoothing" exercise applied to Cliffwater's own index found its true quarterly volatility is understated by roughly 26%, deepening the worst historical drawdown from -6.68% to -9.00% and cutting the Sharpe ratio from 1.39 to 1.03 once the smoothing is corrected for — yet the same study concludes the distortion in senior secured direct lending is still much smaller than in private equity. The honest answer to "is the illiquidity premium real" is: partly yes, partly it is measurement, and the split between the two is exactly what analysts like Cliff Asness and academics like Saiee Katarkar are actively arguing about in 2026, not a settled fact a candidate should state with false confidence.
