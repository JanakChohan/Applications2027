# PF_RISKS — The Bear Case, the Regulators, and the Real Stress Events

## 1. FINDINGS

### 1.1 What the regulators actually say, mechanism by mechanism

**IMF — Global Financial Stability Report, April 2024, Chapter 2 ("The Rise and Risks of Private Credit").** This is the single most-cited regulatory document on the asset class and it set the template every other regulator has since followed. Published 8-16 April 2024 [P250]. The IMF's own summary is precise about the mechanism, not just the worry: credit has been migrating from "regulated banks and relatively transparent markets — such as syndicated loans or corporate bond markets — to the opaque world of private credit." It names five specific vulnerabilities: (1) relatively fragile borrowers — private credit borrowers are typically smaller and carry more debt than similarly-rated syndicated-loan or public-bond borrowers, so they are more exposed to a rate or demand shock; (2) a growing share of semi-liquid vehicles offering periodic redemptions against illiquid loans; (3) multiple layers of leverage (fund-level leverage on top of portfolio-company leverage); (4) stale and potentially subjective valuations, because marks are set by the manager rather than a market price; and (5) unclear interconnections between banks, insurers, and private credit vehicles. Its headline conclusion is carefully hedged: "immediate financial stability risks from private credit appear to be limited," but the "rapid growth of this opaque and highly interconnected segment of the financial system could heighten financial vulnerabilities given its limited oversight" [P250, Confirmed].

**Financial Stability Board — Report on Vulnerabilities in Private Credit, 6 May 2026.** [P251, Confirmed, FETCHED]. This is the most current cross-border regulatory assessment and it is more quantified than the IMF's 2024 chapter. It sizes the global private credit market at "$1.5–2.0 trillion in assets at end-2024" — already a lower and more conservative figure than the commonly cited $1.5–2.5 trillion vendor estimates, illustrating the definitional problem regulators themselves flag. Its named mechanisms: (a) **leverage** — "opaque, multi-layered structures" at both the fund and portfolio-company level, with private credit borrowers showing "higher leverage than borrowers observed in comparable public markets"; (b) **bank interconnection** — it puts a number on this that no earlier report had: across FSB member jurisdictions, "direct exposures of around $220 billion of drawn and undrawn bank credit lines to private credit funds," while unofficial commercial estimates range $270–500 billion, and banks separately extend revolving credit facilities to the same operating companies that borrow from private credit funds, so a single company's stress can hit a bank twice, once through the fund-level credit line and once through the direct corporate facility; (c) **liquidity mismatch** — "the growing popularity of funds offering redemption options to investors...may heighten the procyclicality of private credit," i.e., a semi-liquid vehicle facing redemptions may be forced to sell its most liquid or best-performing assets first, degrading what is left for remaining investors; (d) **sector concentration** — lending is concentrated in "technology, healthcare, and services, complicating surveillance and increasing the risk that a firm- or sector-specific shock turns into broader market stress"; and (e) **data gaps** — differences in definitions across jurisdictions and a lack of loan-level data make it hard for any single regulator to see the whole picture. The FSB's remedy is not a capital rule but a data and coordination programme: close data gaps, harmonise definitions, and have banking and securities supervisors share what they see [P251, Confirmed].

**Bank of England — Financial Stability Reports (2025–2026) and the market-wide stress test.** The Bank's published FSRs (July 2025, December 2025, July 2026) describe the same core problem as the IMF and FSB — "high leverage, opacity and potential conflicts of interest around valuations, and strong interconnections with riskier credit markets such as leveraged loans, which could amplify economic and financial shocks" — but the Bank's most concrete step is procedural rather than a report: in December 2025 it announced a **System-Wide Exploratory Scenario (SWES)** covering the $16 trillion global private equity and private credit industries jointly, running through 2026, with only aggregate (not firm-level) results due in early 2027 [P252, Confirmed]. This is explicitly modelled on the Bank's earlier 2023 SWES of banks, insurers, hedge funds and asset managers around gilt-market liquidity, extended now to a sector it judges too opaque to assess through data collection alone. The exercise asks three specific questions: what happens to UK private-market assets in a global downturn; whether institutional investors and banks pull back lending in a downturn in a way that amplifies stress; and whether the *related* public markets that private credit depends on for pricing and refinancing — leveraged loans, CLOs, high-yield bonds — keep functioning during the stress [P252, Confirmed, FETCHED]. This third question is the sophisticated one: the Bank is not asking "will private credit funds default," it is asking whether private credit's need for the broadly-syndicated and CLO markets to stay open (for refinancing, for price discovery, for exits) creates a channel through which private-market stress could spill into public markets that are themselves systemically important.

**FCA — Private Market Valuation Practices review, 5 March 2025.** [P253, Confirmed, FETCHED via Skadden summary]. The FCA surveyed 36 UK firms (private equity, venture capital, private debt and infrastructure managers) in two phases: a broad information request, then in-depth document review and on-site visits at a subset. It found genuinely good practice in several areas (investor reporting, documentation, use of third-party valuers, consistency of methodology), which matters for fairness to the industry. But its central finding was about **process failure at the moment it matters most**: most firms had no formal process for *ad hoc* valuations between their normal quarterly cycle. That means if a portfolio company suffers a sudden, material adverse event, the fund's official NAV does not move until the next scheduled valuation date — which the FCA states directly creates "a risk of stale valuations." The FCA also catalogued seven specific conflict-of-interest channels where the person or committee setting the valuation has an incentive to set it favourably: management fees that scale with NAV; transfer pricing when an asset moves between funds managed by the same firm; NAV-based subscription and redemption pricing (an investor who redeems today gets the stale NAV, diluting those who stay, or vice versa); using unrealised marks in fundraising materials; loan-to-value covenants on fund-level borrowing that are calculated off the same marks; a general institutional pressure to show smooth, low-volatility performance; and staff compensation tied to valuations. The FCA's own words: "robust valuation practices are important for fairness and confidence in private markets" and firms should demonstrate "independence, expertise, transparency and consistency" [P253, Confirmed].

**Bank for International Settlements.** The BIS's annual report work (flagged in the already-established brief background, 28 June 2026) sits inside the same NBFI (non-bank financial intermediation) framework as the FSB report, since the FSB's private credit work is itself part of the BIS-hosted Basel process on NBFI risk; the BIS's institutional contribution has been to push the "look at the whole intermediation chain" methodology — tracing how leverage accumulates not just within a private credit fund but across the fund, its investors, and its own lenders — which is the same logic the ECB later applied quantitatively (below) [P251, Confirmed as cross-referenced by the FSB report which BIS's Basel-based committees feed into].

**SEC — Form PF amendments.** [P256, Confirmed]. The SEC and CFTC jointly adopted amendments to Form PF (the confidential systemic-risk reporting form filed by registered private fund advisers) in February 2024, requiring more granular reporting on AUM, redemption/withdrawal rights, gross and net asset value, inflows/outflows, borrowings and types of creditors, beneficial ownership and fund performance. Tellingly, the compliance date has been pushed back three times — most recently from October 2026 to July 2027 — and a further round of 2026 proposed amendments would actually *raise* reporting thresholds and *eliminate* private equity quarterly reporting, i.e., the direction of travel under the current SEC leadership is toward less private-fund disclosure, not more, even as the private credit-specific reporting section that commenters asked for (loan-level leverage, financing counterparties, loan maturity, credit quality) was requested for comment but not adopted [P256, Confirmed]. This is a live regulatory tension: the FSB and IMF are calling for closing data gaps at the same moment the US primary regulator is easing the private-fund reporting regime that would close them.

**ESMA / ECB.** The ECB's Financial Stability Review, May 2026, contains a dedicated special feature, "Stress in global private credit markets and its implications for euro area financial stability" [P254, Confirmed, FETCHED]. This is the most quantified regulator-published transmission-channel analysis found in this research. The ECB models euro-area banks' *direct* private credit exposure at €62.5 billion — just 0.2% of total bank assets, or 2.5% of total bank equity — and states that in its stress scenario, "banks' losses from private credit remain contained in all three stages – not exceeding 1.3% of total equity." It identifies three distinct bank-to-private-credit channels: lending to the investors who buy fund stakes, lending to the funds themselves (subscription lines, NAV facilities), and direct co-lending to the same portfolio companies. Insurers hold roughly €211 billion (2.3% of assets) and pension funds roughly €52 billion (1.4% of assets), with holdings notably concentrated in Germany, France and the Netherlands. Critically, the ECB's own scenario finds that the *largest* transmission channel to the real economy is not direct private credit losses at all, but "second-round valuation losses" hitting insurers and pension funds through their *public equity* holdings once private credit stress spreads into leveraged loan and high-yield spreads — i.e., private credit stress matters most as an amplifier of broader public-market stress, not as a standalone shock [P254, Confirmed]. This is a genuinely nuanced, evidence-based defence-relevant finding: it says the direct exposure is small, but it does not say the indirect, market-wide channel is small.

**OFR (US Office of Financial Research).** The OFR's institutional framing, echoed in a March 2026 brief "Measuring Counterparty Exposures to Private Credit," is explicit that data gaps are the core problem it exists to fix: "a substantial fraction of credit extended appears to be high risk, the performance of which is difficult to assess because limited data are collected from these nonbank lenders," and that "counterparty exposures between banks and private credit funds are the main channel for risk transmission" even though "vulnerabilities within this sector appear contained" [P257, Confirmed]. This mirrors the ECB's conclusion almost exactly: contained today, but the bank-counterparty channel is the one to watch, and it is under-measured.

**NAIC (US insurance regulator, state-based).** [P255, Confirmed, FETCHED]. The NAIC's own private credit topic page states plainly that these holdings carry a "lack of a robust secondary market and, therefore, less liquidity," and difficulty in pricing due to "lack of transparency and infrequent valuations" — the insurance-sector mirror of the FCA's valuation concern. It says "recent developments have underscored that these risks are no longer merely theoretical," citing 2026's elevated redemption requests and stress in AI-exposed software borrowers. The concrete regulatory response: the NAIC's Valuation of Securities Task Force in 2025 began requiring Private Rating Letter Rationale Reports within 90 days with "analytical substance" (i.e., no longer accepting a bare rating without the reasoning behind it), and separately the Life Risk-Based Capital Working Group has been developing new capital charges for CLO tranches rated BBB and below, moving away from simply trusting the published credit rating and toward the NAIC's own internal stress modelling of CLO tranche losses, with full implementation targeted for December 2026 [P255; NAIC CLO RBC work also corroborated by industry press, Confirmed]. Separately, industry reporting puts total private illiquid bond holdings across the US life insurance industry at $807 billion at year-end 2025, up $122 billion in a single year, with the ten largest life insurers holding 44% of that total [P283, Reported] — a concentration figure that matters because it means insurer-sector stress, if it came, would be concentrated in a small number of very large balance sheets tied economically to the same private credit managers that sponsor their annuity products (see 1.4 circularity, below).

### 1.2 The named critics — exact words, exact dates

**Jamie Dimon, JPMorgan Chase CEO — the "cockroaches" remark.** [P258, Confirmed, FETCHED]. Said on JPMorgan's Q3 2025 earnings call, 15 October 2025: "I probably shouldn't say this, but when you see one cockroach, there are probably more. And so we should — everyone should be forewarned on this one." The direct trigger was twofold and concrete, not a general anxiety: JPMorgan had just written off $170 million tied to the collapse of subprime auto lender Tricolor, and auto-parts supplier First Brands (which had borrowed more than $10 billion across bank, syndicated and non-bank channels) had just filed for bankruptcy amid fraud allegations. JPMorgan itself had no *direct* exposure to First Brands, which is part of Dimon's point: the risk is that failures like this are the visible tip of underwriting practices that are not visible until something breaks. Market reaction was measurable: the CBOE VIX spiked to a near six-month high of 25.31 on 16 October 2025, and in the six months following the remark the listed private credit index fell 17.7% [P258, Reported for the index-move figure — single-source, worth corroborating]. Other bank executives pushed back the same reporting cycle: JPMorgan CFO Jeremy Barnum said "a lot of the private credit actors are large, very sophisticated, very good at credit underwriting"; Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon said "the vast majority of our lending is collateralized financing and investment-grade rated structures"; Citi CFO Mark Mason said "we're maintaining collateral pools that are well-diversified with concentration limits" [P258, Confirmed, FETCHED].

**Mohamed El-Erian.** [P259, Reported]. El-Erian's most pointed 2026 warning came after Blue Owl Capital's OCIC/OTIC redemption episode (already established in the shared brief), which he said contained "hints of 2007" — an explicit comparison to BNP Paribas's August 2007 freeze of three funds, the episode widely regarded as the opening act of the global financial crisis. His specific mechanism, which he calls the "ATM scenario," is a liquidity-contagion argument distinct from a credit-loss argument: if a private credit vehicle gates or caps redemptions, investors who need cash are forced to sell whatever *can* be sold — often their healthy, liquid holdings elsewhere — rather than what they want to sell, because the private vehicle itself is illiquid by design. "If you can't sell what you want, you sell what you can" is the mechanism; it is a portfolio-liquidity-management problem that can transmit stress to *unrelated, healthy* assets, which is precisely the kind of systemic channel that a pure credit-loss analysis would miss. He has separately flagged private credit's expansion into consumer debt, credit cards, and buy-now-pay-later as expansion "into riskier areas outside their traditional expertise" [P259, Reported — sourced via Yahoo Finance aggregation of El-Erian's public commentary and social media; underlying primary quotes not independently fetched, so treat as Reported not Confirmed].

**Moody's and rating-agency commentary.** Moody's Analytics published "Private Credit & Systemic Risk," dated 2 June 2025 [P271, Reported — PDF content could not be extracted despite fetch attempt; title, date and general subject confirmed via search metadata only, mark PAYWALLED-NOT-READ in effect]. Separately reported Moody's commentary in 2026 describes private credit AUM heading toward "almost $4 trillion by 2030" while flagging that the sector is "leaning on financial innovation tools such as structured credit, rated fund structures, NAV lending and PIK loans to address demand for alternative liquidity funding" — language that reads as regulatory-adjacent code for "using increasingly complex tools to avoid recognising stress" [P274, Reported]. Fitch (not Moody's, but the same ratings-agency tier) is more pointed: Fitch's own broader default measure put the US private credit default rate at 5.8% for the twelve months to January 2026, "the highest since Fitch began tracking it," rising further to 6.0% by April 2026 — several multiples of the ~2.5% Proskauer index figure already established in the shared brief, and the gap between the two figures is itself the story: Proskauer's index tracks formal, payment defaults on senior-secured/unitranche loans, while Fitch's broader definition appears to capture selective defaults and liability-management exercises (see PIK and amend-and-extend, below) that never show up as a formal payment default [P274, Reported].

**Bank of America.** Reported as calling private credit "the lowest quality asset class across our leveraged finance universe" [P274, Reported, single-source attribution via search aggregation, not independently fetched from a BofA primary document — treat with caution].

**Academic critics — the valuation-smoothing / "volatility laundering" literature.** [P271, P272, Reported]. The core academic claim, echoed by the IMF itself, is that private credit NAVs are marked to model at the manager's discretion and updated only quarterly, which can "smooth reported returns, suppress volatility, and inflate Sharpe ratios" relative to an economically equivalent public-market instrument that reprices daily. The empirical evidence cited: private credit fund secondaries (stakes sold on the secondary market before the fund's natural end) traded at average discounts of 9–10% below stated NAV in 2024 — a discount of roughly 2 percentage points per year compounded, which the critics argue is "enough to eliminate most or all of the alpha estimated for broad indexes" once you strip out the smoothing effect [P271, Reported]. A specific August 2026 academic paper, described in secondary coverage as "Volatility Laundering in Private Credit," has renewed this debate [P271, Reported — primary paper not independently located/fetched]. The rebuttal from the industry side (Cliffwater, index providers) is that even after adjusting for smoothing, realised losses over multiple credit cycles remain low relative to income, addressed in section 1.5 below.

**Short sellers.** Hindenburg Research, the most prominent equity short-selling research firm of the prior decade, disbanded in January 2025 and is not active on private credit [P279, Confirmed]. The live short-side voice is Carson Block of Muddy Waters Research, who — notably — pivoted from equity shorting into an explicit *credit* short strategy in April 2026, arguing that AI-driven job displacement will hit corporate credit quality broadly and that this is underpriced in credit spreads, private credit included [P278, Reported]. This is a distinct thesis from the valuation-opacity critique: it is a forward-looking macro call on AI's effect on cash flows, not a claim about mismeasurement of the current state.

**Senator Elizabeth Warren.** Not a market critic but a formal regulatory-political one, and material to the "who is watching" question. On 17 July 2025 Warren wrote to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and to every major rating agency (Moody's, S&P, Fitch, Egan-Jones, Morningstar DBRS, AM Best, Demotech, KBRA), citing reports that private credit ratings were being "inflated," and stated directly: "Inaccurate credit ratings notoriously contributed to the 2008 financial crisis." She separately called for the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) to design and run a stress test of non-bank institutions engaged in private credit [P270, Confirmed via primary Senate Banking Committee letters — letters located but not independently fetched in full; treat text quoted as Reported pending direct read].

### 1.3 The defenders — exact arguments

**Marc Rowan, Apollo Global Management CEO.** The most vocal and specific defender. [P261, Confirmed, FETCHED — remarks of 24 November 2025, during Apollo's Q3 earnings presentation]. His core arguments: (1) **scale-mismatch framing** — critics are "fixating on a tiny sliver of risky loans and treating it as if it represents a $40 trillion market," and "people have really just lost their minds, and the headlines get more and more hysterical and have almost nothing to do with the substance"; (2) **quality composition** — almost all private credit held by insurers and pension funds carries investment-grade ratings, and investors typically reach it by rotating *up* the capital structure and *down* in volatility from equities or high-yield bonds, not by taking on new risk; (3) **transparency reversal** — Rowan argues private credit is actually *more* transparent to the lender than public bonds, because private lenders receive non-public financials, tighter covenants and direct management access, while public bondholders get thinner periodic disclosure; (4) **liquidity evidence** — Apollo has traded roughly $6 billion of its investment-grade private credit book, and the Apollo/State Street ETF vehicle that holds similar assets publishes a daily price. On redemption mechanics specifically (16 April 2026, CNBC-reported, not independently fetched due to a 403 error — Reported not Confirmed): Rowan reportedly said any manager that could not meet 5% redemption caps was, in his words, "an idiot" — a pointed claim that the industry-standard cap structure (5% of NAV per quarter, as in the already-established Blue Owl/BDC episodes) is generous enough that hitting it should not happen to a well-run fund [P261 for the confirmed 24 Nov remarks; the "idiot" quote is P258-adjacent Reported only]. Rowan has also directly rejected UBS Chairman Colm Kelleher's systemic-risk warnings: "Colm is just wrong" [P261, Confirmed, FETCHED].

**Michael Arougheti, Ares Management CEO.** Reported (not independently fetched) as calling UBS's worst-case 13–15% private credit default forecast (see 1.5) "absolutely wrong" and "irresponsible" [P269-adjacent, Reported].

**Jon Gray, Blackstone President.** Defended Blackstone's non-traded BDC (BCRED) with specific portfolio metrics: more than 400 borrowers, 10% average portfolio-company EBITDA growth, 95% of positions senior secured, and an average loan-to-value of 42% — i.e., even if the enterprise value of the average borrower fell by more than half, the loan would still theoretically be covered by collateral [P280, Reported].

**Marc Lipschultz, Blue Owl Capital co-CEO.** Not independently fetched in this research pass (search budget was exhausted before a dedicated query could be run); the already-established brief material on Blue Owl's OCIC/OTIC episode and its "AI-related disruption to software companies" 8-K wording of 2 April 2026 should be treated as the primary Blue Owl-side disclosure, with Lipschultz's public defence a gap — see Section 5.

### 1.4 The actual credit events

**First Brands Group — the clearest lesson on off-balance-sheet financing and lender due diligence.** [P262, P263, P264, Confirmed/Reported, multiple sources FETCHED]. First Brands filed for Chapter 11 on **28 September 2025**. It was an aggressive acquirer in the aftermarket auto-parts industry. The actuarial case study fetched in this research gives the clearest capital-structure breakdown found anywhere in this search: **total debt approximately $11.5 billion**, split between **$6.1 billion on-balance-sheet** (a $5.5 billion broadly syndicated loan plus $0.6 billion of asset-backed financing) and **$5.4 billion off-balance-sheet**, structured across **112 separate bankruptcy-remote special-purpose entities** doing receivable factoring and inventory/supply-chain financing [P263, Reported]. As of 2024 year-end, an estimated 70% of First Brands' revenue had been factored through these structures, per Bloomberg reporting cited in the actuarial summary [P263, Reported, single-source-relayed]. The mechanism that made this dangerous: supply-chain finance and factoring are legally structured as a "true sale" of receivables to a separate entity, which means the resulting debt does not appear on the operating company's own balance sheet or in its leverage covenants — so a lender doing standard leverage-ratio due diligence on the visible balance sheet would systematically understate the company's real indebtedness. First Brands' founder was separately accused of causing the company to incur billions in additional liabilities based on non-existent or doctored invoices, and of double-pledging the same collateral across multiple financing arrangements [prior established search, Reported]. AIMA's industry-side rebuttal, fetched directly, makes an important factual distinction: "the majority of FBG reported debt is not private credit" — First Brands drew on banks, broadly syndicated loan investors and non-bank lenders across several structures, and AIMA's point is that the losses are concentrated in supply-chain-finance and CLO/broadly-syndicated exposures rather than in true, direct-origination private credit funds that hold loans to maturity [P262, Reported — note this is an industry trade body's characterisation and should be weighed as advocacy, not a neutral audit]. The lesson the market drew, stated by the actuarial case study directly: lenders must do "look-through" analysis of true leverage, collateral quality and payment seniority rather than trusting the headline balance sheet; the absence of meaningful covenants removed the early-warning mechanism that would otherwise have caught deteriorating credit quality sooner [P263, Reported]. UBS separately disclosed exposure of more than $500 million through supply-chain financing agreements with First Brands [prior established search result, Reported].

**Tricolor Holdings — subprime auto, fraud, and the "cockroach" trigger.** [P265, P266, Confirmed]. Tricolor, a combined used-car dealer and subprime auto lender extending high-interest loans to borrowers with little or no credit history (68% of its borrowers had no credit score in one bond-deal disclosure), filed for **Chapter 7 bankruptcy on 10 September 2025**. At its peak it had more than 60,000 outstanding car loans and roughly $1 billion in annual revenue. On 18 August 2026, the SEC charged three former executives — CEO Daniel Chu, CFO Jerome Kollar, and Senior Director of Finance Ameryn Seibold — with a multi-year fraud scheme: **double-pledging hundreds of millions of dollars of the same subprime auto loans** to multiple asset-backed securitisation deals and lenders simultaneously, while manipulating loan-performance metrics so that non-paying or already-defaulted loans appeared current and eligible for inclusion in ABS collateral pools [P266, Confirmed, per SEC press release]. JPMorgan took a **$170 million loss** tied to Tricolor, disclosed in October 2025 — the loss that directly preceded Dimon's cockroach remark. Fifth Third Bank and Barclays were also reported as exposed, in the "half-billion-dollar range" for larger creditors collectively, though per-institution figures beyond JPMorgan's $170 million were not independently confirmed in primary documents during this research [Reported, not Confirmed for the non-JPMorgan figures]. The lesson: double-pledging of collateral is a classic securitisation-era fraud pattern (structurally similar to the same-collateral, multiple-claims problem that worsened the 2008 mortgage crisis), and it succeeded here for years without being caught by any single lender's own diligence, because no single lender could see the whole collateral pool across all of Tricolor's funding relationships.

**Pluralsight — the clean NAV-based-rescue case study.** [P267, P268, Confirmed, FETCHED]. Pluralsight, an online technology-skills training company, had been taken private by Vista Equity Partners in 2021 with **over $1.5 billion of acquisition debt**. By late 2023 it was straining under higher rates, rising competition, and weakening demand. In early summer 2024 Vista attempted a liability-management exercise: moving intellectual property into a new restricted subsidiary to raise fresh secured debt and fund an interest payment — a delaying tactic that ultimately proved insufficient. In **August 2024**, the lender consortium — led by **Blue Owl Capital and Ares Management**, with participation from Golub Capital, Oaktree, Benefit Street Partners, Goldman Sachs BDC, and BlackRock TCP Capital — converted **$1.2–1.3 billion of debt into equity**, took **100% ownership** of the company (Vista's equity was wiped to zero and it exited entirely), and injected **roughly $250–275 million** of new capital to fund the business going forward. Blue Owl's own BDC family held the largest single exposure at roughly $250 million combined across seven of its vehicles, out of an estimated $700 million total BDC exposure to the credit. KBRA's rating-agency assessment, issued around the restructuring, is directly relevant to the "how big a deal is this really" question: because the exposure was split across many rated BDC portfolios, no single rated BDC held more than roughly 1% of its portfolio in the name, and KBRA concluded "no effect on ratings expected" — a clean illustration of the diversification-works argument even in a total-loss-of-equity, lender-takes-the-keys outcome [P267, P268, Confirmed]. The forward-looking market lesson, drawn directly by the professionals covering the deal: "the days of amend-and-extend may well be waning" — non-bank lenders increasingly prefer outright equity ownership of a distressed borrower, because as the direct owner they can control and maximise the value of a turnaround rather than simply deferring the problem [P268, Reported].

**Vice Media and Instant Brands — earlier-cycle, non-2025/26 precedents.** Vice Media Group filed Chapter 11 with $834 million in debt; a consortium of former lenders including Fortress Investment Group, Soros Fund Management and Monroe Capital ultimately bought the company via a $225 million credit bid (2023) [Reported, prior established search]. Instant Brands (maker of Instant Pot and Pyrex) filed Chapter 11 on 12 June 2023 with $512.3 million of outstanding funded debt, citing tighter credit and higher rates; it was recapitalised under lender control with the appliances division later sold to Centre Lane Partners [Reported, prior established search]. Both predate the 2025–2026 stress episodes and are useful mainly as evidence that "lender takes the company" outcomes are not new to this cycle — private credit and direct-lending consortia had already been resolving distressed credits this way for several years before First Brands and Tricolor made headlines.

**2026 events.** The clearest 2026-dated events found are regulatory and market-structure, rather than new individual borrower failures beyond what is already established in the shared brief (Blue Owl OCIC/OTIC, Partners Group gate, the 10-of-16 BDC breach data): the UBS worst-case default forecast (raised from ~13% to ~14–15% between early and mid-February 2026, explicitly for an AI-disruption scenario hitting software borrowers), the SEC's Tricolor executive charges (18 August 2026), the FSB's May 2026 report, the ECB's May 2026 special feature, and the Bank of England's December 2025-announced, 2026-running system-wide stress test. No further individually named 2026 corporate-borrower failure on the scale of First Brands or Tricolor was found in this research pass; this is flagged as a gap in Section 5, since the brief specifically asks for 2026 events.

### 1.5 The specific risk mechanisms, explained plainly

- **Valuation opacity and stale marks.** A private credit loan has no daily market price. The manager sets its own "mark" each quarter, using models and judgement rather than an observable trade. If nothing bad has happened, this is a reasonable simplification. If something bad *has* happened between valuation dates, the FCA's own finding is that most managers had no formal process to update the mark immediately — so the NAV an investor sees, and the price at which they can subscribe or redeem, can be stale by up to a full quarter [P253, Confirmed]. This directly feeds return-smoothing: because bad news arrives with a lag, and because the manager has some discretion even when marks do update, reported volatility looks lower than the true economic volatility of the underlying loans — precisely what the academic "volatility laundering" critique claims [P271, Reported].

- **PIK (payment-in-kind) as deferred recognition of stress.** A loan can pay interest in cash, or it can let the borrower add unpaid interest to the loan's principal balance instead ("payment-in-kind"). PIK is a legitimate tool for a borrower with temporary cash-flow strain, but it also means a borrower who genuinely cannot afford its debt does not show up as defaulted — the lender simply lets the balance grow. The already-established brief data (PIK rising from 5.9% of BDC income in 2023 to about 8% in 2026) and the 2026 reporting that public BDCs are "now receiving an average of 8% of investment income via PIK" both point the same way: a rising PIK share is read across the market as a leading indicator of borrower stress that has not yet, and may never, show up in a formal default statistic [P278, Reported].

- **Amend-and-extend as a way of avoiding formal default.** When a borrower cannot meet its obligations, the lender (who often would rather not force a fire-sale write-down of a hard-to-mark loan) can simply extend the maturity, relax a covenant, or restructure terms — "amend and extend" — rather than declaring default. This keeps the loan off default statistics while the underlying credit quality may not have improved at all. This is precisely why analysts distinguish a headline default rate (Proskauer's, based on formal payment default, ~2.5% through 2026) from a "true" default rate that credit analysts estimate closer to 5% once amend-and-extend and other liability-management exercises are counted [P274-adjacent, Reported]. The Pluralsight case shows the *end* of this road: professionals covering that deal explicitly said "the days of amend-and-extend may well be waning," because lenders are increasingly choosing to take equity ownership outright rather than keep extending [P268, Reported].

- **Covenant erosion and EBITDA add-backs.** A financial covenant (e.g., a maximum leverage ratio, tested quarterly) is an early-warning system: it forces a conversation between lender and borrower before things get worse. "Covenant-lite" loans drop this protection entirely. As competition for large, sponsor-backed deals intensified, terms loosened broadly — the segment where covenant erosion is most severe is large-cap and upper-middle-market sponsor deals, whereas true middle-market direct loans still typically retain at least one maintenance covenant, reflecting tighter, more bilateral documentation [P281, Reported]. Compounding this, "EBITDA add-backs" — adjustments lenders and sponsors agree to add back to a company's reported earnings to reflect a rosier post-synergy, post-cost-cutting future — can inflate the apparent debt capacity a company qualifies for at origination, meaning the leverage ratio calculated at the time of the loan understates the company's true, current debt burden relative to its true, current cash flow. First Brands' actuarial case study explicitly flags the "absence of meaningful covenants" as removing the early-warning mechanism that should have flagged deteriorating credit sooner [P263, Reported].

- **Concentration in a small number of very large managers.** The ten largest private credit managers now control more than 65% of the roughly $1.7 trillion global market, and in 2025 the ten largest fund closes alone captured 46% of all capital raised that year [P280, Reported]. Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, Blue Owl, HPS, KKR, Golub, Oaktree, Sixth Street and Antares each individually manage over $100 billion in credit assets. This matters for systemic risk in two ways: first, a problem at any one of these managers (a valuation scandal, a run on an evergreen vehicle, a bad underwriting cycle) is large enough in absolute dollar terms to matter to the wider financial system on its own; second, concentration reduces the diversity of underwriting standards and risk appetite across the market — if the largest managers converge on similarly loose terms in competing for the same large sponsor-backed deals, that correlated behaviour removes the natural diversification that a fragmented market would otherwise provide.

- **Interconnection with banks through subscription lines, NAV loans, and back leverage.** A subscription line is a bank credit facility to a fund, secured by the fund's uncalled investor commitments rather than its portfolio assets; it lets a fund draw cash quickly without formally calling capital from its limited partners, which can flatter a fund's early-vintage-year internal rate of return by shortening the time capital is shown as "at work." A NAV loan is different: it is secured by the *value of the fund's actual portfolio* rather than uncalled commitments, and is used later in a fund's life, often to fund follow-on investments, pay distributions, or provide liquidity, without having to sell underlying assets [P275, Confirmed, FETCHED]. "Back leverage" is leverage a private credit fund itself takes on (from a bank or another lender) against its loan portfolio, on top of whatever leverage the underlying portfolio companies already carry — the "multiple layers of leverage" the IMF specifically flags [P250, Confirmed]. The FSB's $220 billion figure for direct bank credit-line exposure to private credit funds, against unofficial commercial estimates of $270–500 billion, is the best available quantification of this channel, and the gap between the official and unofficial figures is itself evidence of the FSB's own point about data gaps [P251, Confirmed]. NAV lending specifically has grown extremely fast: from a market that barely existed before 2017 to one that saw a record $12.9 billion of NAV lending fund closes in 2025, including the largest fund ever raised in the strategy (17Capital's $5.5 billion Strategic Lending Fund 6) [prior established search result, Reported], with projections that the broader NAV finance market could reach ~$150 billion by 2030 from ~$50 billion currently [P275, Confirmed for structure, Reported for the specific size projection].

- **Lending to funds rather than to operating companies.** NAV lending and subscription lines are, by definition, loans where the *borrower* is a fund or its general partner, not an operating business with its own revenue. This is a structural shift in what "private credit" means: capital increasingly flows to *financial intermediaries* (funds) rather than directly to companies that make things or provide services. The risk this introduces is that the collateral backing the loan (a portfolio of other private, hard-to-value fund investments) is itself opaque and only realisable by selling down illiquid underlying stakes — a NAV lender's true recovery in a downside scenario depends on being able to value and eventually monetise assets that are, by construction, not publicly traded.

- **Circularity between PE sponsors, their portfolio companies, and affiliated lenders.** Several of the largest private credit managers (Apollo/Athene, Blackstone, Ares, KKR) also run large private equity businesses and, in some cases, own or are affiliated with insurance companies (life insurers and annuity writers) that provide a captive source of long-duration capital. This creates a closed loop: the private equity arm buys a company; the credit arm lends to that same company (or a competitor sponsor's company, in a reciprocal arrangement across managers); the insurance arm holds the resulting loan or a fund stake as an asset backing its policyholder liabilities; and fees are earned at every stage. This is not necessarily fraudulent or even improper — Rowan's own defence explicitly leans on the insurance-company angle, arguing the resulting assets are conservatively structured investment-grade credit — but it does mean that a valuation or credit problem inside the credit arm can transmit directly to the balance sheet of an affiliated insurer, and that the same firm is simultaneously the seller, structurer, valuer and (via its insurance arm) buyer of the same underlying risk, which is the textbook definition of a conflict of interest the FCA specifically flagged in its March 2025 review [P253, Confirmed].

- **Liquidity mismatch in semi-liquid ("evergreen") vehicles.** This is the mechanism already well documented in the shared brief's Blue Owl, Partners Group and BDC-cap examples, and the FSB, IMF and El-Erian all independently identify it as a priority concern: an evergreen fund offers investors periodic (monthly or quarterly) redemption windows against a portfolio of loans that cannot actually be sold quickly or at the marked price in a stress scenario. When redemption requests exceed the fund's pre-set cap (often 5% of NAV per quarter, as in SEC Rule 23c-3 interval fund limits of 5–25%, or the UK LTAF's minimum 90-day notice), the fund gates or pro-rates redemptions rather than paying out in full — which is exactly what happened at scale in 2026 (OCIC 21.9% of NAV requested, OTIC 40.7%, both capped at 5%; 10 of 16 tracked BDCs breaching caps in Q2 2026 at an average 10.3% request rate — all already established in the shared brief). The FSB's framing is that this liquidity-offering feature "may heighten the procyclicality of private credit" — meaning redemption pressure is likely to be highest exactly when asset values are under the most stress, which is the worst possible time for a fund to be a forced, even if gated, seller [P251, Confirmed].

### 1.6 The strongest defence, stated fairly

The industry's best arguments, and the evidence behind each:

1. **These are mostly senior secured, floating-rate loans with real collateral, not unsecured or subordinated risk.** Jon Gray's BCRED example — 95% senior secured, average loan-to-value of 42% — is representative of how the large, direct-lending-focused vehicles are actually structured: even a severe halving of enterprise value would still theoretically leave the loan covered by collateral at that LTV [P280, Reported]. Rowan's point that most insurer- and pension-held private credit carries investment-grade ratings is a related, if more contested, version of the same claim [P261, Confirmed].

2. **Losses have so far been modest, on a formal basis.** Proskauer's Private Credit Default Index — a purpose-built index tracking senior-secured and unitranche US loans — has moved between 1.84% and 2.73%, settling at 2.51% for Q2 2026 (already established in the shared brief), and Cliffwater's 20-year Direct Lending Index has averaged 9.5% annual returns with only one negative year (2008), with income exceeding realised losses even at the peak of stress periods across five separate credit cycles [P282, Reported]. The honest caveat, already noted above, is that "true" default rates that include amend-and-extend liability-management exercises are estimated closer to 5%, and Fitch's broader methodology shows 5.8–6.0% — so the "modest losses" claim depends heavily on which definition of default is used, and the industry's preferred index (Proskauer's) uses the narrowest one.

3. **The capital is largely locked up, so it cannot run the way bank deposits can.** This is a real structural difference and one regulators themselves accept: most institutional private credit capital sits in closed-end, multi-year-lockup drawdown funds with no redemption rights at all, which cannot experience a depositor-style overnight run by design. The genuine caveat — and the one every regulator flags — is that this defence applies with full force only to *closed-end* vehicles; it applies much more weakly to the roughly $607 billion of evergreen/semi-liquid vehicles already established in the shared brief, which do offer periodic redemption and which are exactly where the 2026 stress episodes (Blue Owl, Partners Group, the BDC cap breaches) actually occurred. So the "no bank-style run" defence is true for the majority of the asset class by AUM, but not for the specific, fastest-growing segment that is under the most current stress.

4. **Leverage is lower than at banks, and there is no maturity transformation of the bank kind.** A bank takes short-term, callable deposits and lends long-term — the definitional maturity mismatch that makes bank runs possible. A closed-end private credit fund's leverage — reported by the ECB as roughly 30% for US private credit funds and around 40% for euro-area private debt alternative investment funds [ECB/ESRB-sourced figure via search aggregation, Reported] — sits well below a typical bank's leverage (a bank often operates at 90%+ leverage relative to assets, i.e., single-digit equity-to-assets ratios), and a private credit fund does not fund long-term illiquid loans with instruly short-term, runnable liabilities the way a bank funds mortgages with demand deposits. Morgan Stanley's comparison of private credit and BDC leverage today against 2008 levels, cited by industry-side reporting, points the same way: leverage is lower now than it was in the vehicles that amplified the 2008 crisis [P274-adjacent, Reported].

5. **The asset class has already been tested by the 2022–2024 rate shock, and performed within historical norms.** The Cliffwater data — tracking close to 20,000 loans across publicly traded, non-traded and private BDC portfolios — shows non-accrual rates on both a cost and fair-value basis "comfortably under" their respective 10-year averages and not trending upward through the 2022–2024 rate-hike cycle, despite that cycle representing one of the fastest rate increases in decades and a genuine stress test for floating-rate borrowers whose interest costs rose sharply even as revenue growth slowed [P282, Reported]. This is a real data point in the industry's favour and should be weighed seriously: the single largest and most obvious macro stress private credit has faced in its modern, large-scale form did not produce a wave of realised losses.

### 1.7 The honest verdict — what would actually have to happen [Inferred]

[Inferred] Based on everything gathered above, private credit causing *investor losses* — even large ones, concentrated in specific funds, specific vintages, or specific sectors like AI-exposed software — is not the same question as private credit causing a *systemic* problem, and the regulatory documents fetched in this research are unusually consistent in drawing exactly that line themselves (IMF: "immediate financial stability risks... appear limited" but rapid, opaque growth "could heighten" vulnerability; ECB: direct bank losses "contained... not exceeding 1.3% of total equity" even in a designed stress scenario; OFR: "vulnerabilities within this sector appear contained" but the bank-counterparty channel is "the main channel for risk transmission").

[Inferred] The transmission channel that would actually have to activate for this to become systemic, rather than a sequence of investor losses, has three necessary links, all of which currently show only partial, not full, connection:

- **First**, a shock large enough and correlated enough across many borrowers simultaneously to overwhelm the loss-absorption capacity built into the senior-secured, low-headline-leverage structure described in the defence above — the AI-driven software-sector shock UBS models (13-15% worst-case default rate concentrated in one sector, roughly 35% of the market by UBS's own exposure estimate) is the most concrete candidate currently being actively discussed by serious analysts, precisely because private credit's sector concentration in technology, healthcare and services (flagged independently by the FSB) means a single-sector shock can hit a disproportionate share of the market at once, unlike a diversified public bond index.

- **Second**, that shock would need to hit the semi-liquid, redemption-offering segment of the market (currently ~$607bn, a minority of the ~$1.5-2.5tn total) hard enough, and broadly enough across managers, to produce redemption requests that break through gates at many funds simultaneously rather than in isolated cases — turning El-Erian's "ATM scenario" from a localised, contained episode (as OCIC/OTIC and Partners Group were, each individually gated and contained) into a market-wide dash for cash that forces correlated selling of the *other*, healthier assets that gated investors hold, transmitting stress well beyond private credit itself.

- **Third**, and this is the link every primary regulatory document converges on as the one actually under-measured, that stress would need to pass through the bank-counterparty channel — the $220-500bn of subscription lines, NAV facilities and back-leverage the FSB, ECB and OFR all separately flag — in a way that impairs a systemically important bank's own capital or liquidity, rather than being a private, bilateral loss absorbed quietly on a fund's own balance sheet. The ECB's own modelling suggests this link is currently weak (1.3% of euro-area bank equity in a designed stress scenario), and the FSB explicitly says its own $220bn figure is likely an underestimate given data gaps — meaning the honest answer is that regulators do not currently know with confidence how strong this third link actually is, which is precisely why the Bank of England is running a two-year system-wide exploratory scenario rather than relying on existing data collection, and why the SEC's Form PF amendments (which would have measured exactly this) have been delayed three times rather than adopted.

[Inferred] Put plainly: private credit today looks less like 2008-style bank maturity transformation and more like a large, genuinely under-measured pocket of credit risk that is mostly held by investors who cannot easily run (pension funds, insurers, endowments, locked-up drawdown vehicles) — which is a reason for real caution about investor losses and manager selection, but a specific reason for skepticism about a 2008-style systemic event, unless and until the AI-software-sector-concentration shock, the evergreen-fund liquidity mismatch, and the bank-counterparty channel all activate together rather than in isolation. Every regulator that has looked closely (IMF, FSB, ECB, BoE, OFR) has reached some version of that same conditional conclusion, and none has yet found evidence that all three links are activating simultaneously — which is also exactly why each of them is now building the specific tools (Form PF-style data, the BoE's SWES, the FSB's harmonised definitions project) to detect it early if it starts to.

## 6. WHAT A BEGINNER MUST UNDERSTAND FROM THIS

1. **A regulator saying "we are worried" and a regulator finding evidence of actual, quantified system-wide danger are two different things — and right now, every major regulator that has looked closely (IMF, FSB, ECB, OFR) is doing the first, not the second.** The best worked example: the ECB modelled its own stress scenario for private credit hitting euro-area banks and found losses would not exceed 1.3% of bank equity — a real number, from a real central bank, that is small. The same report's authors still published pages of warnings. A beginner who can hold both of those facts at once — "the number I found is small" and "the people who found it are still worried" — understands the actual state of the debate better than someone who picks one side.

2. **"Private credit is opaque" is not a vague complaint — it is a precise, three-part mechanical claim: the manager sets the price (not the market), the manager only resets that price once a quarter (not continuously), and the manager has at least seven distinct, FCA-catalogued reasons to want that price to look good rather than accurate.** The best worked example: the FCA's own review found that most firms had no formal process to update a valuation between quarters even after something bad happened to the underlying company — meaning an investor's account statement can be showing a stale, pre-bad-news price for up to three months. This is the single mechanism underneath almost every other criticism in this file (return smoothing, "volatility laundering," conflicts of interest).

3. **Every major "private credit blew up" headline in 2025-2026 traces back to two specific, largely idiosyncratic frauds — not to a broad wave of ordinary companies failing to pay their debts.** First Brands hid $5.4 billion of debt in 112 off-balance-sheet vehicles and allegedly used doctored invoices; Tricolor allegedly double-pledged the same collateral to multiple lenders for years. The best worked example: use First Brands to teach "why do lenders sometimes not see the real leverage" — because factoring and supply-chain finance are legally structured as a true sale, so the resulting debt simply does not appear on the balance sheet a lender is checking against its covenants. This is a genuinely important, teachable mechanism, and it is exactly the kind of specific example that separates a candidate who has done real homework from one reciting talking points.

4. **The most sophisticated version of the "systemic risk" argument is not "borrowers will default," it is "the redemption feature on newer, semi-liquid funds could force healthy investors to sell healthy, unrelated assets"** — El-Erian's "ATM scenario." The best worked example: contrast a traditional 10-year, no-redemption drawdown fund (which literally cannot experience a run, because there is nothing to redeem) with a monthly-dealing evergreen BDC that gated at 40.7% of NAV requested in 2026. Understanding why those two structures carry completely different liquidity risk — despite both being called "private credit" — is probably the single highest-value distinction a beginner can master, because it is the one point on which the industry's best defence (illiquid capital cannot run) and the regulators' sharpest worry (liquidity mismatch in semi-liquid vehicles) are both simultaneously true, just about different parts of the same market.

5. **The honest, defensible answer to "is this the next 2008" is: probably not, unless three specific and currently only partially connected things happen together — a big enough, correlated shock (the AI/software scenario is the live candidate); a liquidity run through the redemption-offering evergreen vehicles specifically, not the whole market; and transmission through the bank-counterparty channel (subscription lines and NAV loans) rather than staying contained on funds' own balance sheets.** The best worked example/analogy: think of private credit today as a large body of water behind several separate, mostly-sound dams (fund structures, low headline leverage, senior secured collateral, locked-up capital). The dams have some known cracks (opacity, PIK, amend-and-extend hiding true stress, covenant erosion) that make it hard to know exactly how much water is really behind each one. Regulators are not saying the dams have failed; they are building better instruments (the BoE's stress test, the FSB's data project, Form PF) precisely because they currently cannot see the cracks well enough to be sure — which is a mature, credible answer for an interview, because it is honest about the limits of what is actually known.
