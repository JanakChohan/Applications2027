# PA_ORIGINS — Where Private Credit Came From, Why It Exists, How Big It Actually Is

Source-ID range used: P1–P29 (within assigned P1–P49).

# 1. FINDINGS

## 1.1 Definition and boundary

Private credit is debt that is negotiated bilaterally (or among a small club of lenders) directly between a non-bank lender and a company, is not issued to the public, does not trade on a market, is usually held by the lender to maturity, and is usually floating rate, priced as a spread over a reference rate such as SOFR or SONIA [Confirmed, P1, P19]. The Financial Stability Board's own working definition, used across its 2026 report, is narrower than most commercial definitions: "nonbank direct lending to medium-sized companies negotiated on a bilateral basis" [Confirmed, P11]. That narrowness matters for Section 1.4 below.

The boundary against neighbouring markets:
- **Broadly syndicated loans (BSL):** arranged by a bank, sold down to dozens of buyers (CLOs, mutual funds), rated, and traded in a secondary market. Private credit loans are the opposite on every count: one or a few lenders, unrated in most cases, no secondary market [Confirmed, P1, P19].
- **High yield bonds:** public, rated, fixed-rate, larger minimum issue size. The primer literature notes high-yield issuance historically required "two public credit ratings, extensive and SEC-compliant disclosure" and a soft minimum deal size around $125 million — a threshold private mezzanine finance grew explicitly to serve companies below [Confirmed, P2].
- **Investment-grade bonds:** even larger, publicly rated companies; private placements (see 1.2) are the historical private-market cousin of IG bonds, not of private credit direct lending.
- **Bank lending:** private credit is often described as what banks used to do and increasingly do not, for reasons covered in Section 1.3.
- **CLOs (collateralised loan obligations):** a CLO is a securitisation vehicle that pools broadly syndicated (or, increasingly, private-credit) loans and slices them into tranches sold to different risk buyers; a private credit loan is a single bilateral obligation with no tranching or public trading [Confirmed, P19]. A related and rapidly growing hybrid is the "private credit CLO" (also called a middle-market CLO): a securitisation of privately originated loans. The FSB found the private-credit-CLO market at $155 billion outstanding as of October 2025, about 16% of the roughly $977 billion US CLO market, with a higher share of CCC-rated collateral, lower weighted-average ratings and weaker recoveries than traditional balance-sheet CLOs, but also greater loss-absorbing capacity for senior investors because issuers typically retain the whole equity tranche [Confirmed, P11].

**Standard sub-categories.** Preqin's taxonomy, reproduced in the NAIC's primer, gives five core private credit strategies plus a newer sixth:
1. **Direct lending** — a non-bank lender (alone or in a small club) lends directly to a company and typically holds to maturity. This is the largest segment, roughly 40% of the US private credit market [Confirmed, P19, P1].
2. **Distressed debt** — buying debt trading well below par, or new financing to a distressed company, betting on a restructuring or liquidation payoff.
3. **Venture debt** — lending to pre-profit startups, sized off recurring revenue rather than EBITDA.
4. **Mezzanine finance** — subordinated debt sitting between senior loans and equity, historically paired with warrants.
5. **Special situations** — event-driven lending not tied to routine fundamentals.
6. **Fund financing** (newer) — lending to funds themselves via NAV loans, collateralised fund obligations and rated feeder-fund notes [Confirmed, P19].
Direct lending structures are typically senior debt, junior/subordinated debt, or unitranche (a single blended tranche combining senior and subordinated economics), and carry more protective covenants than BSLs [Confirmed, P19].

## 1.2 The prehistory

Before there was a "private credit industry" there were insurance companies. From the 1950s–60s onward, US life insurers (Prudential, MetLife, New York Life, TIAA-CREF, Northwestern Mutual are named repeatedly in the literature) originated unrated "notes" directly to companies with strong borrowing records — the private placement market — with typical maturities of 7–15 years, fixed coupons, and annual issuance historically in the $80–100 billion range [Confirmed, P2]. This served investment-grade-adjacent companies that didn't want the public disclosure burden of a bond deal, not the middle market that direct lending funds serve today.

The high-yield bond market opened in the late 1970s/early 1980s for non-investment-grade issuers, but its disclosure and scale requirements (two public ratings, SEC-level disclosure, a soft $125 million minimum) shut out smaller private companies [Confirmed, P2]. To fill that gap, a private "mezzanine" market grew through the late 1980s and 1990s. Early mezzanine investors were "select insurance companies and S&Ls" before the S&L crisis; the important investor base that consolidated during the 1990s was dedicated mezzanine funds. Mezzanine deals were structured to target an 18%–20% return via a coupon plus equity warrants, in sizes of roughly $10–75 million — well below what the public high-yield market required [Confirmed, P2].

**The Business Development Company (BDC).** The vehicle that today wraps a large share of US private credit was created by the Small Business Investment Incentive Act of 1980 (H.R. 7554, 96th Congress, enacted as Public Law 96-477), which amended the Investment Company Act of 1940 by adding a new Section 54. President Carter signed it on 21 October 1980; his signing statement describes it as providing "a new statutory framework to streamline legal structures and encourage venture capital to invest in small businesses," reducing "regulatory and statutory constraints," and improving coordination among federal agencies involved in small-business financing [Confirmed, P12, P13]. The mechanism: a BDC is a closed-end investment company that must invest at least 70% of its assets in "eligible portfolio companies" (broadly, private or thinly-traded US companies), and it must offer "significant managerial assistance" to those companies [Confirmed, P9]. To avoid entity-level tax, a BDC elects to be treated as a Regulated Investment Company (RIC) under the tax code, which requires it to distribute at least 90% of its net taxable income to shareholders annually (in practice most distribute 98% or more to also avoid a 4% federal excise tax) [Confirmed — general RIC income/distribution requirement].

On leverage, the original 1940-Act framework (as it applied to BDCs) capped leverage via a 200% asset-coverage test — equivalent to a maximum debt-to-equity ratio of about 1:1. The Small Business Credit Availability Act, signed by President Trump on 23 March 2018, cut the required asset-coverage ratio from 200% to 150%, which lets a BDC run debt-to-equity up to about 2:1 [Confirmed, P14, P9]. That leverage increase is not automatic: a BDC must get approval either from a majority of shareholders (leverage can rise the day after approval) or from a majority of the board plus a majority of directors with no financial interest in the BDC (leverage cannot rise until one year after board approval), and the BDC must keep disclosing the leverage taken, the approval basis, and the associated risks in its periodic reports [Confirmed, P14].

## 1.3 The real origin: the post-2008 bank retreat

This is the actual engine of the modern industry, and it runs through four connected regulatory and market changes.

**Basel III capital requirements.** Basel III was agreed by the Basel Committee after the 2008 crisis and phased in from roughly 2013 through 2019 for the core buffers, with full implementation originally targeted by 2022 for later elements [Reported, P — general regulatory timeline; see gaps]. Regulatory anticipation of higher risk-weighted-asset (RWA) charges for leveraged and middle-market loans made those loans capital-expensive for banks to hold on balance sheet relative to fee-generating activities such as originate-to-distribute syndication or advisory work.

That capital math sharpened again in July 2023, when US regulators (Fed, OCC, FDIC) proposed the so-called "Basel III Endgame" package. The agencies' own estimates showed aggregate RWA increasing by about 24% for Category I and II banks (the largest, globally systemic banks) and about 9% for Category III and IV banks, with domestic non-GSIB estimates generally running 16%–20% higher required capital. The proposal also introduced a more punitive "expanded risk-based approach," including a 65% risk weight for higher-quality corporate exposures tied to public trading of the borrower's securities — a test many private, sponsor-backed middle-market borrowers cannot meet, pushing their risk weight (and hence capital cost) higher still [Confirmed, P — KPMG/PwC synthesis, primary FDIC/Fed rule].

**The March 2013 interagency leveraged lending guidance.** On 21 March 2013 (published in the Federal Register 22 March 2013, compliance date 21 May 2013), the Federal Reserve, the OCC and the FDIC jointly issued the "Interagency Guidance on Leveraged Lending." Its stated purpose was to ensure that leveraged lending by regulated banks was conducted safely and soundly, and to strengthen banks' risk management so that leveraged lending did not "heighten risk in the banking system or the broader financial system" [Confirmed, P15]. In practice, examiners used the guidance to push back hard on deals they judged too highly levered or too weakly covenanted, and banks pulled back their appetite for exactly the kind of leveraged, sponsor-backed middle-market loan that private credit funds now dominate. Notably, in December 2025 the OCC and FDIC formally withdrew from the 2013 guidance (and its 2014 FAQs), stating the guidance had been "overly restrictive," had "impeded banks' application of existing risk management principles," was "overly broad," and had never been properly submitted to Congress for review — a rescission that itself is evidence of how much bite the original guidance had for over a decade [Confirmed, P16].

**The Volcker Rule.** Section 619 of the Dodd-Frank Act, finalised by five federal regulators (Fed, CFTC, FDIC, OCC, SEC) on 10 December 2013 with a conformance deadline of 21 July 2015, bars banking entities from proprietary trading and from investing in or sponsoring hedge funds or private equity funds [Confirmed, P17]. It is a narrower instrument than Basel III or the leveraged lending guidance, but it reinforced the same direction of travel: banks were pushed to keep their core lending clean of market-risk exposures and investment-fund relationships, further reducing the economic case for banks to hold illiquid, higher-risk leveraged and middle-market credit on balance sheet.

**Why middle-market lending became uneconomic for banks, and the resulting gap.** Put together, higher risk-weighted capital charges, supervisory pressure via the leveraged lending guidance, and Volcker's restriction on fund-adjacent activity made it structurally cheaper for a bank to arrange and distribute a loan (earning a fee, holding little or none of the risk) than to originate and hold a middle-market loan on its own balance sheet. Non-bank lenders — funds raising long-duration, largely locked-up capital from pensions, insurers and sovereign wealth funds — do not face bank capital rules and can hold the same loan to maturity without a regulatory capital penalty. That created the gap private credit filled.

**The data on the bank retreat.** Commercial banks' share of the leveraged loan market fell from roughly 71% in 1994 to under 25% by 2022 [Reported, P — search synthesis, single-source figure, treat as approximate]. More granular and more recent middle-market data: full-year 2024 middle-market leveraged buyout volume financed by direct lenders reached 90%, up from 80.3% in full-year 2023 [Reported, P18]. Globally, private debt funds provided 77% of leveraged buyout debt financing in 2024 — the highest private-credit share since 2015 — leaving banks with 23%, their lowest share over the same period [Reported, P18]. As of March 2026, however, there were signs the pendulum was starting to swing back: easing bank rules and strains in private credit were reported to be reopening space for banks to reclaim leveraged-lending share, described in one CNBC headline as "the tug of war is just starting" [Reported, P18].

## 1.4 The growth timeline, and why the numbers disagree

This is the section where sourcing discipline matters most, because five credible organisations can give five different market-size figures for the same year, and none of them is wrong — they are measuring different things.

**Early history.** Private credit funds' assets under management grew from about $0.2 billion in the early 2000s to over $2.5 trillion "today" (BIS's figure, using vendor AUM data, dated to the report's ~2024/25 reference period) [Confirmed, P3]. Separately, total private-credit loan volumes outstanding (a narrower, deployed-capital measure) grew from about $100 billion in 2010 to over $1.2 trillion in 2024, of which the United States alone accounts for roughly 87% (over $1 trillion in 2024, up from about $90 billion in 2010); Europe ex-UK is about 6% and the UK about 3%–4% of that same loan-volume measure [Confirmed, P3].

**Why they differ, explicitly (this is the point the brief asks to make):**
1. **Scope of "private credit."** The FSB's own report says plainly there is "no common definition" and that it deliberately uses "a narrower definition of private credit as nonbank direct lending to medium-sized companies negotiated on a bilateral basis" [Confirmed, P11]. ACC/AIMA and BIS's vendor-sourced figures fold in asset-based finance, real estate debt, infrastructure debt and fund financing — asset classes the FSB's narrow figure excludes. That alone explains most of the gap between the FSB's ~$1.5–2trn and ACC's $3.5trn.
2. **AUM (committed capital, including dry powder) versus loans actually outstanding.** BIS explicitly distinguishes "AUM" (over $2.5trn) from outstanding loan volumes (~$1.2trn) — AUM includes capital that has been raised and committed but not yet deployed ("dry powder") [Confirmed, P3]. A figure quoted as "market size" without specifying which of these it means is not comparable to a figure that does specify.
3. **Geographic coverage.** Some figures (BIS loan-volume data) are explicitly global; others are implicitly US-heavy because that is where reporting is best; ACC's figure is global by design and shows Europe at nearly 30% of AUM under its methodology, which sits oddly next to BIS's global loan-volume data showing Europe ex-UK at only ~6% — again a sign the two are measuring different bases (AUM commitments to managers vs. loans outstanding) [Confirmed, both from P3 and P5].
4. **Data source and methodology.** FSB uses regulator-submitted supervisory data from member jurisdictions; ACC/AIMA and Houlihan Lokey survey asset managers directly on their own AUM; Preqin and PitchBook aggregate fund-level disclosures and manager surveys; BIS blends commercial vendor data with its own analysis. Self-reported AUM from managers who have a commercial interest in appearing large is a different animal from supervisory data collected under compulsion.

**Forecasts (with attribution and dates), and how much they vary:**
- Moody's, as reported by Alternative Credit Investor on 17 October 2024, sized the market at $1.5 trillion (with direct lending at $800bn of that) and forecast growth to "close to $3 trillion" over the following three years, i.e., roughly by 2027–2028 [Reported, P8].
- Moody's own 2026 outlook blog updates this trajectory upward: global private credit AUM is "set to exceed $2 trillion in 2026" and to "approach $4 trillion by 2030," driven increasingly by asset-backed finance rather than direct lending alone [Confirmed, P7].
- The NAIC primer's side-by-side comparison of contemporaneous 2024-era forecasts shows just how much providers can diverge for the same target year: Moody's ~$2 trillion by 2027; Preqin $2.7 trillion by 2028; BlackRock $3.5 trillion by 2028 [Confirmed, P19].
- A more recent BlackRock projection, reported by PitchBook (exact publication date not independently confirmed — WebFetch of the article returned HTTP 403), puts private credit AUM at $4.5 trillion by 2030, explicitly citing AI-related datacentre and infrastructure buildout as a growth driver [Reported/PAYWALLED-NOT-READ, P6].
- No Apollo-branded market-size forecast in the $3–4.5 trillion range could be independently sourced within this workstream's search budget; this is flagged as a gap in Section 5 rather than invented.

## 1.5 The zero-rate era and its end

A decade of near-zero policy rates (the Fed's federal funds rate and equivalents were pinned near zero for most of 2009–2015 and again 2020–2022) pushed institutional allocators to "reach for yield." US public pension allocations to alternative assets (private equity, real estate, high-yield credit, private credit) rose from about one-tenth of assets two decades ago to about one-third by the early 2020s, with the alternatives share rising from roughly 20% in 2009 to roughly 35% by 2022 [Reported, P22 — pension-sector synthesis]. Private credit direct lending offered a persistent yield premium: the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index has shown gross yield spreads over the ten years to 30 September 2025 averaging 5.95 percentage points over high-yield bonds and 4.38 points over bank loans [Reported, P — search synthesis of Cliffwater/iCapital materials].

Because private credit loans are overwhelmingly floating rate, the 2022 rate-hiking cycle cut both ways. A loan priced at, say, SOFR + 500bps that yielded about 5% when SOFR was near zero suddenly yielded 10%+ once SOFR rose above 5% — pure upside for the lender's coupon income, on the same loan, with no change in the borrower's underlying business [Reported, P — search synthesis]. For the borrower, the same mechanism is pure downside: debt service on unchanged principal roughly doubled.

**Interest coverage ratios, with real data 2021–2026.** Interest coverage ratio (ICR) is EBITDA divided by cash interest expense — how many times over a company's operating earnings cover its interest bill. The Fed's own February 2024 FEDS Note found the average ICR for private-credit borrowers had shown "a significant decline in recent quarters," averaging around 2.0x, below the roughly 2.7x seen in broadly syndicated leveraged-loan borrowers over the same period [Confirmed, P4]. Search-indexed commentary (not independently fetched, so Reported rather than Confirmed) put the peak ICR at roughly 3.2x in 2021, falling toward roughly 1.5x by late 2025/2026, with a median of 1.72x reported for Q2 2026, and the share of borrowers with ICR below 1.5x rising from about 7% in Q4 2020 to about 47% more recently [Reported, P — search synthesis, single-source aggregation, flagged for corroboration].

The FSB's May 2026 report, read directly, corroborates the direction if not every exact figure: leverage in US private credit and middle-market loans (measured as total debt/EBITDA) rose from roughly 4x in the low-rate years after 2009 to roughly 5x–6x by the mid-2020s, and can be closer to 7x on a "true," non-EBITDA-adjusted basis once add-backs are stripped out [Confirmed, P11, Graph 7 read directly]. On cash-flow coverage specifically, a study of middle-market CLO borrowers cited in the FSB report found about 10% lacked sufficient cash flow to cover interest payments even before accounting for tax, working capital and capex needs, and the FSB's own chart of "negative cash earnings" share among middle-market CLO borrowers shows the share unable to cover interest-plus-tax-plus-working-capital rising from roughly 25%–30% in 2021 to a peak around 45% in 2023, before easing only slightly through Q3 2025 [Confirmed, P11, Graph 8 read directly]. Separately, the IMF's October 2025 Global Financial Stability Report is reported (single-source, not independently fetched) to have found about 40% of private credit borrowers with negative free cash flow, up from about 25% in 2021 [Reported, P23]. Payment-in-kind (PIK) loans — where a borrower defers cash interest by adding it to loan principal instead — are a direct symptom of coverage compression: PIK usage rose from about 7% of loans in late 2022 to over 10%–12% by 2025–2026, with PIK "toggles" (the borrower's option to defer) making up roughly half of that PIK-using population, and use of both PIK notes and toggles rising sharply since 2022 alongside the rate-hiking cycle and showing no sign of reversing as of the FSB's most recent data [Confirmed, P11].

## 1.6 Geography: US, Europe, Asia

The United States remains overwhelmingly dominant by loan volume: roughly 87% of the global private-credit loan stock as measured by BIS, versus roughly 6% for Europe ex-UK and 3%–4% for the UK [Confirmed, P3]. But growth momentum has clearly shifted toward Europe. European private-credit fundraising hit a record roughly $65 billion through the first nine months of 2025 — 14% above all of 2024's $57 billion — and Europe-focused funds captured 46% of global private-debt fundraising in the first three quarters of 2025, nearly double their 23% share in 2024; North America's fundraising share fell from close to half of the global total to just under a quarter over the same window [Reported, P — search synthesis of multiple trade-press sources]. On an AUM basis (a different measure again), Carlyle put the current European private-credit market at about $530 billion, projecting growth to about $940 billion by 2030 at roughly 12% annual growth, with direct lending remaining the dominant strategy at about 69% of AUM by 2030 [Confirmed, P10].

**Why Europe lagged.** The structural reason is that Europe never went through as complete a bank retreat as the US did. Bank lending still accounts for roughly 50% of corporate credit in Europe, versus roughly 25% in the US [Confirmed, P10]. European corporate finance also has a long "club deal" culture — a small group of relationship banks jointly financing a borrower, rather than the US model of a single arranger syndicating widely or a single direct lender writing the whole cheque — which slowed the wholesale institutional-fund model's takeover of the market. Europe is also not one market: Carlyle characterises it as "interconnected regional markets rather than a unified market," with the UK and France the most mature (over 60% of transactions there attract bids from multiple lenders) and Italy and Spain earlier-stage but catching up fast (over 80 transactions between them in the most recent year, double 2023's volume) [Confirmed, P10]. Currency plays a real role too: sterling tranches are a specific niche where private lenders have an edge, because large GBP-denominated tranches are comparatively illiquid and hard to place in the syndicated loan market, giving direct lenders a structural advantage in UK sterling deals [Reported, P — search synthesis].

**The UK specifically** is Europe's largest private-credit market, put at over £60 billion of AUM, with London home to the largest concentration of private-debt fund managers in the UK (207 funds by one count) [Reported, P — search synthesis]. Typical UK mid-market deal terms run £30–200 million in size, 4–6x EBITDA leverage, and pricing around SONIA + 550–800 basis points [Reported, P — search synthesis]. Growing institutional inflows from insurers, pension funds and sovereign wealth funds into UK direct lending are attributed to the same illiquidity-premium logic driving the US market.

**Asia** appears in this research only as a stated growth theme in AIMA/ACC materials (a report specifically flagged Asia-Pacific's "growing global private credit market" in its title) but was not independently verified with hard figures within this workstream's budget; treat as a gap (Section 5).

## 1.7 Why it was founded — stated plainly

**The benign answer.** Post-2008 capital rules made banks structurally poor holders of illiquid, long-duration, higher-risk middle-market and leveraged loans: the risk-weighted capital charge on such a loan, held for years on a bank's balance sheet funded partly by short-term deposits, is expensive and creates duration/liquidity mismatch risk for an institution whose core liabilities can run at short notice. Long-duration capital — pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, endowments — has the opposite liability profile: long-dated, relatively illiquid, patient. Moving the loan from the bank's balance sheet to a locked-up private-credit fund financed by that kind of capital is, on this reading, simply a better economic match: the risk moves to the type of capital structurally suited to hold it, banks become safer and more resilient by shedding a concentration of risk, and borrowers who fall outside standard bank credit boxes (too small, too specialised, needing speed and flexibility) get financing they would otherwise struggle to obtain. The FSB's own report states this case in almost these words: private credit "can help diversify lending across the financial system" and "reduce the concentration of risk within the banking sector, enhancing the resilience of the financial system," while offering borrowers tailored terms and faster execution and offering investors "diversification opportunities" and a floating-rate, low-rate-sensitivity return stream that suits long-dated liabilities [Confirmed, P11, P19].

**The critical answer.** The same set of facts supports a less benign reading: regulatory arbitrage. Academic work on the broader shadow-banking literature finds that a material share of non-bank lending growth in other markets (mortgage lending is the best-studied case, where one estimate attributes roughly 60% of shadow-bank mortgage growth to regulatory arbitrage) is explained not by comparative advantage but by activity migrating to wherever bank-style capital and conduct rules do not reach [Reported, P — search synthesis of academic shadow-banking literature]. Applied to private credit: the same underlying credit risk that a bank used to hold is now held by a fund, but outside bank capital, liquidity and stress-testing regimes, and largely outside the public disclosure regime that applies to syndicated loans and bonds. The Federal Reserve's own note on the topic states plainly that because private credit transactions are private, "the Federal Reserve cannot see the concentration of risk until it manifests as a liquidity event" [Reported, P — search synthesis of Fed commentary, corroborated in substance by the FSB's own repeated emphasis on data gaps]. The FSB's May 2026 report makes the same point institutionally, devoting an entire section to "data challenges" and stating that authorities rely on "regulatory, public, and commercial datasets, as well as ad hoc surveys," hampered by "the lack of harmonised private credit definitions, and limited granular fund- and loan-level data" [Confirmed, P11]. On this reading, private credit did not simply find a better home for the same risk; it found a home with materially lighter supervision for the same risk, and the industry's rapid, largely unregulated growth to a market of $1.5–3.5 trillion (depending on definition) in under two decades is itself evidence that the arbitrage, not just the maturity-match logic, has been a real driver.

Both readings are consistent with the same facts. A fair synthesis, and the one a beginner should be able to defend under questioning, is: private credit exists because post-2008 bank regulation (Basel III capital rules, the 2013 leveraged lending guidance, and the Volcker Rule) made banks worse economic holders of a specific category of loan risk at the exact moment a decade of near-zero rates made long-duration institutional capital desperate for yield; the resulting migration of that risk to lightly regulated, long-locked-up fund capital is genuinely a better balance-sheet match in some respects and genuinely a regulatory-arbitrage story in others, and regulators (FSB, Fed, BoE, IMF, BIS) now openly say they cannot fully see the risk they helped push there.

# 6. WHAT A BEGINNER MUST UNDERSTAND FROM THIS

1. **Private credit is not one thing; it is a definition fight, and the definition determines the market-size number you see quoted.** The single most important thing to be able to say in an interview is: "it depends what you count." The FSB itself, the world's top financial-stability coordinator, says there is "no common definition" and deliberately uses a narrow one ($1.5-2trn) while a trade body using a broader one gets $3.5trn for the same end-2024 date. Best worked example: put the FSB's $1.5-2trn (bilateral direct lending only) next to ACC/AIMA's $3.5trn (adds asset-based finance, real estate debt, infrastructure debt) side by side and explain the difference is definitional scope, not disagreement about facts. That single comparison, quoted with dates and sources, will survive almost any follow-up question about "how big is private credit really."

2. **Private credit exists because of a regulatory squeeze on banks meeting a rate-driven pull from investors — timing both, not either.** The squeeze: Basel III capital rules, the March 2013 leveraged lending guidance, and the Volcker Rule all made it structurally more expensive and more scrutinised for banks to hold middle-market and leveraged loans on balance sheet after 2008. The pull: near-zero policy rates for most of the 2010s left pensions and insurers hunting for yield precisely when banks were retreating. Best worked example: the OCC and FDIC formally withdrew the 2013 leveraged lending guidance in December 2025, thirteen years after issuing it, explicitly calling it "overly restrictive" — that single fact proves the guidance had real teeth for over a decade, which is exactly the mechanism that gave non-banks room to grow.

3. **Floating-rate structure means the 2022+ rate cycle helped lenders and hurt borrowers on the very same loans, and that shows up in falling interest coverage ratios.** A loan priced at a reference rate plus a fixed spread pays the lender more, dollar for dollar, as rates rise, while the borrower's cash cost of debt service rises on an unchanged EBITDA base. Best worked example: the Fed's own February 2024 note put average private-credit interest coverage at about 2.0x versus 2.7x for broadly syndicated loans, and the FSB's May 2026 report shows the share of middle-market CLO borrowers unable to cover interest, tax, and working-capital needs out of cash rising from roughly a quarter in 2021 to nearly half at its 2023 peak. PIK loans (deferring cash interest into loan principal) are the borrower's escape valve, and their rising use since 2022 is a coverage-stress indicator, not a neutral financing choice.

4. **The bank-retreat story is really a capital-efficiency story, and BDCs are the specific legal wrapper that let non-bank capital compete with banks on cost.** The Business Development Company, created in 1980 to solve small-business financing after the 1970s recession, sat mostly dormant as a niche vehicle for decades. It became the workhorse of the modern private-credit industry only after two things happened together: near-zero rates made retail and institutional money hungry for the yield a BDC could offer, and the 2018 Small Business Credit Availability Act let BDCs double their permitted leverage (from roughly 1:1 to 2:1 debt-to-equity). Best worked example: BIS research finds the cost-of-capital gap between listed BDCs and banks narrowed by about 200 basis points between the financial crisis and 2019 — a good chunk of that convergence came directly from BDCs being allowed, and choosing, to run more leverage.

5. **Europe is not "behind" for accidental reasons — it is behind because European banks never left the way US banks did, and that gap is now closing fast.** Bank lending is still roughly half of European corporate credit versus roughly a quarter in the US, which is the flip side of the exact regulatory-retreat story that built the US market. Best worked example: European private-credit fundraising jumped from a 23% share of the global total in 2024 to 46% in the first three quarters of 2025, while North America's share fell from near half to under a quarter over the same window — a genuine regime change happening in real time, driven by the same forces (tighter bank capital rules, this time Basel IV, plus fee-hungry investors) that built the US market fifteen years earlier.
