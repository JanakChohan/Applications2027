competitive cycle, real-economy effects, UK picture. Source-ID range used: P300-P339.

# 1. FINDINGS

## 1.1 Why a company picks private credit over a bank or a bond

Four things borrowers and their advisers actually say, backed by evidence rather than marketing:

**Speed.** A private direct lender can give a final credit decision two to four weeks after
receiving a complete data room and management meeting, because one fund (or a small club of two
or three) makes the whole decision. A broadly syndicated loan (BSL) needs a ratings process,
premarketing, then a multi-week syndication to dozens of CLO and institutional buyers, typically
six to ten weeks for a marketed deal [Confirmed, multiple trade sources] [P337][P319]. In the UK,
the same pattern holds: private direct lenders offer "fast and certain execution" without needing
a credit-committee sign-off, versus clearing banks whose committees and regulatory screening slow
things down [Confirmed] [P307].

**Certainty of execution.** Because a private credit fund is lending its own committed capital, the
price and size agreed in the term sheet is very unlikely to move before closing. A syndicated loan
or bond can be "flexed" (repriced or resized) if investor demand is weak, and the whole process can
be paused or pulled if public markets turn volatile — which happened repeatedly to the BSL and
high-yield bond markets in 2022. Private credit "typically remains open" through that kind of
volatility because it does not need to place paper with dispersed public investors [Confirmed]
[P319][P306]. This is the single biggest reason private credit took share from BSLs in 2022-23 (see
section 4).

**Confidentiality.** A private loan is agreed bilaterally or in a small club. There is no public
ratings process (no Moody's/S&P public rating needed), no confidential information memorandum
circulated to a wide bank/investor group, and no loan-level disclosure to the public market. For a
company that does not want competitors, customers or employees to see its financials or the terms
of its financing, or that wants to keep an M&A process quiet, this matters a great deal [Confirmed]
[P319][P337].

**Certainty of counterparty, especially in distress.** With a syndicated loan or a bond, if the
company later needs an amendment, a covenant waiver or a full restructuring, it must negotiate with
a dispersed and sometimes fractious group of institutional holders (in a bond, potentially hundreds
of holders across different classes with different incentives — some may be short the credit via
CDS, some may be distressed-debt funds that bought in at a discount and want a fight). With private
credit, the borrower typically negotiates with one lender or a small, known club that was there at
origination and has a long-term relationship to protect. Advisers describe direct lenders as
willing to "amend credit agreements, waive covenants, extend maturities, or adjust how interest is
serviced" rather than force an immediate default [Confirmed] [P327]. This is a genuine advantage in
a downturn, though as section 5 shows, it cuts both ways.

**Structural flexibility.** Private credit routinely offers things banks and the syndicated market
struggle to match: delayed draw term loans (DDTLs, capital reserved and drawn later, often to fund
future bolt-on acquisitions without a fresh financing process each time); PIK (payment-in-kind)
toggles, which let a borrower elect to pay interest by adding it to the loan principal instead of
in cash for a period (commonly around two years), preserving cash in a downturn or during a
build-out; and "synthetic PIK", where a DDTL is drawn specifically to fund a cash interest payment
on the main loan [Confirmed] [P338][P324][P325]. Roughly 9-11% of private credit deals carried PIK
provisions by late 2025, versus a much smaller and rarer share in the syndicated market [Reported]
[P306]. Bespoke, borrower-specific covenants (as opposed to the standardised covenant packages
banks and CLOs need to be sellable) are also a private-credit hallmark [Confirmed] [P307].

**Financing businesses banks and public markets will not touch.** Banks lend against tangible
collateral and historical, positive EBITDA; Basel-style capital rules make it expensive for a bank
to hold a loan to a highly levered, asset-light borrower. A fast-growing SaaS (software-as-a-service)
company with 90%+ gross margins and strong net revenue retention but little or no GAAP EBITDA does
not fit a conventional bank credit box [Confirmed] [P320][P321]. Private credit funds built
"recurring-revenue" or "ARR-based" (annual recurring revenue) loan structures specifically to serve
this segment: a company with, for example, $100m of ARR and no earnings could historically borrow
around $200m, with the loan converting to ordinary EBITDA-based covenants after two or three years
as the business matured [Reported] [P321]. Note that this specific structure has been retreating
in 2026: among eight software loans placed by Lincoln International in the first four months of
2026, none were ARR-based; all were priced and covenanted against EBITDA instead, reflecting
lender wariness about AI-driven disruption to software revenue durability [Reported] [P322]. This
is itself a useful, concrete example of the private credit market adapting its own underwriting in
real time.

**No ratings requirement.** A syndicated loan or public bond usually needs a public rating from at
least one, often two, of Moody's, S&P or Fitch, which costs money, takes time and permanently
publishes a credit opinion on the company. A private credit loan does not need one at all, though
some funds do obtain a private (unpublished) rating for regulatory capital purposes on their own
side [Confirmed] [P306].

## 1.2 What it costs the borrower, and how the premium has moved

The historical rule of thumb is that private credit costs more than the equivalent syndicated loan,
because the borrower is paying for the certainty, speed, confidentiality and flexibility described
above. But the size of that premium has swung sharply and is a live, contested number.

- In 2022-23, when the BSL and leveraged-loan markets were largely shut to new LBO issuance,
  new-issue direct lending spreads ran as high as SOFR+600 to SOFR+650bp [Reported] [P337].
- Competition among an estimated $250bn of dry powder chasing a limited pool of creditworthy
  borrowers compressed pricing through 2024: deals that priced above S+600 in 2023 were pricing at
  S+475 to S+550 by 2024/25 [Reported] [P337].
- By Q1 2026, new-issue private credit spreads averaged about S+475bp against a syndicated-loan
  average of roughly S+352bp, a gap of about 123bp [Confirmed, KBRA DLD-style loan data reported via
  Kadenwood] [P300].
- The gap then widened again: in the three months to 31 August 2026, new-issue private credit
  spreads averaged S+502bp against syndicated loans, a gap of 162bp, about 39bp wider than Q1 2026,
  and the share of sponsor-backed direct loans pricing in the S+500-549bp band rose from 25% of
  deals in Q1 to 52% by August [Confirmed] [P300]. On a hypothetical $25m loan this is worth roughly
  $97,500 a year in extra interest cost versus Q1 pricing [Inferred, arithmetic on the reported
  spread change] [P300].
- Separately and more starkly, Reuters/an analysis reported by Investing.com found that by
  spring 2026 risky loans were running roughly 200bp cheaper in the syndicated market than in
  direct lending (direct lending spreads of S+550-600bp versus public leveraged-loan spreads of
  S+350-400bp) [Reported] [P301]. At least $4.3bn across four deals moved from direct lending back
  to the syndicated market in early 2026 on the strength of that gap, while the number of new
  direct-lending deals in Q1 2026 fell to 104, down from 216 in Q1 2025 [Reported] [P301].
- Why the widening/reversal is happening now, per multiple analysts, is retail-investor redemption
  pressure on evergreen, semi-liquid private credit vehicles (interval funds and non-traded BDCs).
  Managers facing 16-17% redemption requests on some vehicles have had to shrink new lending
  volumes and tighten position sizing, which is passed through to borrowers as wider spreads, not
  because underlying credit quality worsened [Reported, DC Advisory analysis cited by multiple
  outlets] [P300][P302].

**Why "private credit is now sometimes cheaper than the syndicated market" would be surprising, and
when it has actually been true.** The 2022-24 episode where BSL new issuance effectively stopped
handed private credit years of pricing power over LBO financing, and it is well documented that for
a period in mid-2022 private credit was financing "nearly all" new LBOs [Reported] [P303]. That
briefly meant private credit was the ONLY available price, not necessarily cheaper on a like-for-
like basis. The genuinely surprising claim, that private credit can beat the syndicated market on
raw price for the SAME credit, is not the steady state: it happened only in that closed-market
window, and it has been reversing hard through 2024-26 as the BSL market reopened and repriced
(see section 4). The 2026 story is a widening premium, not a narrowing one, driven by fund-level
liquidity stress rather than credit fundamentals [Confirmed] [P300][P301].

## 1.3 Who the borrowers are

**Size bands (measured by EBITDA — earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation).**
Definitions vary by manager, which the reader should be told explicitly rather than given one
"true" number:
- One common three-way split: lower middle market $10m-$25m EBITDA; core middle market $25m-$100m;
  upper middle market $100m-$300m [Reported] [P339].
- Audax Private Debt's own house definition: lower middle market under $15m EBITDA; middle market
  $15m-$75m; upper middle market above $75m [Reported] [P339].
- KKR defines its own upper-middle-market target as $50m-$200m EBITDA, with its actual portfolio
  averaging over $100m [Confirmed, direct from manager] [P309]. KKR argues (self-interestedly, but
  with data) that this segment has about 60bp lower historical default rates than the lower middle
  market, while the extra return an investor gets for going down-market to smaller borrowers has
  averaged only about 24bp since 2018, too little compensation for the added volatility [Reported]
  [P309]. KKR also states roughly 90% of the direct lending market by lender count operates in the
  lower middle market, competing hard on a small pool of deals, while only a handful of funds can
  write the larger, more complex upper-middle-market cheques [Reported] [P309].
- "Large-cap" or "large-cap direct lending" describes the newest frontier: loans to companies with
  well over $100m, often $200m+, of EBITDA, historically syndicated-market territory, now also
  written by the largest direct lenders (Ares, Blackstone, Blue Owl, HPS, Apollo etc). The
  Electronic Arts buyout financing (see below) sits at the very top of this range.

**Sponsored versus non-sponsored.** "Sponsored" means the borrower is owned by a private equity
firm; "non-sponsored" means an independent, often family- or founder-owned, company borrowing
directly. Reported shares cluster around 70-85% sponsored / 15-30% non-sponsored in the US private
credit market, though exact figures vary by data source and year: one estimate for 2019 put 73% of
loan facilities as sponsor-backed; Invesco states sponsors "now account for the majority" of direct
lending without giving an exact number [Reported] [P310][P339 covers general framing;
non-sponsored PDF itself could not be read, see gaps]. The trend among some managers (LGIM,
PGIM, Brookfield) is to argue non-sponsored lending is a growing, underexploited niche because it
faces less competition and can carry better terms for the lender, precisely because there is no
PE sponsor bidding the price down [Reported] [P311].

**Sector concentration: software and healthcare.** Private-credit fund holdings are reported to be
concentrated in five sectors — industrials, financials, healthcare, information technology and
consumer discretionary — together about 76% of asset fair value across tracked funds [Reported]
[P318]. At the single-fund level, Cliffwater's flagship interval fund (CCLFX, ~$27.9bn net assets
as of 31 March 2025) reported technology at 26.1% of its corporate investments by fair value as of
30 September 2025, with analysts noting true software/SaaS exposure is likely higher still because
some healthcare and business-services borrowers are themselves software-enabled businesses
[Reported] [P318]. This concentration is precisely why the 2026 "AI disruption to software" worry
(flagged elsewhere in this project's research, e.g. Blue Owl's OTIC 8-K) matters so much to the
asset class as a whole: software and healthcare are not a side bet, they are close to the core of
what private credit actually holds [Inferred, connecting P318 to the already-established Blue Owl
episode].

**Largest deals.** The largest private-credit loan on record at the time (August 2023) was the
$5.3bn financing for Finastra Group Holdings (a fintech company owned by Vista Equity Partners),
made up of a $4.8bn unitranche and a $500m revolver, provided by a club of 19 direct-lending funds
including Oak Hill Advisors, Blue Owl Capital and HPS Investment Partners [Reported, Bloomberg,
not independently re-verified here] [P305]. That record has since been dwarfed on the debt side (if
not purely on the private-credit side) by the Electronic Arts take-private: announced September
2025, an all-cash deal worth roughly $55bn enterprise value led by Public Investment Fund (PIF),
Silver Lake and Affinity Partners, the largest LBO ever, ahead of the 2007 TXU Energy buyout at
$32bn [Confirmed across multiple outlets] [P313][P314]. The EA deal is backed by a $20bn debt
financing commitment, of which about $18bn was expected to be funded at close, provided solely by
JPMorgan as a traditional bank underwrite, not a private-credit club [Confirmed, per CreditSights
detail] [P313]. Pro forma gross leverage was reported at roughly 6x fiscal-2027E EBITDA of about
$3.1bn [Reported] [P313]. The deal closed on 4 August 2026 after shareholders approved it on 22
December 2025 with about 99% of votes cast in favour [Reported] [P313]. This is an important
nuance for the reader: the single largest LBO in history was financed by a bank, not by private
credit, even in 2025-26, which shows the two markets still compete head-to-head for the very
largest, highest-quality mandates, and banks can still win them.

## 1.4 The competitive battle with the syndicated loan market

This is a genuine back-and-forth, not a one-way structural shift, and it is one of the more
interesting live dynamics in credit markets right now.

**2022-23: private credit takes share.** When the Fed's rapid rate rises and market volatility
closed the BSL and high-yield markets to new LBO issuance, private credit stepped in as the only
reliably open source of large-scale acquisition debt; for a period around mid-2022 private credit
was reported to be financing nearly all new LBOs [Reported] [P303]. This is the origin of private
credit's reputation as the more "dependable" market.

**2024: the syndicated market reopens and repriced hard.** As rate-cut expectations built and
investor demand for leveraged loans and CLOs recovered, BSL issuance came roaring back.
Refinancing and repricing activity in the US leveraged loan market jumped 137% year on year to a
record $1.2 trillion in 2024, made up of about $757bn of repricings and $259bn of refinancings
[Reported] [P303]. Direct lenders were refinanced out of almost $30bn of deals by October 2024 as
borrowers moved back to the cheaper public market [Reported] [P303]. Borrowers who switched saved
an average of about 263bp, ranging from roughly 200bp to 375bp, with the largest savings on the
most expensive unitranche and second-lien private loans written in 2022-23 [Reported] [P303].

**Named deals moving from private credit back to syndicated:**
- Catalent: initially financed with a $4.2bn direct-lending term loan for its Novo Holdings-related
  acquisition financing, later refinanced into a $4.1bn syndicated loan, cutting annual interest by
  roughly $100m [Reported] [P302].
- FDH Aero: a $1.1bn term loan originally used to fund a Bain Capital-backed acquisition moved from
  direct-lender financing to syndicated financing [Reported] [P302].
- Electrical Components International (ECI): a $1.1bn senior secured credit facility from private
  credit lenders was used in May to refinance existing broadly syndicated loans, i.e. moving the
  other direction, into private credit, showing the flow genuinely runs both ways deal by deal
  [Reported] [P334].
- Mitratech (HR software) lined up a roughly $2.2bn private credit package priced near SOFR+475bp
  in late 2025 specifically to refinance an existing broadly syndicated loan, again moving from
  public to private [Reported] [P334].
- Cordis (healthcare), a HPS-led private credit refinancing of over $500m at roughly SOFR+450-500bp
  [Reported] [P334].
- Direct lenders refinanced $36.9bn of syndicated loans in 2025 overall, the highest level in at
  least four years, underlining that the flow toward private credit did not stop even as the
  reverse flow (private credit to syndicated) also grew [Reported] [P334].

**2025-26: parallel growth rather than a winner-take-all outcome.** By 2025, US primary syndicated
loan issuance hit roughly $404bn in Q3 alone (a quarterly record) and the broadly syndicated loan
market held around $1.55 trillion outstanding, while private credit assets were reported at over
$3 trillion and projected toward roughly $5 trillion by 2029 [Reported, market-size figures vary
by provider, flagged per Rule 8] [P306]. Practitioners increasingly describe the relationship as
"not either/or" but "yes, and": banks and direct lenders are co-arranging deals together (a
so-called "originate to share" or hybrid model), and JPMorgan itself expanded its own direct
lending commitment to $50bn in February 2025, i.e. a bank building private-credit capacity rather
than simply losing business to it [Reported] [P306]. Documentation gaps between the two markets
persist: about 98% of 2025 syndicated loans were "covenant-lite" (i.e., without the ongoing
maintenance financial covenants that let a lender intervene early) versus about 53% of private
credit deals, meaning private credit still, on average, offers lenders more protection and
borrowers more true covenant negotiation, even as pricing converges [Reported] [P306].

## 1.5 The real-economy case: does private credit actually help?

**The case for.** Academic work using natural experiments (regulatory shocks and specialty-lender
failures that cut off some companies' access to Business Development Company, or BDC, financing
while leaving similar companies' access intact) finds that gaining access to BDC/direct-lending
financing causes increased employment growth and higher rates of patenting at the borrower level,
evidence the credit relationship itself, not just the cash, adds real value through monitoring and
advice [Reported, citing Davydiuk et al. and related work summarised in an academic survey]
[P316]. Separately, Federal Reserve analysis reportedly found private credit direct loans defaulting
at about 1.6%, against roughly 3.3% for high-yield bonds and about 5% for syndicated loans over a
comparable period, evidence that closer monitoring by a concentrated private lender can mean fewer
defaults, not more, for a comparable risk profile [Reported] [P317]. Research on lender behaviour
(Jang 2024; Jang & Rosen 2025) also found no evidence that PE-affiliated lenders give their own
sponsor's portfolio companies below-market terms, i.e. underwriting discipline appears to hold even
inside sponsor-lender relationships [Reported] [P317]. The core, uncontroversial real-economy
argument is supply-side: banks pulled back sharply from leveraged, asset-light and smaller-company
lending after the 2008 crisis and subsequent capital rules, and private credit funds "moved
relatively swiftly to fill the corporate funding gap" this left, particularly in commercial real
estate and riskier corporate/SME lending [Reported, UK-focused] [P307][P331]. US bank lending's
share of corporate borrowing fell from about 44% in 2020 to about 35% in 2023 as private credit
grew into the gap [Reported] [P312].

**The case against, or at least the serious complication.** A large academic and legal literature
on leveraged buyouts documents that the debt ratios routinely used in LBOs would be considered
imprudent for a comparable standalone public company, and argues that leverage, cash extraction and
short exit horizons "externalise predictable risks to third parties including workers, ... unsecured
creditors, communities" [Reported, legal-academic argument, contested] [P330]. NBER research on the
economic effects of PE buyouts finds a split outcome by target type: buyouts of previously PUBLIC
targets are associated with layoffs from "organisational streamlining", while buyouts of previously
PRIVATE targets are associated with accelerated employment growth as new owners modernise
production [Reported] [P328]. On the credit side specifically, private credit's own flexibility, the
willingness to "amend, waive, extend maturities, or adjust how interest is serviced" rather than
force a restructuring, cuts both ways: it can rescue a fundamentally sound company through a rough
patch, but critics call this "extend-and-pretend", masking underlying stress and delaying a
reckoning rather than resolving it, particularly given roughly 80% of private credit loans are
floating-rate and borrowers whose loans once cost 4-5% have in some cases seen costs rise to
12-15% as rates rose [Reported] [P327]. The already-established finding in this project that PIK
income (payment deferred rather than received in cash) has risen from about 5.9% of BDC income in
2023 to about 8% in 2026 is a direct, quantifiable symptom of this dynamic: more borrowers are
paying their lenders in more debt rather than cash [background, already established per brief].
Default-rate comparisons also need care: while headline default rates in private credit can look
low, that is partly definitional, since an "amend and extend" avoids being counted as a formal
default at all; middle-market direct lending shows reported annual default rates of roughly
1.8%-3.2% against 1.1%-2.4% for BSLs over 2010-2023, and even these are likely understated because
private-credit borrowers are smaller, more leveraged and less transparent than rated companies
[Reported] [P316]. Recovery rates are reported higher for senior secured first-lien direct loans
(around 69%) than for senior secured syndicated loans (around 65%), which is a genuine credit
advantage of concentrated lender control when a restructuring does happen [Reported] [P316].

**Net read for a beginner.** Both sides have real evidence behind them and neither should be
presented as settled. Private credit demonstrably reaches companies banks will not touch and this
has real, measured employment and innovation effects at the borrower level [P316]. It also
demonstrably enables and sometimes masks higher leverage, and its cost is materially higher than
bank or public-market debt for a comparable credit, a real and permanent drag on the borrower's
free cash flow that has to be weighed against the benefits of speed, certainty and flexibility.

## 1.6 The UK and European picture

UK mid-market borrowers choose between three broad types of lender, each in a different regulatory
box: (1) the big clearing banks (Barclays, HSBC, Lloyds, NatWest), dual-regulated by the Prudential
Regulation Authority and the FCA, offering the most competitive pricing for straightforward,
lower-risk borrowers but slower, committee-driven execution and more maintenance-covenant-heavy
loans; (2) "challenger" banks, pricing between relationship-bank and private-credit economics, with
shorter credit chains and some sector specialism (healthcare, hospitality, real estate) enabling
faster decisions, but limited by smaller balance sheets and internal concentration limits; and (3)
private direct lenders, sitting outside bank prudential capital rules entirely (so unconstrained by
Basel 3.1-style requirements), able to offer higher leverage, more complex cash-flow-based
structures, unitranche facilities, PIK features and bespoke covenants, at a "meaningful premium" to
senior bank debt [Confirmed] [P307]. This closely mirrors the US picture: UK banks reduced lending
most sharply after the financial crisis in commercial real estate and in riskier corporate/SME
lending, and private credit funds "moved relatively swiftly to fill" that gap [Reported, per
industry submission to a UK parliamentary committee] [P331]. The British Business Bank, the UK
government's state-owned economic development bank, treats private credit as a distinct financing
option worth explaining to smaller UK businesses in its own guidance, and its commercial subsidiary
British Business Investments has committed more than £3.8bn since 2014 to providers of finance to
UK smaller businesses, including targeted commitments to private-credit vehicles serving the lower
mid-market (for example a £50m private-credit co-investment vehicle with Dunport announced 7 May
2024, and a separately managed account commitment to Prefequity, a private credit firm targeting UK
owner-managed businesses with EBITDA of £2m-£10m and turnover under £100m, announced 17 July 2025)
[Confirmed, direct from British Business Bank press releases] [P308]. This is a clear, primary-
source example of the UK state itself treating private credit as a tool to plug a financing gap for
smaller UK companies that banks are not fully serving, rather than as a risk to be merely monitored
(contrast with the Bank of England's Financial Stability Report stance, already established
elsewhere in this project, which flags private credit's system-wide risks).

# 6. WHAT A BEGINNER MUST UNDERSTAND FROM THIS

1. **Private credit is not just "the same loan but from a different lender." It is a genuinely
   different product**, and the biggest single reason companies choose it is certainty: a private
   lender commits its own money and can promise a fixed price and a fixed close date in weeks,
   while a syndicated loan has to be sold to dozens of buyers and can be re-priced or pulled if the
   market wobbles. Best worked example: 2022, when public leveraged-loan markets shut down and
   private credit briefly financed almost all new LBOs, not because it was cheaper, but because it
   was open when nothing else was [P303].

2. **The premium a company pays for that certainty is real, measurable in basis points, and it
   moves with supply and demand, not just with credit risk.** In 2022-23 it was worth 250bp or more
   over syndicated pricing; by 2024 competition among lenders (chasing an estimated $250bn of dry
   powder) crushed that premium; through 2026 the premium widened again to around 162bp, driven not
   by worse credit quality but by private credit fund managers facing their own investor
   redemptions and needing to protect margins. Best worked example: the same $25m borrower would
   pay roughly $97,500 a year more in interest in August 2026 than in Q1 2026 for what may be an
   identical credit, purely because of stress inside the lending vehicle, not the borrowing company
   [P300].

3. **This is a genuine two-way market, not a one-way disruption story.** Companies flow from
   private credit to syndicated loans (Catalent, FDH Aero) when public markets reopen and get
   cheap, and flow the other way (ECI, Mitratech, Cordis) when they want speed, confidentiality or
   a structure banks cannot offer. Best worked example: the same company type, mid-sized, sponsor-
   backed, appears on both sides of this trade within the same eighteen months, proving neither
   market has a permanent structural advantage; it depends on which market is "open" and cheap at
   that moment [P302][P334].

4. **"Private credit reaches companies banks have abandoned" is true and it has been measured to
   have real economic effects, not just financial ones.** Academic work using natural experiments
   shows that companies that gain access to direct lending see real employment growth and more
   patenting, not just cheaper access to money. Best worked example/analogy: think of a fast-
   growing software company with no profit yet but strong recurring subscription revenue. A bank's
   credit committee, built to look at hard collateral and historical EBITDA, cannot say yes. A
   private credit fund can build a loan around the subscription revenue instead, price it for the
   risk, and get the company through its growth phase. That is capital reaching somewhere a
   standardised, regulated banking system structurally cannot go [P316][P320][P321].

5. **The same flexibility that makes private credit attractive can also hide problems rather than
   solve them, and this is the honest tension at the heart of the "is this good for the economy"
   question.** Because a borrower in trouble negotiates with one lender rather than a crowd of bond
   holders, that lender can quietly extend the loan, waive a covenant, or let interest be paid in
   more debt (PIK) instead of forcing a public reckoning. This can rescue a good company through a
   bad patch. It can also let a genuinely struggling, over-levered company limp on for years
   ("extend-and-pretend" or a "zombie" company) without the market ever seeing a default. Best
   worked example/analogy: a stretched borrower whose loan cost 4-5% a few years ago now costs
   12-15% as rates rose; instead of a visible default, the lender and sponsor quietly amend the
   loan again. To an outside observer using headline default-rate statistics, nothing looks wrong.
   To someone who understands PIK income (rising, per the brief's established figures, from about
   5.9% of BDC income in 2023 toward about 8% in 2026) and amend-and-extend activity, that is a
   warning sign hiding inside a good-looking headline number [P327][P316].
