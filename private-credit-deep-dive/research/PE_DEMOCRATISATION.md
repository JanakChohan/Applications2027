## 1.2 Why this is a big deal: explaining the inversion

For roughly forty years the private markets pecking order was simple and unambiguous. Institutions
(pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, endowments) got first access to the best managers, through
closed-end, drawdown-structured, ten-year funds. You commit capital up front (a "commitment"), the
manager calls it in tranches over several years as it finds deals (a "capital call"), and you get
cash back only as deals are sold (a "distribution"), often not meaningfully until years 5-8 of a
10-12 year fund life. In exchange for locking your money up and bearing that uncertainty, you paid
the lowest fees in the industry (often "2 and 20": a 2% annual management fee on committed capital,
and 20% of profits above a hurdle, sometimes lower for large institutional tickets) and, in theory,
got the highest expected returns, an "illiquidity premium." Retail investors, meanwhile, essentially
had no legal or practical way in. Minimum commitments of $5m-$25m, multi-year lockups and complex
subscription paperwork kept the asset class institutional almost by construction.

Evergreen (also called "perpetual" or "semi-liquid") funds were the industry's answer to the
individual investor. They were explicitly a downgrade in terms designed to make the product
deliverable to people who could not tolerate a 10-year lockup or write an eight-figure cheque: no
fixed end date, continuous or periodic subscriptions (you can often buy in monthly), and periodic
redemption windows (commonly monthly or quarterly, subject to caps, rather than a specific exit
event). In exchange for that convenience, wealth-channel investors have historically accepted higher
ongoing fees, sometimes layered distribution and platform fees on top of the management fee
[P219] [Reported], and in some accounts, "potentially lower returns than traditional private market
vehicles" [P201] [Confirmed].

So the inversion is this: **the "worse" product, engineered as second-best for people the industry
could not otherwise reach, is now being bought by the same institutions the "better" product was
built for.** That is not a footnote. If large, sophisticated pension funds, the investors closed-end
funds were purpose-built to serve, are voluntarily choosing the retail-grade wrapper over the
institutional-grade one, it raises the uncomfortable question of whether the traditional closed-end
structure's supposed advantages (better terms, better access, better alignment) are still worth what
they cost investors in locked-up, uncalled and hard-to-exit capital. It suggests the product built
"for the masses" might, in some circumstances, actually behave better than the product built "for
the elite," a genuinely rare structural challenge to an asset class's core distribution model.

**What is actually driving this**, based on the material I could verify:

1. **Distribution drought and the exit backlog.** Private equity managers have struggled to sell
portfolio companies and return cash to LPs, because high interest rates depressed valuations and
IPO markets stayed difficult, leaving buyout funds sitting on large unrealised (paper) gains they
cannot easily crystallise. One source specifically flags "relatively low DPI figures in buyout
funds" as a live industry concern in 2026 [P215] [Reported, single source; I could not independently
pin down an exact DPI percentage or exit-backlog dollar figure from a primary source such as Bain,
Preqin or Cambridge Associates within this workstream's time; see GAPS]. DPI stands for
"Distributions to Paid-In capital": cash actually returned to investors, divided by cash they have
put in. A DPI of 1.0x means you have got your money back; anything below that after several years
means your capital is still locked up in unsold, unrealised positions. When DPI stays low across the
industry for years, LPs with real, near-term cash needs (paying pensions, funding university
operations) start to prize any structure that gives them a *chance* at cash back sooner, even a
"periodic redemption window, not liquidity" as one source rightly distinguishes it [P206]
[Reported].

2. **The denominator effect, in reverse.** The "denominator effect" describes what happens when
public markets fall: an institution's private-market holdings (the numerator) stay marked at their
last (stale) valuation while public equities (part of the denominator, total assets) fall in value,
mechanically pushing the private allocation above its target percentage and forcing the investor to
either sell private assets (hard to do) or stop making new commitments. By 2026, with public markets
higher, the acute 2022-23 version of this problem has eased for many allocators, and the emphasis
in current commentary has shifted from "too much locked in private markets" to "a scramble for yield
in private credit and infrastructure" [as one industry summary put it, P218] [Reported, single
source]. In other words, institutions are not backing away from private markets, they are trying to
get exposure through a wrapper that gives them more control over pacing.

3. **Avoiding the J-curve and capital-call drag.** In a closed-end fund, committed capital sits
uninvested (earning close to nothing, sometimes parked in cash or a low-yield facility) until the
manager calls it, and early years are typically loss-making on paper because fees are charged from
day one while few investments have had time to appreciate. This is the "J-curve": returns dip before
they rise. Evergreen funds are typically close to fully invested from day one and stay that way,
using a small "liquidity sleeve" (a slice of cash or liquid assets held back to fund redemptions) to
manage redemptions without needing to sell the whole book [P216] [Confirmed]. One concrete,
verifiable illustration of how large this effect can be: Partners Group has calculated that a
closed-end fund strategy would need to generate a 20% IRR to match an 11% return from an equivalent
open-ended evergreen programme, purely because of the drag of uncalled capital sitting idle and
capital from early exits not being recycled into new deals [P216] [Confirmed, direct quote from
Cleary Gottlieb's summary of Partners Group's own published analysis]. That is a very large gap to
explain by capital-call mechanics alone, and it is the single clearest "worked example" for why an
institution might rationally prefer immediate, continuous deployment over the classic drawdown
structure.

4. **Administrative simplicity.** Institutions running dozens or hundreds of closed-end
commitments must forecast and manage capital calls and distributions across all of them
simultaneously (a "pacing model"). An evergreen allocation is, administratively, closer to writing
one cheque and monitoring one NAV, which is materially simpler for a pension fund's investment
team to manage, though this point is more of an industry-repeated rationale than something I found
hard data quantifying; treat it as directionally plausible rather than independently measured.

## 1.3 The product landscape individuals can actually buy

The wealth-channel private credit product set has grown fast and now spans several distinct legal
wrappers, each with different liquidity mechanics. I checked five named non-traded business
development companies (BDCs) directly against their most recent SEC filings (all for the quarter
ended 30 June 2026, filed in August 2026) rather than relying on marketing figures, because BDC net
assets move fast and vendor figures vary in what they count (gross assets including leverage, vs.
net assets/NAV attributable to shareholders). Net assets (shareholders' equity) figures, straight
from each fund's own XBRL-tagged financial statements on SEC EDGAR:

- **BCRED** (Blackstone Private Credit Fund): $42.78bn net assets, period ended 30 June 2026
  [P221] [Confirmed, primary source].
- **OCIC** (Blue Owl Credit Income Corp): $18.43bn net assets, 30 June 2026 [P224] [Confirmed,
  primary source]. (This is the fund already established in this project as breaching its
  redemption cap in Q1 2026 with requests at 21.9% of NAV against a 5% cap.)
- **ACRED** (Apollo Debt Solutions BDC): $14.00bn net assets, 30 June 2026 [P223] [Confirmed,
  primary source].
- **HLEND** (HPS Corporate Lending Fund): $12.05bn net assets, 30 June 2026 [P225] [Confirmed,
  primary source].
- **ASIF** (Ares Strategic Income Fund): $10.21bn net assets, 30 June 2026 [P222] [Confirmed,
  primary source].

Together these five funds alone hold roughly $97bn of net assets as of mid-2026. This is a
useful, checkable anchor: it shows the scale of just the best-known non-traded BDCs is already
comparable to a mid-sized country's entire pension system, built almost entirely since these
vehicles began scaling up after 2020-21. For market-wide context (not independently verified by me
at the same primary-source level, so held at [Reported]): non-traded BDCs as a category are said to
have grown from essentially zero in 2021 to over $200bn by mid-2025 [P215] [Reported, single
source], and evergreen private credit assets overall reached about $644bn as of 30 June 2025, up
28% from end-2024 (roughly 45% year-on-year) [P215] [Reported, single source; this is a similar
order of magnitude to, but not identical to, the ~$607bn Q1 2026 figure already established
elsewhere in this project, and the difference is plausibly explained by different cut-off dates and
different vendor methodologies, consistent with the brief's warning that private-market sizing
estimates vary a lot by provider].

Beyond BDCs, the other wrappers a beginner needs to know:

- **Interval funds**: registered under the Investment Company Act, offering to repurchase a fixed
  percentage of shares (commonly 5-25%, under SEC Rule 23c-3, already established in this project)
  at set intervals, typically quarterly.
- **Tender offer funds**: similar in spirit but the repurchase offer is discretionary rather than a
  standing commitment, giving the manager more flexibility (and less certainty for the investor)
  than an interval fund.
- **ELTIF 2.0** (European Long-Term Investment Fund, second-generation EU rules): the reformed EU
  regime that made it materially easier to market long-term private-asset funds to retail investors
  across the EU, lowering minimum investment thresholds and easing diversification and liquidity
  rules versus the original 2015 ELTIF regime. European semi-liquid fund assets under this and
  related regimes stood at roughly €20bn as of October 2025 by one estimate [P214] [Reported,
  single source], with one industry forecast (explicitly speculative, not a market-data provider)
  projecting a rise past €75bn by late 2026 [P214] [Reported, single source, framed by its own
  author as a "bold prediction," not a measured fact].
- **UK LTAF** (Long-Term Asset Fund): already established elsewhere in this project (FCA PS21/14 and
  PS23/7), the UK's own answer to the same problem, with minimum 90-day redemption notice and
  monthly maximum dealing.
- **Model portfolios**: increasingly, wealth managers are blending small evergreen private-market
  sleeves into otherwise-liquid model portfolios sold to mass-affluent clients through platforms,
  rather than selling a single fund as a standalone product.
- **Defined contribution (DC) pensions and 401(k) plans**: the newest and largest frontier by
  potential dollar size. In the US, President Trump signed **Executive Order 14330, "Democratizing
  Access to Alternative Assets for 401(k) Investors,"** on 7 August 2025 [P220] [Confirmed, primary
  source, read directly]. The order notes that most of the 90+ million participants in US
  employer-sponsored 401(k) plans currently have no practical access to private-market investments,
  and it directs the Secretary of Labor, within 180 days, to: (i) re-examine ERISA fiduciary
  guidance on offering "alternative asset" allocation funds to participants, including potentially
  rescinding the Department of Labor's own 21 December 2021 Supplemental Statement on Private
  Equity in Defined Contribution Plans; (ii) clarify what a fiduciary process should look like when
  weighing expenses against long-term returns and diversification for these products; (iii) propose
  rules or safe harbours defining fiduciary duty when offering alternative-asset funds; and (iv)
  look at reducing fiduciary litigation risk as a barrier. It separately directs the SEC to consider
  its own regulatory changes to ease access. One later industry account reports that the DOL
  proposed a rule in March 2026 that would let 401(k) plan sponsors add evergreen funds to lineups
  without extra fiduciary liability [P218] [Reported, single source; I could not independently
  confirm this specific proposed rule via a DOL or Federal Register primary source in the time
  available; treat as a plausible but unconfirmed follow-through on the EO, and see GAPS]. Given
  that with intelligence's report puts the theoretical addressable market at "the roughly $13
  trillion US defined contribution market" [P215] [Reported, single source], even a very small
  percentage allocation would dwarf every other distribution channel discussed in this file.

  I was not able to verify the UK's "Mansion House Accord" within this workstream (repeated direct
  fetch attempts against gov.uk and related pages returned 404s, and I had exhausted this session's
  search budget before I could locate the primary announcement). This is a genuine gap; see Section
  5. What can be said with the material already established elsewhere in this project is that the
  UK's parallel policy vehicle for private-market access is the LTAF regime (FCA PS21/14, PS23/7),
  which does now permit retail and DC pension access under specific conditions.

## 1.4 The ETF and semi-liquid frontier

The most liquidity-stretching product to date is **PRIV**, the SPDR SSGA Apollo IG Public & Private
Credit ETF, run jointly by State Street Global Advisors and Apollo. Checked directly against the
issuer's own product page: PRIV launched on **26 February 2025**, and had **$811.72 million** in
assets as of **17 September 2026** [P226] [Confirmed, primary source, issuer's own site]. It is an
actively managed, exchange-traded (so, in principle, intraday-liquid, unlike an interval fund or
BDC) fund whose stated target allocation is that private credit will "generally range between 10%
and 35%" of the portfolio, though the issuer explicitly notes it may sometimes fall outside that
band [P226] [Confirmed, primary source]. As of the same date, the actual portfolio was 78.31%
"Non-Apollo Sourced" investments, 13.60% Apollo-sourced asset-backed finance, and 8.09%
Apollo-sourced corporate finance, across 361 holdings, with the largest sector weightings in
industrials, mortgage-backed securities and Treasuries [P226] [Confirmed, primary source]. In plain
terms: most of PRIV's book today is liquid, publicly traded, or public-market-adjacent debt, with a
minority sleeve of genuinely illiquid, Apollo-originated private credit blended in underneath an
ETF wrapper that trades every second the stock market is open.

This is precisely where the structural tension the brief asks about lives: an ETF's whole promise
is that you can sell your shares at any moment during market hours at a price backed by liquid
"authorized participant" arbitrage mechanics, but the private-credit sleeve inside PRIV cannot
itself be sold in seconds, or in some cases days or weeks, at the marked price. I was **not able to
independently verify or read specific SEC commentary or concerns about PRIV** within this
workstream. I found general secondary references asserting that "regulators are paying closer
attention" to evergreen and semi-liquid vehicles' investor protection, disclosure, liquidity
management and valuation practices [P219] [Reported, single source, no named regulator, no quote,
no date], but nothing I could trace to an actual SEC statement, speech, or comment letter naming
PRIV specifically. Direct searches of the SEC's own speeches-and-statements and press-release
archives did not surface anything on point in the time available. **This is a real gap, and given
the brief's emphasis on the SEC's concerns about PRIV as a named topic, it should be a priority for
follow-up research**, ideally starting from SEC comment letters on the fund's original exemptive
relief application (interval-fund-style redemption terms wrapped inside an ETF ticker) rather than
press coverage. See GAPS and Section 6.

The broader debate this sits inside, stated plainly: private assets are, by definition, hard to
price and hard to sell quickly. Every wrapper discussed in this file, BDC, interval fund, tender
offer fund, ELTIF, LTAF, evergreen fund, ETF, is an attempt to give investors *some* liquidity
without requiring the underlying assets themselves to become liquid. The mechanisms differ (queues
and caps for BDCs and interval funds; a liquidity sleeve and market-maker arbitrage for an ETF like
PRIV), but none of them can conjure real liquidity for the private-credit loans sitting underneath
if many holders want out simultaneously, which is exactly what happened to Blue Owl's OCIC and OTIC
and to Partners Group's Global Value fund in the redemption episodes already established elsewhere
in this project.

## 1.5 Fee and access economics

Fee structures in the wealth channel are harder to make an apples-to-apples comparison for, because
so many of them layer several charges together (management fee, incentive fee/carry, and often a
separate distribution or "servicing" fee paid to the platform or intermediary that sold the fund),
and different fund types quote fees against different bases (percentage of NAV for evergreen and
BDC structures, versus percentage of committed capital for closed-end funds, which is not the same
number even at an identical headline rate).

What I could verify: one industry source states that "all-in costs for tender offer funds typically
range from 250-400 basis points on NAV" and that historically egregious retail-channel front-end
sales loads, which averaged around 7% "over a decade ago," have since been cut roughly in half and
are often waived entirely on newer products [P217] [Confirmed, direct quote sourced and fetched].
Separately, "evergreen structures often layer additional fees, including distribution and servicing
fees" on top of the management fee, and the combined effect is "complex fee structures that are
difficult for investors to fully understand" [P219] [Reported, single source, direct quote].

Put together with the J-curve point in Section 2 (Partners Group's 20% vs. 11% IRR comparison
[P216]), the honest picture for a beginner is: **evergreen vehicles typically charge a higher
ongoing (ad valorem) fee than a comparable closed-end fund's headline rate, but they also avoid the
performance drag of uncalled, uninvested capital sitting idle for years.** Whether an investor comes
out ahead net of fees depends on which effect dominates for a given fund and vintage; nobody I found
has published a clean, apples-to-apples net-of-all-fees comparison across a large enough sample to
settle this generally, and the brief's own instruction to distinguish gross from net returns applies
directly here, so I record this as [Inferred] reasoning built on two separately [Confirmed] facts,
not as a settled empirical finding.

On **intermediaries**: iCapital is confirmed, from its own published materials, to have built
dedicated "tender offer fund" wrappers in partnership with major managers, for example the iCapital-
affiliated iDirect Private Markets Fund built with KKR, Vista Equity Partners and Warburg Pincus,
with an iCapital subsidiary acting as the fund's independent advisor [P217] [Confirmed, primary
source, issuer's own published page]. iCapital frames the addressable "wealth channel" opportunity
at roughly $27 trillion held across more than 19 million accredited-investor households [P217]
[Confirmed, primary source]. I could not find comparably specific, directly-sourced figures for CAIS
within this workstream's time budget (see GAPS); it is understood in the industry to play a similar
role, as a platform connecting independent wealth advisors to alternative-investment products,
alongside iCapital, but I am not going to state figures for it that I have not verified.

## 1.6 The risk this creates

The concern raised by the brief, that the marginal buyer of private credit may increasingly be a
retail (or retail-adjacent, platform-sold) investor who does not fully understand the liquidity
terms, has real, if not fully specific, support in what I found. One piece aimed at institutional
allocators explicitly frames "growth of private wealth channels" as having been ranked, in an ILPA
(Institutional Limited Partners Association) survey, as the **top threat to GP-LP alignment** in
private markets [P212] [Reported, single source, no exact survey date given]. The same source flags
a related mechanical risk: because evergreen and semi-liquid vehicles are typically valued quarterly
rather than continuously, and because managers can earn carried interest on unrealised
("paper") gains before those gains are actually cashed in, there is a structural incentive risk
around what one source calls "NAV squeezing" [P212] [Reported, single source]. Separately, one
industry piece specifically names a "volatility illusion" risk in evergreen structures: because
quarterly (rather than market-based, continuous) valuation smooths reported returns, investors can
be lulled into underestimating how risky the underlying portfolio actually is [P219] [Reported,
single source].

This connects directly to the 2026 redemption episodes already established elsewhere in this
project (Blue Owl's OCIC and OTIC breaching their 5% caps at 21.9% and 40.7% of NAV respectively;
Partners Group's Global Value fund gated at 9.8% of NAV against an $8.6bn base; 10 of 16 tracked
non-traded BDCs breaching redemption caps in Q2 2026 at an average of 10.3%). Those episodes are the
concrete evidence for exactly the mismatch the brief is asking about: a redemption queue nobody
modelled for, hitting products whose legal terms always said "periodic redemption windows, not
liquidity" [P206] [Reported] but which were sold, and increasingly bought by institutions too, on
the promise of flexibility.

**I could not find specific, named regulator commentary on "retailisation" as a labelled
phenomenon** (as opposed to general remarks on private credit risk, already covered elsewhere in
this project via the BIS annual report and Bank of England Financial Stability Report) within this
workstream's search and fetch budget. This is the clearest remaining gap for this section, and I
would prioritise it in any follow-up: check the FCA's and SEC's own published speeches, discussion
papers or Dear CEO / Dear Chair letters specifically on retail or DC-pension access to illiquid
assets, and IOSCO's work on open-ended fund liquidity mismatch, none of which I was able to pull
directly in this pass.

# 6. WHAT A BEGINNER MUST UNDERSTAND FROM THIS

1. **"Evergreen" was built as the consolation prize, and that is exactly why its takeover by
institutions matters.** For decades, the deal was: institutions get closed-end funds (best terms,
lowest fees, longest lockups), retail investors get nothing. Evergreen funds were the industry's
concession to individuals who could not accept a 10-year lockup, deliberately a notch down in terms.
**Best worked example**: KKR's own K-Series evergreen private equity funds, launched in 2023
specifically "designed primarily for wealthy individuals" [P202][P203], have grown to nearly $12bn,
and KKR has now had to renegotiate its own internal deal-allocation rules (7.5% to ~20%) because
those "retail" vehicles need enough deal flow to keep growing [P202][P203][P204][P205]. When the
product built for the B-list starts needing A-list deal access, the old hierarchy is visibly
cracking.

2. **The J-curve and capital-call drag are not abstract; they are worth roughly nine percentage
points of annual return, by one manager's own math.** A beginner should be able to explain, without
notes, why an institution might now prefer a fully-invested evergreen fund over a closed-end one:
committed capital that just sits there for years, uncalled, earns nothing, while fees still often
apply. **Best worked example**: Partners Group's own calculation that a closed-end strategy needs a
20% IRR to match an evergreen fund's 11% IRR [P216], purely because of that idle-capital drag plus
the inability to recycle early exit proceeds. That one comparison does more explanatory work than
any amount of describing "the J-curve" in the abstract.

3. **Liquidity in these products is a queue with a cap, not a promise.** Every wrapper covered here,
BDC, interval fund, ELTIF, LTAF, evergreen fund, even an ETF like PRIV that trades every second the
market is open, is an attempt to manufacture *some* liquidity out of assets that are fundamentally
illiquid, using redemption caps, notice periods, or a liquidity sleeve. None of these mechanisms can
survive everyone wanting out at once. **Best worked example**: PRIV holds a private-credit sleeve
(targeted 10-35% of the portfolio [P226]) inside a wrapper that trades intraday like any stock; that
mismatch, between how fast you can trade the ticker and how fast the manager can actually sell the
underlying loans, is the entire debate about whether private assets belong in a daily-liquid vehicle
at all, in miniature.

4. **Fees are genuinely hard to compare across these structures, and that is itself a talking
point.** Evergreen and BDC-style vehicles quote fees as a percentage of NAV (net asset value, what
the fund is actually worth today), often layering a distribution or servicing fee for the platform
that sold it on top; closed-end funds quote fees against committed capital, a different, usually
lower-sounding number that does not include the capital-drag cost from point 2. **Best worked
example**: tender offer funds' 250-400bps all-in cost on NAV [P217], compared against a "2 and 20"
closed-end structure that sounds cheaper on the surface but can, per Partners Group's own numbers,
need almost double the gross IRR to deliver the same net result once idle capital is accounted for.
A good interview answer distinguishes "which fee is higher" from "which product returns more net of
everything," and shows you know these are different questions.

5. **The risk is genuinely structural, not just a bad headline year.** Retail-adjacent and
now institutional money is flowing into vehicles whose entire commercial promise rests on most
investors not asking for their money back at the same time. Every established 2026 redemption
episode in this project (Blue Owl's OCIC/OTIC, Partners Group's Global Value fund, the wider
non-traded BDC cap breaches) is what happens when that promise is tested. **Best worked example**:
tie this directly to Section 1's inversion story. If institutions, the investors evergreen funds
were never designed for, are now buying in specifically because closed-end funds have stopped
returning cash fast enough, then the marginal buyer base for these products is becoming more varied
(retail individuals plus cash-hungry institutions), and more varied buyer bases with different
liquidity needs are exactly the ingredient that makes a redemption queue unpredictable. That is the
single clearest reason a client portfolio manager for private credit needs to understand liquidity
mechanics cold, not as a compliance footnote, but as the central risk of the job.
