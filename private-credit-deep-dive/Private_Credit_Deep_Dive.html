Part 0

Start here

The ten things that actually matter, in the order they build. If you read only this page you will know more than most people who use the phrase "private credit" in conversation.

  1. It is lending, not owning. The upside is capped at being repaid with interest. The downside is protected by the owners' money underneath you. That asymmetry drives everything. Part 1.2.
  2. The defining feature is that there is no market price. The loan never trades. That is why it can be held calmly through a panic, and why its reported value is an estimate produced by the firm that is paid on the basis of it. Both consequences are real. Part 5.5.
  3. It exists because regulation moved it. After 2008, capital rules and the 2013 US leveraged lending guidance made these loans expensive for banks to hold. The loans went to funds instead. In December 2025 the regulators withdrew that guidance as "overly restrictive"[P6], which is a twist worth knowing. Part 2.2.
  4. Nobody agrees how big it is, and knowing why is a better answer than any single figure. Estimates run from roughly $1.5trn on a narrow definition to about $3.5trn on a broad one, because of scope, dry powder and dates. Part 2.3.
  5. The return is contractual income. Around 8.5% to 9.5% all-in on senior loans in 2026[P61]: a floating reference rate, plus a credit spread, plus fees. You collect interest; you are not waiting for an exit. Part 5.2.
  6. It is mostly senior and secured. Around 86% of documented deals are first lien or unitranche[P51]. The caricature of private credit as exotic is wrong. But US Federal Reserve research put recoveries after default at about 33%, below syndicated loans at 52%[P120], which complicates the standard pitch. Part 5.4.
  7. Headline default rates understate the strain, because amend-and-extend, PIK and covenant waivers keep loans out of the statistics. PIK-featured loans rose from 5.4% to 9.8% of BDC loans between 2022 and 2026[P69]. Part 5.4.
  8. The growth is no longer in corporate lending. Practitioners name asset-based finance and fund finance as the fastest-growing areas[P52]. If you describe private credit as "lending to companies", you are describing 2018. Part 3.
  9. The biggest structural story is insurance. Managers have bought annuity companies to obtain permanent capital, converting fee income into spread income on their own balance sheet. Part 8.1.
  10. The unresolved question is whether the money is as patient as the assets. The industry's best defence is that its capital is locked up. Its growth came from evergreen vehicles where it is not. That tension broke into the open in 2026. Part 8.3 and Part 9.5.
The one sentence that holds it together

Private credit is the business of matching patient money to loans that cannot be sold. Every argument in this document, historical, commercial or regulatory, is a version of the question "is the money actually patient enough?" Banks turned out not to be the right holder. Insurance companies are. Whether quarterly-redemption retail vehicles are is the live question of 2026.

Part 1

What private credit
actually is

Start here. Three ideas, and if you have all three you can follow everything that comes afterwards: what a loan is as an investment, what makes one private, and why that changes everything.

@@ANCHOR:p1a@@

1.1 The definition, and what it is not

Private credit is lending money directly to a company, through a fund rather than a bank, where the loan is negotiated privately between the two sides and never traded on a market.

That is the whole thing. Every complication later is a variation on it.

Pull the sentence apart:

  • Lending, not owning. You are a creditor, not a shareholder. You get interest and your money back. You do not get a share of the upside if the business triples in value. See 1.2, because this is the distinction people get wrong.
  • Directly. One lender, or a small group, negotiating with one borrower. Not hundreds of anonymous buyers of a bond.
  • Through a fund. The money comes from pension schemes, insurers, endowments, sovereign wealth funds and, increasingly, wealthy individuals and ordinary savers. A manager pools it and lends it out.
  • Never traded. There is no screen anywhere showing what the loan is worth. This is the fact that generates both the returns and the anxieties.
Key term · the word "private"

"Private" here does not mean secret or shady. It means not publicly traded. A public bond can be bought and sold by anyone through a broker and has a price that moves every day. A private loan is a bilateral contract that sits on the lender's books until it is repaid. Same underlying idea, completely different plumbing.

1.2 Debt versus equity, in one page

If you take one thing from this document, take this. Private credit and private equity are constantly mentioned together, and they are close to opposites in what they promise.

Imagine a company worth £100m, funded with £60m of borrowed money and £40m of shareholders' money. Now ask what each side gets in three different futures.

The consequence, stated properly

Lending has a capped upside and a protected downside. Owning has an unlimited upside and is first in line for losses. So the two disciplines are completely different jobs:

  • A private equity investor asks "how good could this get?" They need a few investments to be spectacular, because most will be ordinary.
  • A private credit investor asks "what could go wrong, and do I still get paid?" They need almost every loan to perform, because no loan can ever return more than it promised. One total loss can wipe out the margin on twenty good loans.

That asymmetry explains almost everything about how credit people behave: the obsession with downside cases, with collateral, with covenants, and with what the business is worth if it has to be sold in a hurry.

The sentence to remember

Equity investors are paid to be right. Credit investors are paid not to be wrong. If you can explain why that sentence is true using Figure 2, you understand the asset class better than most people who mention it.

1.3 The four things that make a loan "private"

Four attributes travel together. Each one has a benefit and a cost, and the pairs are the reason every argument about this asset class exists.

AttributeWhat it meansWhat it buysWhat it costs

Being untraded genuinely protects a lender from being forced to sell at a bad price. It also means the reported value of the portfolio is produced by the same firm that is paid on the basis of it. Both things are true at once. Someone who only says the first is selling. Someone who only says the second is not paying attention. Part 5.5 works through the mechanics properly.

Part 2

Where it came from,
and why

Private credit did not appear because someone invented a clever product. It appeared because regulation made banks bad owners of certain loans, and the loans had to go somewhere. That sentence is the honest version, and it has a benign reading and a critical one.

@@ANCHOR:p2a@@

2.1 The prehistory

Lending privately is not new. Three strands ran for decades before anyone used the phrase.

  • Insurance private placements. Life insurers have bought privately negotiated corporate debt since the middle of the twentieth century. They have long-dated liabilities and no need to trade, which makes them natural holders of loans that never trade. This is the oldest form of the idea, and Part 8.1 shows it returning in force.
  • Mezzanine funds, 1980s and 1990s. Funds lending the risky layer between bank debt and equity in buyouts. The first recognisably modern private credit strategy.
  • The BDC. The Small Business Investment Incentive Act of 1980, signed on 21 October 1980, created the business development company: a vehicle designed to channel capital to smaller American companies[P4] Confirmed. A BDC must distribute most of its income and faces statutory limits on borrowing. That structure, forty-five years old, is the wrapper inside which much of today's retail private credit sits.
One change that mattered more than it sounds

The Small Business Credit Availability Act, signed 23 March 2018, relaxed BDC leverage limits from 1:1 to 2:1, by lowering the required asset coverage from 200% to 150%[P5] Confirmed.

In plain English: a BDC could suddenly borrow twice as much against the same investor equity. That doubles the loans a given pool of capital can make, and it multiplies both returns and risk. Much of the growth in retail-facing private credit after 2018 rests on this one statutory change, and very few people mentioning "the growth of private credit" know it happened.

@@ANCHOR:p2b@@

2.2 The real origin: banks walked away

After 2008, regulators made banks hold far more capital against risky loans. That made mid-sized corporate lending a poor use of a bank's balance sheet. The demand for loans did not disappear, so the supply moved.

Why the loans moved, in one paragraph

A bank funds itself largely with deposits, which can be withdrawn at any time, and it must hold regulatory capital against the risk of its loans. A credit fund funds itself with money committed for years and holds no regulatory capital in the banking sense. For a loan that will not be sold and may be volatile in value, the fund is the structurally better owner: it cannot be forced to sell by a run, and it is not penalised for holding risk. That is the benign argument, and it is genuinely strong Inferred.

@@ANCHOR:p2c@@

2.3 How big it is, and why nobody agrees

You will see private credit described as a $1.5 trillion market and as a $3.5 trillion market in the same week. Both figures are honest. Understanding why is a genuine signal of competence.

How to talk about the size without being wrong

Never give one number flatly. Say: "estimates run from roughly $1.5 trillion on a narrow definition of corporate lending to about $3.5 trillion if you include asset-based, real estate and infrastructure credit, and part of the spread is dry powder that has not been lent yet." That is accurate, it shows you know the definitions are contested, and it is far more impressive than a confident single figure.

On direction of travel, Moody's has projected roughly $4 trillion by 2030[P11] Reported. Attribute forecasts to whoever made them.

2.4 The zero-rate era, and what its end revealed

For a decade after 2008, interest rates were near zero and investors could not find yield in bonds. Private credit offered more, and money poured in. Then rates rose sharply from 2022, and because these loans are mostly floating rate, two things happened at once:

  • Lenders' income rose automatically. Yields went up without the lender doing anything. Returns looked excellent.
  • Borrowers' interest bills rose by exactly the same amount. The companies had been leveraged on the assumption of cheap money.

The data shows the strain. The share of middle-market borrowers in one dataset unable to cover interest, tax and working capital from their own cash rose from roughly 25% to 30% in 2021 to a peak near 45% in 2023, easing somewhat through 2025[P12] Confirmed. Over the same period, headline borrowing rose from about four times earnings to five or six times, and closer to seven once earnings adjustments are stripped out[P12].

The thing to understand about floating rate

"Floating rate protects you from interest rate risk" is true for the lender and only for the lender. The risk does not vanish. It converts from interest rate risk into credit risk, because the borrower now has a bigger bill. A lender who says floating rate makes the loan safe has moved the risk rather than removed it, and noticing that is one of the more genuinely analytical observations available in this asset class.

@@ANCHOR:p2d@@

2.5 So why does it exist? Both answers

The benign answer

Bank regulation is designed for institutions funded by deposits that can run. Long-term illiquid loans are a bad match for that funding and a good match for pension and insurance money that has no need to sell. Moving those loans from banks to funds puts risk where it can be held patiently, and gets mid-sized companies financed that banks abandoned. The system is safer, not less safe, because the holder cannot be run on.

The critical answer

Nothing about the loans changed. The same credit risk moved from an institution that is supervised, capitalised and stress-tested to one that is none of those things, and which is often financed by the very banks the risk supposedly left. Regulators cannot see it properly, valuations are self-reported, and the growth happened in a period of unusually low defaults. This is regulatory arbitrage with a better story attached.

Both are serious. The most defensible position is that the benign account is largely right about the structure and the critical account is largely right about the visibility: patient capital really is a better owner of illiquid loans, and regulators really cannot see what they would need to see to know whether it has been done prudently. Part 9 tests that Inferred.

Part 3

The strategy map

"Private credit" covers a dozen different businesses with different risks and different returns. Knowing the map is the fastest way to stop sounding like someone who read one article.

3.1 The core: corporate direct lending

This is what most people mean by private credit, and it is where most of the money is. A fund lends to an operating company, usually senior secured, usually floating rate, usually held to maturity.

One structural fact worth carrying: a law firm's review of the loans it documented found that 86% were first lien or unitranche[P51] Confirmed. Private credit is overwhelmingly a senior secured business, not an exotic one. That matters when you meet the caricature of private credit as reckless.

Why NAV lending attracts criticism

A NAV loan is secured against a private equity fund's whole portfolio. Funds have used them to return cash to investors when they cannot sell companies. The objection: investors receive a distribution that looks like a realisation but is actually borrowed money, secured on assets they still own, adding leverage on top of leverage already inside each company.

Defenders say it is ordinary portfolio financing and often cheaper than selling a good asset into a bad market. If you want one topic that marks you out as reading past the headlines, this is a strong candidate.

actually works

The capital structure, where the yield comes from, what covenants do, and the life of a loan from first meeting to repayment. This is the part that makes you sound like someone who has looked at a term sheet.

@@ANCHOR:p4a@@

4.1 The capital structure, and what "senior secured" buys you

Every company funded with a mix of debt and equity has an order of repayment. If things go wrong, money is distributed strictly down the ladder. Nobody on a lower rung gets anything until the rung above is paid in full.

4.2 Unitranche, the signature instrument

Instead of a company arranging a senior loan from one lender and a junior loan from another, with two sets of documents and an inter-creditor fight waiting to happen, a single lender provides one loan at one blended rate.

Behind the scenes, the lenders privately divide it through an agreement among lenders, or AAL. One group takes the safer "first-out" piece at a lower rate; another takes the "last-out" piece at a higher rate and absorbs losses first. The borrower never sees this, and deals with one counterparty.

Spreads on direct lending moved from roughly S+600 to 650 in 2022-23, down to about S+475 by early 2026, then back out to around S+502 by August 2026[P301] Reported.

Read that as a story. Spreads fell because too much money chased too few deals: competition, not improving credit. That is an unusual and interesting mechanism: the price of corporate loans moved because of the behaviour of retail investors in the funds that hold them.

4.4 Covenants, and the argument about them

A maintenance covenant requires a borrower to keep meeting a financial test, typically quarterly. If it fails, the lender gets a seat at the table early, while there is still value to protect. An incurrence covenant only bites when the company actively does something, such as raising more debt.

Private credit's standard claim is that it kept maintenance covenants while the syndicated market abandoned them. The evidence says: true at the smaller end, eroding at the larger end. Covenant retention is reported at around 98% in the lower middle market, while covenant-lite packages reach as much as 93% of larger private credit loans[P66] Reported.

It matters financially. Lenders with maintenance covenants have been found to recover roughly 10 percentage points more than lenders who can only act once a payment is actually missed[P67] Reported.

The add-back problem, which is where the real leverage hides

Loan size is set as a multiple of EBITDA, a measure of earnings. But EBITDA is negotiated. Borrowers argue for "add-backs": costs they say are one-off or will disappear.

One study of 2022-vintage loans found add-backs equal to more than 29% of projected EBITDA and around 55% of the earnings actually reported over the last twelve months. The same body of work found 95% of deals from 2015 to 2020 missed their first-year earnings projections[P68] Reported.

Read that carefully. If earnings are overstated by half, then a loan described as five times earnings may really be seven times. This is the single most important thing to understand about why headline leverage figures understate risk, and it is why lenders now try to cap add-backs at around 25% of earnings[P68].

PIK: the early warning

Payment in kind means the borrower pays interest by adding to the loan balance rather than in cash. It is legitimate flexibility for a growing company and a red flag when it spreads across a portfolio, because it means cash is not being generated.

The share of BDC loans with a PIK feature rose from about 5.4% in early 2022 to 9.8% by early 2026[P69] Reported. Research cited in the same material links amendment-driven PIK to a one to two percentage point increase in the probability of delinquency in the following quarter, against a base rate of roughly 3%[P69].

4.5 The life of a loan

Sourcing

A private equity sponsor buying a company asks several lenders to bid. Around 70% or more of direct lending is sponsor-backed[P70], because the sponsor brings deal flow, equity underneath the loan, and a repeat relationship.

Screening and diligence

Typically three to six weeks[P71]. Financial, commercial, legal. The lender gets access to information no public bond investor ever sees, which is one of the genuine advantages of the format.

Credit committee

The internal approval body. The decision-making heart of the firm, and where a credit culture is actually visible.

Term sheet and documentation

Pricing, leverage, covenants, add-back definitions, delayed draw commitments. Every argument in 4.4 is settled here, in writing.

Funding and monitoring

The loan is drawn. Then quarterly reporting, covenant testing, and regular contact with management. Maturities are typically five to seven years, though loans are often refinanced after three or four[P72].

Amendment, or trouble

Most stress is resolved by negotiation: a waiver, a maturity extension, a switch to PIK, an equity injection from the sponsor. Refinancings were about 28% of all direct lending activity in 2025[P73].

Exit, or restructuring

Repayment or refinancing in the good case. In the bad case, a restructuring in which lenders may take ownership of the business. Part 9 has a real example where exactly that happened.

The detail that shows you understand the job

Most of the work in private credit is not choosing loans. It is the monitoring and the amendments: the quarterly call, the covenant test, the waiver negotiation. A public bond investor who dislikes a company sells it. A private lender cannot sell, so the only available action is to engage. That is why these firms employ restructuring specialists, and why "workout capability" is a genuine differentiator rather than marketing.

5.1 How the manager gets paid

Here are real fee terms, taken from a filed investment advisory agreement of one of the largest listed BDCs. Read the structure rather than the numbers, because the structure is the same across the industry.

The one word that changes everything: "gross"

A management fee charged on gross assets rather than net assets means the manager is paid on borrowed money too. If a fund has £100 of investor equity and borrows £100, it holds £200 of gross assets, so a 1.5% fee on gross assets is effectively 3% of the investor's own money.

Now recall from Part 2.1 that the permitted leverage for a BDC was doubled in March 2018. A structure that lets the manager be paid on borrowed assets, combined with a statutory change that doubled how much could be borrowed, is a powerful incentive. That is not an accusation against any particular firm. It is a feature of the structure that an investor should understand, and it is exactly the sort of thing a client will ask a product specialist about.

Note also that fee terms do move in the investor's favour under competition. One large BDC cut its incentive fee from 20% to 17.5% in an amended agreement in 2021[P101] Confirmed.

Why the headline default rate understates the strain

A loan only shows up as a default if something forces it to. Three mechanisms keep loans out of the statistics:

  • Amend and extend. Push out the maturity and the loan never matures into a default.
  • Switching to PIK. The borrower stops paying cash interest, but by agreement, so nothing is missed. PIK-featured loans rose from 5.4% to 9.8% of BDC loans between 2022 and 2026[P69].
  • Covenant waivers. The test is failed, and the lender agrees to look away.

Analysts estimate a "shadow default" rate of around 6% once these are counted[P119] Reported. That Moody's finding above, that 65% of defaults were distressed restructurings rather than missed payments, is the same phenomenon visible in the official data.

5.5 Valuation: the hardest problem in the asset class

There is no market price. So the value of a loan is an estimate, produced by a firm that is paid on the basis of that estimate. Everyone in the industry knows this. Very few explanations of private credit say it plainly.

The FCA reviewed valuation practices across 36 firms managing around £3 trillion and published its findings in March 2025, identifying weaknesses in governance and in the management of conflicts of interest, and listing seven distinct channels through which conflicts can arise[P125] Confirmed.

The most concrete evidence available, and the best thing in this document to remember

Analysis of BDC holdings found the same loan to the same company, in the same quarter, carried at materially different values by different funds[P126] Confirmed.

In one documented case, a loan to Lithium Technologies was marked at 50 cents, 53 cents and 77 cents on the dollar by three different BDCs in the same quarter. That is a 27-point spread on an identical instrument. Similar divergences were documented for Pluralsight, Auven Therapeutics, Isagenix and Allucent[P126].

Nobody is necessarily doing anything wrong. Valuation of an untraded loan involves judgement, and reasonable people differ. But it is the clearest possible demonstration that "net asset value" in private credit is an opinion, not an observation. If you remember one fact from this entire document, make it this one.

The "volatility laundering" argument

Because values are estimated rather than observed, reported returns are smoother than the underlying reality. Cliff Asness of AQR has argued that this smoothness is itself part of the product's appeal, and coined the phrase volatility laundering for it[P130] Confirmed. His provocative point is that investors may be paying for the appearance of stability.

Academic work attempting to correct for the smoothing found the direct lending index's true volatility understated by roughly 26%, which lowered its risk-adjusted return measure from about 1.39 to 1.03[P131] Reported. Notably, the same analysis concluded the distortion is smaller than in private equity.

Where a thoughtful person lands

The honest synthesis: the returns have been real and the losses genuinely modest, but the risk has been understated because the price is not observed. An investor who is genuinely long-term may not care, since they were never going to sell at the bottom anyway. An investor who might need their money back, or who is using reported volatility to size their allocation, very much should care.

That distinction between the investor who can hold and the investor who might have to sell is the thread connecting Part 5 to Part 8.3 and Part 9. It is the most important idea in this document.

6.3 For the firm doing the lending

Why has every large asset manager rushed into this? Follow the economics from Part 5.1.

  • Fees are far higher than in public markets. A management fee on gross assets plus a 20% incentive fee, against perhaps 50 basis points on a public bond fund.
  • The capital is locked up. A closed-end fund's investors cannot leave, so fee income is contracted for years. In a business where public-market fees are compressing, that predictability is worth enormous amounts.
  • It scales. Once the origination team exists, lending more money does not require proportionally more people.
  • It is defensible. Origination relationships and workout capability are genuinely hard to copy, in a way that buying an index is not.
The honest framing of the manager's incentive

Every structural feature that makes private credit attractive to a manager also creates a tension with the investor. Fees on gross assets reward leverage. Fees on committed capital reward raising money over deploying it well. Self-reported valuations feed the fee calculation. Locked-up capital means an unhappy investor cannot easily leave.

None of this implies bad faith, and the industry has produced real returns. But if you want one question that demonstrates you are thinking rather than reciting, it is: "what is this manager paid on, and what behaviour does that reward?" It is the right question to ask about any asset manager, and it is sharper here than almost anywhere else.

7.2 What actually separates one manager from another

In a market where everyone lends at roughly the same spread against roughly the same companies, four things genuinely differentiate:

Origination

Whether you see the deal at all. Sponsor relationships are the currency, because 70% or more of direct lending is sponsor-backed[P70]. A firm that sees every deal can decline the bad ones; a firm that sees few must take what it is shown.

Scale

The ability to write a billion-dollar cheque alone is a genuine competitive weapon, because it lets a sponsor do a deal in one conversation. It is also the reason the industry has concentrated.

Workout capability

You cannot sell a private loan. When a borrower struggles, your only options are to negotiate or to take the keys. Firms with real restructuring teams recover more. This is under-appreciated and hard to copy.

Permanent capital

Money that never has to be given back, from an insurance balance sheet or an evergreen vehicle. It removes the pressure to deploy before a fund's investment period expires. See 8.1.

7.3 The consolidation wave

Traditional asset managers, facing fee compression in public markets, have been buying private credit capability rather than building it. BlackRock's purchase of HPS is the landmark, alongside Nuveen and Arcmont, Janus Henderson and Victory Park, T. Rowe Price and Oak Hill, Manulife and CQS, Franklin Templeton and Benefit Street, and Blue Owl's own run of acquisitions[P152] Reported.

The strategic logic is simple. A public equity fund earns perhaps 50 basis points and is losing share to index funds. A private credit fund earns 150 basis points plus incentive fees on capital that cannot leave. If you are a large traditional manager, you either build that capability, buy it, or watch your revenue shrink.

Why an annuity company is the perfect partner for a private lender

An annuity is a promise to pay someone an income for decades. So an annuity writer has long-dated, predictable liabilities and cannot be run on in the way a bank can. It needs assets that pay a reliable yield over many years and it does not care about daily prices.

That is an almost perfect match for private credit. And it transforms the manager's own business: instead of earning a fee on someone else's money, which can be withdrawn, the firm owns a permanent balance sheet and earns the spread between what it pays annuity holders and what its loans yield. Fee income is cyclical. Spread income on permanent capital is not.

If you want to understand why Apollo trades as a different kind of company from a traditional asset manager, this is the reason.

The concern, stated fairly. Insurance regulators have noticed. 9.4 The defence, stated at its strongest

Take this seriously, because it is largely correct.

  • The capital cannot run. A bank funds thirty-year assets with deposits repayable today. A closed-end credit fund funds five-year loans with money committed for ten years. That is the opposite of a bank's maturity mismatch, and it is the single best argument in the asset class's favour.
  • Leverage is modest. A BDC is limited to roughly two times. A bank operates at ten times or more.
  • The loans are senior and secured, with equity beneath them, and around 86% are first lien or unitranche[P51].
  • It has been tested. The 2022 to 2024 rate shock more than doubled borrowing costs for these companies. Defaults rose but stayed in low single digits[P115][P117]. That is real evidence, not a promise.
  • The exposure is small relative to banking. Under 0.5% of bank assets on FSB data[P173]; around 1.3% of equity in the ECB's euro-area stress[P255].

Part 10

Forming your own view

Five questions where thoughtful people disagree. For each, the strongest version of both sides. You will be asked at least one of these, and the answer they want is a reasoned position, not a summary.

Is the extra return real, or an illusion of smooth pricing?

Real

The income is contractual and arrives in cash. Twenty-year returns of around 9.5% with cumulative losses of about 1% against 1.5% for high yield are not an accounting artefact[P110]. Someone genuinely paid that interest.

Partly an illusion

The return may be real while the risk is understated. Unsmoothing work found true volatility understated by around 26%, cutting the risk-adjusted measure from 1.39 to 1.03[P131]. If you size an allocation using reported volatility, you are taking more risk than you think.

Where to land: both, and the distinction matters. The return is real; the reported stability is manufactured by the absence of a price. For an investor who genuinely cannot sell, that may be harmless or even helpful. For one who might need the money, it is a trap.

Has the asset class outgrown its opportunity?

Yes

Spreads compressed from S+600-650 to about S+475 by early 2026[P301], which is competition rather than improving credit. Add-backs inflate earnings by as much as half[P68]. Covenant-lite reaches 93% of larger loans[P66]. Too much money chasing the same deals.

No

The borrower universe is still expanding, banks remain constrained, and growth has shifted to genuinely different areas: asset-based finance and fund finance rather than more of the same corporate lending[P52]. And spreads widened again in 2026.

Where to land: segment it. Large-cap sponsor lending looks crowded, and the syndicated market won 63% of large buyouts back in 2025[P62]. The lower middle market and asset-based finance look less so. "It depends which part" is the right answer and shows you know there are parts.

Should ordinary savers be in this at all?

Yes

Excluding ordinary savers from the asset classes institutions use is its own unfairness. A pension saver has a thirty-year horizon, which is exactly the patient capital these loans need. Policy in both the US and UK has deliberately moved this way[P205].

No

The 2026 redemption episodes showed the newest investors did not understand the exit terms. A default-fund member never chose this. And the product's fee load is heaviest precisely in the retail wrappers.

Where to land: the honest position separates the asset from the wrapper. Private credit inside a thirty-year pension default is a reasonable match. Private credit in a vehicle someone expects to be able to exit quarterly is a mismatch waiting to be discovered, and that is what 2026 discovered.

Is it a systemic risk?

Covered in 9.5. Short version: probably not on current evidence, because it is small relative to banking and the capital is mostly locked. The thing to watch is whether the newer, less patient money changes that.

Does it actually help the economy?

Evidence on both sides in 6.2. Academic work finds employment and innovation benefits at borrowers[P303]; the British Business Bank uses private credit deliberately to fill a UK mid-market gap[P304]. Against that, it enables higher leverage and may sustain companies that should restructure.

How to hold any of these in an interview

State the position, give the strongest counter-argument yourself, then say what would change your mind. That last clause is the one that marks out a credit mind: people who lend for a living think constantly about what would make them wrong, because being wrong costs them everything and being right pays a coupon.

The J-curve needs explainingLess severe in credit than private equity because income arrives early, but still real.
Liquidity terms are now the main eventAfter 2026, every conversation about a semi-liquid vehicle involves the cap, the queue and the notice period. Part 8.3.
Due diligence is adversarialA consultant's questionnaire on a credit fund asks about valuation governance, workout capability, PIK exposure and covenant packages. These are technical questions requiring technical answers.
Key terms · the performance vocabulary, defined properly
  • IRR — internal rate of return. Annualised, accounts for timing. Can be flattered by borrowing early so investor cash is called later.
  • MOIC — multiple on invested capital. How many times the money came back, ignoring time entirely.
  • DPI — distributions to paid-in. Cash actually returned, over cash put in. The number that cannot be manipulated by a valuation.
  • RVPI — residual value to paid-in. What is still held, at the manager's estimate.
  • TVPI — total value to paid-in. DPI plus RVPI. The headline multiple.
  • Vintage year — the year the fund began investing. Funds are compared within vintages because conditions dominate.

If you learn one relationship: TVPI = DPI + RVPI. A fund with a high TVPI and a low DPI is telling you most of its reported value is still unrealised and rests on the manager's own estimate. Asking for the DPI split is the single most revealing question an investor can ask, and knowing that is a genuine signal.

11.4 Fifteen questions that test whether you actually know this

If you are going to say you want to specialise in private credit, assume you will get several of these. Each note says what a good answer contains, not what to say.

  1. What is private credit? Should include: direct lending, not traded, usually senior secured and floating rate, held to maturity. Part 1.1.
  2. How is it different from private equity? Capped upside, protected downside, contractual income rather than exit-driven gains. Part 1.2.
  3. Why does it exist? Bank capital rules after 2008, and the 2013 leveraged lending guidance. Bonus for knowing that guidance was withdrawn in December 2025. Part 2.2.
  4. How big is it? A range with the reason for the range, not a single number. Part 2.3.
  5. Where does the return come from? Reference rate, plus spread, plus OID and fees, minus losses, fees and leverage cost. Part 5.2.
  6. What is a unitranche? One blended loan, split privately behind the scenes through an agreement among lenders. Part 4.2.
  7. How do you value a loan that does not trade? Mark to model, third-party agents, board oversight, and honest acknowledgement of the conflict. Part 5.5.
  8. IRR versus MOIC versus DPI? See the box above. Knowing why DPI is the hardest to flatter is the differentiator.
  9. What is PIK and why does it matter? Interest paid in more debt. A legitimate tool and a stress signal when it spreads. Part 4.4.
  10. What happens when a borrower breaches a covenant? Negotiation, waiver, amendment, possibly ownership. You cannot sell. Part 4.5.
  11. What is a gate and why do these funds have them? Part 8.3, plus the 2026 episodes.
  12. Are default rates a good measure of stress? No, and the reasons are amend-and-extend, PIK and waivers. Part 5.4.
  13. What is the biggest risk in private credit? A view, with a mechanism. Part 9.5 gives you the transmission chain.
  14. Why are institutions buying evergreen funds? Your FT article. Capital-call drag, the J-curve, slow distributions, and the 20% versus 11% arithmetic. Part 8.3.
  15. Why do you want to work on this rather than equities? The honest personal answer. If it is that the analysis is about downside and structure rather than forecasting upside, say that.

Glossary

78 terms. If you can define forty of these, you can hold a conversation with a private credit investor. If you can define all of them, you can hold one with their client.

Add-back
An adjustment that increases reported EBITDA for costs a company argues are one-off or will disappear. Heavily negotiated, because the more add-backs are allowed, the more the company can borrow against the same real earnings.
Agreement among lenders (AAL)
The private contract that splits a unitranche loan into a safer first-out piece and a riskier last-out piece. The borrower sees one loan; the lenders privately divide the risk.
Amend and extend
Renegotiating a loan to push out its maturity rather than letting it default. Sensible workout tool, and also a way a troubled loan can avoid ever appearing in default statistics.
Amortisation
Repaying principal in instalments over the life of a loan, rather than all at maturity. Most direct lending is bullet repayment with little amortisation.
Asset-based finance (ABF)
Lending secured against specific assets or cash flows rather than a whole company: receivables, equipment, royalties, aircraft, consumer loans. The fastest-growing corner of private credit.
AUM
Assets under management. For private credit, read carefully: some figures include undrawn commitments (dry powder) and some do not, which is why market-size estimates differ so much.
BDC
Business development company. A US listed or non-traded vehicle created by legislation in 1980 to channel capital to smaller companies. Must distribute most of its income and faces limits on leverage.
Bilateral
A loan between one lender and one borrower, with no syndication.
Broadly syndicated loan (BSL)
A large loan arranged by a bank and sold in slices to many institutional investors. It trades, so it has a market price. Private credit's main competitor.
Bullet
A loan repaid in a single lump at maturity.
Capital call
A demand from a fund to its investors to send in a portion of the money they committed. The mechanism by which closed-end funds draw capital as deals arise.
Carried interest
The manager's share of profits above a hurdle, traditionally 20% in private equity and often lower in credit.
CLO
Collateralised loan obligation. A vehicle that buys a pool of loans and issues tranches of securities against them. A financing structure, not an asset class.
Club deal
A loan provided by a small group of lenders who know each other, rather than one lender or a broad syndicate.
Cov-lite
A loan without maintenance covenants. Common in the syndicated market. Private credit has historically retained maintenance covenants, though less universally than its marketing suggests.
Covenant
A promise in a loan agreement. A maintenance covenant must be met on a continuing basis and is tested regularly. An incurrence covenant only bites when the company does something specific, such as taking on more debt.
Credit committee
The internal body that approves or rejects a loan. The decision-making heart of a lending firm.
Default
Failure to meet an obligation under the loan agreement. Note that a covenant breach is a default even if every interest payment has been made, which is why covenants matter so much.
Delayed draw term loan (DDTL)
A commitment to lend more later, typically to fund acquisitions. Valuable to a borrower planning to buy other businesses.
Direct lending
The core of private credit: funds lending directly to companies, usually senior secured and floating rate.
Distressed debt
Buying the debt of companies already in serious trouble, usually at a deep discount, often with the aim of converting it into ownership.
DPI
Distributions to paid-in capital. How much cash a fund has actually returned, divided by how much investors have put in. The number that cannot be flattered by a valuation.
Drawdown fund
The traditional closed-end structure: commit for around ten years, capital is called as needed, money is returned as investments are realised, and there are no redemptions.
Dry powder
Capital that investors have committed but the fund has not yet invested.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. The rough proxy for cash generation against which loan size is measured. Its definition is negotiated, which is why add-backs matter.
ELTIF
European Long-Term Investment Fund. The EU structure for holding illiquid assets, reformed as ELTIF 2.0 to widen retail access.
Evergreen fund
A fund with no end date that accepts money continuously and allows limited periodic withdrawals, usually quarterly and typically capped at 5% of value.
First lien
The debt with the first claim on collateral in a default. The safest position in the capital structure.
First-out / last-out
The two halves of a unitranche. The first-out piece is repaid first and earns less; the last-out earns more and absorbs loss first.
Floating rate
Interest that resets with a reference rate. Most private credit is floating, so income rises when rates rise.
Fund finance
Lending to funds themselves rather than to operating companies. Includes subscription lines and NAV loans.
Gate
A limit on how much can be withdrawn from a fund in a given period.
Hurdle rate
The return a fund must exceed before the manager earns performance fees. Typically expressed as an annual percentage.
Illiquidity premium
The extra return an investor expects for accepting that they cannot sell. Whether it is genuinely earned or partly an artefact of not marking to market is a live debate.
Incurrence covenant
A restriction that applies only when a company takes a specific action, such as raising more debt.
Interest coverage ratio
Earnings divided by interest expense. How many times over a company can pay its interest bill. The single most watched stress indicator in private credit.
Interval fund
A US registered fund that must offer to repurchase between 5% and 25% of its shares each period under SEC Rule 23c-3.
IRR
Internal rate of return. An annualised return that accounts for the timing of cash flows. Can be flattered by using borrowed money early to delay calling investor capital.
J-curve
The tendency for a fund's early returns to look negative because fees are charged before investments mature. Less pronounced in credit than in private equity, because credit pays income from the start.
Junior debt
Debt ranking behind senior debt for repayment. Higher return, first to lose after equity.
Leverage
Borrowed money. It appears at three levels in private credit: inside the borrowing company, inside the fund, and sometimes at the investor. Each layer multiplies both return and risk.
Liquidity sleeve
Cash and readily saleable assets a semi-liquid fund holds specifically to meet withdrawals.
LTAF
Long-Term Asset Fund. The UK structure for holding illiquid assets, created by the FCA with a minimum 90-day redemption notice period and dealing no more often than monthly.
Mark to model
Valuing an asset using an internal estimate because no market price exists. Standard practice in private credit and the source of most valuation controversy.
Maturity
When the loan must be repaid.
Mezzanine
Debt sitting between senior debt and equity, often with an equity component. Higher return, higher risk.
Middle market
The band of companies private credit traditionally serves. Definitions vary; commonly companies with EBITDA between roughly $10m and $100m, with an upper middle market above that.
MOIC
Multiple on invested capital. How many times the original investment has been returned, ignoring timing. Reported alongside IRR because each hides something the other reveals.
NAV
Net asset value. What a fund says its holdings are worth, after debt. In private credit this is an estimate, not an observed price.
NAV loan
A loan secured against a fund's portfolio of investments rather than against a single company. Controversial because it can add leverage at the fund level to return cash to investors.
Non-sponsored
A borrower not owned by a private equity firm. Usually harder to source, often better priced, and requires more diligence.
OID
Original issue discount. Lending £98 for a loan that repays £100, which raises the effective yield. A standard way of adding return at the front.
Originate
To source, structure and make a loan yourself, rather than buying one someone else made. Origination capability is the main thing that separates private credit managers.
PIK
Payment in kind. Interest paid by increasing the loan balance rather than in cash. Useful flexibility for a growing borrower, and a warning sign when it rises across a portfolio.
PIK toggle
A provision letting a borrower choose to pay interest in kind rather than cash, usually at a higher rate.
Private placement
A security sold directly to a small number of investors rather than publicly offered. Insurance companies have done this for decades, and it is a forerunner of modern private credit.
Pro rata
Proportionate. When withdrawal requests exceed a fund's cap, each investor typically receives the same proportion of what they asked for.
Recovery rate
How much a lender gets back on a defaulted loan, as a percentage of what was owed. Just as important as the default rate, and less often quoted.
Reference rate
The floating benchmark a loan is priced over: SOFR in the US, SONIA in sterling, EURIBOR in euros.
RVPI
Residual value to paid-in capital. The value of what a fund still holds, relative to what investors contributed. The unrealised half of TVPI.
Second lien
Debt ranking behind first lien on the same collateral.
Senior secured
Debt with the first claim on repayment and specific assets pledged against it. The bulk of direct lending.
SOFR
Secured Overnight Financing Rate. The main US floating reference rate, which replaced US dollar LIBOR.
Special situations
Investing in complex or stressed circumstances that do not fit standard categories. Sits between direct lending and distressed.
Sponsor
A private equity firm that owns the borrowing company. Sponsor-backed lending is the majority of direct lending, because the sponsor supplies equity, governance and a repeat relationship.
Spread
The margin over the reference rate. Quoted as, for example, SOFR plus 500 basis points.
SRT
Significant risk transfer. A bank buys protection on a portfolio of its loans, transferring credit risk to an investor while keeping the loans. A fast-growing overlap between banks and private credit.
Subordinated
Ranking behind other debt for repayment.
Subscription line
A short-term loan to a fund secured against investors' unfunded commitments. Smooths cash flow, and flatters IRR by delaying capital calls.
Syndication
Selling parts of a loan to other lenders.
Tranche
A slice of a financing with its own terms and ranking.
TVPI
Total value to paid-in capital. Distributions plus remaining value, over capital contributed. The headline multiple.
Unitranche
A single blended loan that replaces separate senior and junior tranches. The signature private credit instrument: one lender, one document, one price, with the risk split privately behind the scenes.
Venture debt
Lending to young, loss-making companies, usually alongside venture capital equity and often with warrants attached.
Vintage year
The year a fund started investing. Vintages are compared with each other because market conditions dominate outcomes.
Waterfall
The order in which money from a company or a fund is distributed. Senior debt first, then junior, then equity.
Workout
The process of dealing with a loan that has gone wrong: renegotiating, restructuring, or enforcing security.
Yield to maturity
The total annualised return on a loan if held to repayment, including spread, fees and any discount.

check yourself

What this research could not establish, and which claims are thinner than they look. Included because a document about an opaque asset class that pretended to be complete would be missing its own point.

14.1 Claims to handle with care

ClaimThe problemWhat to do
That USS is buying evergreen funds Search summaries of the Financial Times report name it. No reliable fetchable source corroborated it, and the trade outlets read in full name no institution[P203]. The FT was unreachable. Do not name the institution. The structural claim is well supported; the name is not.
Recovery rate of 33% One Federal Reserve study[P120]. Industry sources have historically claimed much higher figures. It has not been reconciled. Attribute it explicitly. It is a strong point precisely because it is contested.
The add-back figures The 29% and 55% figures come from a study whose primary text was paywalled[P68]. Say "studies have found add-backs of roughly half of reported earnings" rather than quoting precisely.
Spread levels Direct lending spreads are not published on an exchange. Every figure is a vendor or adviser estimate, and they differ[P301]. Use them for direction of travel, not as precise levels.
Market size and forecasts Covered in Part 2.3. Forecasts to 2030 are firms' projections, several with an interest in the answer. Always attribute a forecast to whoever made it.
AUM league table figures Not like-for-like across firms[P150]. Use for ranking and scale only.
Pay ranges Two US data points, base salary only, no UK disclosure[P343]. Do not treat as a market rate.

14.2 What could not be established

  • The original FT article. The site was unreachable. Everything about the institutional-into-evergreen story here comes from corroborating trade coverage.
  • Any regulator commentary specifically labelling "retailisation" as a risk. Sought, not found at citable quality. Regulators discuss liquidity mismatch and valuation; the retail framing appears mostly in press.
  • Asia-Pacific private credit is essentially unresearched here. The document is US and Europe weighted, and the UK material is thinner than the US material.
  • A clean year-by-year return series comparing direct lending against syndicated loans, high yield and equities. The index figures quoted are point estimates from summaries, not a built series.
  • The exact numeric thresholds in the 2013 leveraged lending guidance. Its existence, date, issuing agencies and December 2025 withdrawal are confirmed; the specific leverage limits it set are not.
  • Any 2026 named borrower collapse beyond those in Part 9.1. Absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

  1. Read the FSB report of 6 May 2026 on vulnerabilities in private credit. Free, primary, and it is the single best document on the risk debate.
  2. Read the FCA valuation review of March 2025. Short, and it makes Part 5.5 concrete.

  4. Read the Alternative Credit Council's Financing the Economy for the industry's own account, and notice where it differs from the regulators'.
  5. Find and read your FT article properly, so you can name the institutions yourself rather than relying on this document's caveat.
A last thought on why this asset class suits the job you want

Private credit has no price, contested valuations, four different default rates, market-size estimates that vary twofold, and a defence that rests on an assumption about investor behaviour that was tested for the first time in 2026.

That is either a reason to avoid it or a reason it needs people who can explain things honestly. An asset class where the numbers are unambiguous does not need a translator. This one does. If you want an answer to "why private credit?" that is about the work rather than the returns, that is the strongest one available, and it happens to be true.
