Part 10

Forming your own view

Five questions where thoughtful people disagree. For each, the strongest version of both sides. You will be asked at least one of these, and the answer they want is a reasoned position, not a summary.

Is the extra return real, or an illusion of smooth pricing?

Real

The income is contractual and arrives in cash. Twenty-year returns of around 9.5% with cumulative losses of about 1% against 1.5% for high yield are not an accounting artefact[P110]. Someone genuinely paid that interest.

Partly an illusion

The return may be real while the risk is understated. Unsmoothing work found true volatility understated by around 26%, cutting the risk-adjusted measure from 1.39 to 1.03[P131]. If you size an allocation using reported volatility, you are taking more risk than you think.

Where to land: both, and the distinction matters. The return is real; the reported stability is manufactured by the absence of a price. For an investor who genuinely cannot sell, that may be harmless or even helpful. For one who might need the money, it is a trap.

Has the asset class outgrown its opportunity?

Yes

Spreads compressed from S+600-650 to about S+475 by early 2026[P301], which is competition rather than improving credit. Add-backs inflate earnings by as much as half[P68]. Covenant-lite reaches 93% of larger loans[P66]. Too much money chasing the same deals.

No

The borrower universe is still expanding, banks remain constrained, and growth has shifted to genuinely different areas: asset-based finance and fund finance rather than more of the same corporate lending[P52]. And spreads widened again in 2026.

Where to land: segment it. Large-cap sponsor lending looks crowded, and the syndicated market won 63% of large buyouts back in 2025[P62]. The lower middle market and asset-based finance look less so. "It depends which part" is the right answer and shows you know there are parts.

Should ordinary savers be in this at all?

Yes

Excluding ordinary savers from the asset classes institutions use is its own unfairness. A pension saver has a thirty-year horizon, which is exactly the patient capital these loans need. Policy in both the US and UK has deliberately moved this way[P205].

No

The 2026 redemption episodes showed the newest investors did not understand the exit terms. A default-fund member never chose this. And the product's fee load is heaviest precisely in the retail wrappers.

Where to land: the honest position separates the asset from the wrapper. Private credit inside a thirty-year pension default is a reasonable match. Private credit in a vehicle someone expects to be able to exit quarterly is a mismatch waiting to be discovered, and that is what 2026 discovered.

Is it a systemic risk?

Covered in 9.5. Short version: probably not on current evidence, because it is small relative to banking and the capital is mostly locked. The thing to watch is whether the newer, less patient money changes that.

Does it actually help the economy?

Evidence on both sides in 6.2. Academic work finds employment and innovation benefits at borrowers[P303]; the British Business Bank uses private credit deliberately to fill a UK mid-market gap[P304]. Against that, it enables higher leverage and may sustain companies that should restructure.

How to hold any of these in an interview

State the position, give the strongest counter-argument yourself, then say what would change your mind. That last clause is the one that marks out a credit mind: people who lend for a living think constantly about what would make them wrong, because being wrong costs them everything and being right pays a coupon.
