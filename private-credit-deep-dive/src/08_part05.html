5.1 How the manager gets paid

Here are real fee terms, taken from a filed investment advisory agreement of one of the largest listed BDCs. Read the structure rather than the numbers, because the structure is the same across the industry.

The one word that changes everything: "gross"

A management fee charged on gross assets rather than net assets means the manager is paid on borrowed money too. If a fund has £100 of investor equity and borrows £100, it holds £200 of gross assets, so a 1.5% fee on gross assets is effectively 3% of the investor's own money.

Now recall from Part 2.1 that the permitted leverage for a BDC was doubled in March 2018. A structure that lets the manager be paid on borrowed assets, combined with a statutory change that doubled how much could be borrowed, is a powerful incentive. That is not an accusation against any particular firm. It is a feature of the structure that an investor should understand, and it is exactly the sort of thing a client will ask a product specialist about.

Note also that fee terms do move in the investor's favour under competition. One large BDC cut its incentive fee from 20% to 17.5% in an amended agreement in 2021[P101] Confirmed.

Why the headline default rate understates the strain

A loan only shows up as a default if something forces it to. Three mechanisms keep loans out of the statistics:

  • Amend and extend. Push out the maturity and the loan never matures into a default.
  • Switching to PIK. The borrower stops paying cash interest, but by agreement, so nothing is missed. PIK-featured loans rose from 5.4% to 9.8% of BDC loans between 2022 and 2026[P69].
  • Covenant waivers. The test is failed, and the lender agrees to look away.

Analysts estimate a "shadow default" rate of around 6% once these are counted[P119] Reported. That Moody's finding above, that 65% of defaults were distressed restructurings rather than missed payments, is the same phenomenon visible in the official data.

5.5 Valuation: the hardest problem in the asset class

There is no market price. So the value of a loan is an estimate, produced by a firm that is paid on the basis of that estimate. Everyone in the industry knows this. Very few explanations of private credit say it plainly.

The FCA reviewed valuation practices across 36 firms managing around £3 trillion and published its findings in March 2025, identifying weaknesses in governance and in the management of conflicts of interest, and listing seven distinct channels through which conflicts can arise[P125] Confirmed.

The most concrete evidence available, and the best thing in this document to remember

Analysis of BDC holdings found the same loan to the same company, in the same quarter, carried at materially different values by different funds[P126] Confirmed.

In one documented case, a loan to Lithium Technologies was marked at 50 cents, 53 cents and 77 cents on the dollar by three different BDCs in the same quarter. That is a 27-point spread on an identical instrument. Similar divergences were documented for Pluralsight, Auven Therapeutics, Isagenix and Allucent[P126].

Nobody is necessarily doing anything wrong. Valuation of an untraded loan involves judgement, and reasonable people differ. But it is the clearest possible demonstration that "net asset value" in private credit is an opinion, not an observation. If you remember one fact from this entire document, make it this one.

The "volatility laundering" argument

Because values are estimated rather than observed, reported returns are smoother than the underlying reality. Cliff Asness of AQR has argued that this smoothness is itself part of the product's appeal, and coined the phrase volatility laundering for it[P130] Confirmed. His provocative point is that investors may be paying for the appearance of stability.

Academic work attempting to correct for the smoothing found the direct lending index's true volatility understated by roughly 26%, which lowered its risk-adjusted return measure from about 1.39 to 1.03[P131] Reported. Notably, the same analysis concluded the distortion is smaller than in private equity.

Where a thoughtful person lands

The honest synthesis: the returns have been real and the losses genuinely modest, but the risk has been understated because the price is not observed. An investor who is genuinely long-term may not care, since they were never going to sell at the bottom anyway. An investor who might need their money back, or who is using reported volatility to size their allocation, very much should care.

That distinction between the investor who can hold and the investor who might have to sell is the thread connecting Part 5 to Part 8.3 and Part 9. It is the most important idea in this document.
