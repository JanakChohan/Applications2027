Part 9

The bear case, and
the real blow-ups

What the regulators actually say, what the critics actually say, three companies that actually failed, and the strongest version of the defence. Anyone can say "private credit is risky". Naming the mechanism and the case is what separates you.

9.1 Three real failures worth knowing by name

Speaking in generalities about risk is easy. Naming three situations and what each revealed is how you demonstrate you have actually followed this.

First Brands · collapsed 28 September 2025The off-balance-sheet lesson
What happened
An auto parts supplier collapsed with around $11.5bn of debt, of which roughly $5.4bn sat off balance sheet across 112 separate special purpose vehicles[P251] Confirmed.
What it revealed
Lenders did not know the full extent of the borrowing. Supply-chain and receivables financing sat outside the accounts lenders were analysing. This is not a credit judgement failure, it is a visibility failure.
Why it matters to the argument
It is the single strongest piece of evidence for the critics' case: if experienced lenders could miss $5.4bn of obligations at one borrower, the claim that private lenders have superior information needs qualifying.
Tricolor HoldingsThe fraud lesson
What happened
A subprime auto lender collapsed after what regulators characterised as double-pledging of collateral, meaning the same assets were pledged to more than one lender. The SEC brought charges on 18 August 2026. JPMorgan disclosed a loss of around $170m[P252] Reported.
What it revealed
Secured lending is only as good as the security. "Senior secured" is a claim about paperwork, and paperwork can be falsified.
The honest caveat
Fraud is not a private credit phenomenon. Banks have been defrauded for centuries. The fair point is narrower: rapid growth and competition for deals reduce the time spent verifying.
Pluralsight · August 2024The restructuring lesson
What happened
A technology education company with roughly $1.2bn to $1.3bn of debt was restructured. Lenders led by Blue Owl and Ares converted debt to equity and took 100% ownership, with the private equity sponsor's equity written off[P253] Confirmed.
What it revealed
Two things, pulling in opposite directions. The process worked: lenders enforced, took the keys, and preserved value without a public bankruptcy. And a sponsor's equity cushion vanished entirely, which is what the cushion is for.
Use it carefully
Critics cite it as evidence of losses. Defenders cite it as evidence the structure functions. Both readings are legitimate, and saying so is more impressive than picking one.

9.3 The named critics, and the named defenders

The critics
  • Jamie Dimon, JPMorgan, 15 October 2025: the "cockroaches" remark, made in the wake of the Tricolor and First Brands failures, to the effect that when you see one problem there are usually more[P257].
  • Mohamed El-Erian, on the risk of investors treating semi-liquid funds as a cash machine[P258].
  • Senator Elizabeth Warren, letters to ratings agencies, July 2025, on ratings of private credit[P259].
  • Carson Block of Muddy Waters, known for short selling, publicly turning attention to credit[P260].
  • Cliff Asness of AQR, on volatility laundering. Part 5.5.
The defenders
  • Marc Rowan, Apollo, detailed rebuttals dated 24 November 2025, arguing the risk is mispriced by critics who conflate illiquidity with credit risk[P261].
  • Jon Gray, Blackstone, pointing to the actual credit metrics of its largest vehicle[P262].
  • Michael Arougheti, Ares, publicly rejecting a bank's elevated default forecast[P263].
  • And the structural argument: locked-up capital cannot run, leverage is far below bank levels, and the loans are senior and secured.

9.4 The defence, stated at its strongest

Take this seriously, because it is largely correct.

  • The capital cannot run. A bank funds thirty-year assets with deposits repayable today. A closed-end credit fund funds five-year loans with money committed for ten years. That is the opposite of a bank's maturity mismatch, and it is the single best argument in the asset class's favour.
  • Leverage is modest. A BDC is limited to roughly two times. A bank operates at ten times or more.
  • The loans are senior and secured, with equity beneath them, and around 86% are first lien or unitranche[P51].
  • It has been tested. The 2022 to 2024 rate shock more than doubled borrowing costs for these companies. Defaults rose but stayed in low single digits[P115][P117]. That is real evidence, not a promise.
  • The exposure is small relative to banking. Under 0.5% of bank assets on FSB data[P173]; around 1.3% of equity in the ECB's euro-area stress[P255].

9.5 The honest verdict

Your own view goes here

You do not have to agree with the verdict above, and an interviewer will be more interested in how you reason than in where you land. But notice what the argument turns on. It is not really about credit quality. It is about whether the liability side, the money funding the loans, matches the asset side. That is the same question as Part 2.2, where banks turned out to be a poor match for these loans, and the same question as Part 8.3, where the industry went looking for new money.

If you can see that the whole history of this asset class is one repeated question about matching patient money to patient assets, you are thinking about it properly.
