each of the three sides

A market only survives if everyone in it is getting something. Here is what each of the three parties actually gets, and what each gives up. The borrower's side is the one most explanations skip, and it is the one that shows you have thought about it.

6.2 For the borrowing company

This is the half of the argument that gets left out, and it is the half that explains why the asset class kept growing even when cheaper money was available elsewhere.

The competition runs both ways, which is worth knowing

The simple story is "private credit is eating the syndicated loan market". The data says it is a back-and-forth. In 2025, 63% of US leveraged buyouts above $1bn were financed in the syndicated market, up from 39% in 2023[P62] Confirmed. The syndicated market reopened, repriced, and won large deals back.

Deals move in both directions. Named borrowers have refinanced out of private credit into cheaper syndicated debt, and others have moved the other way[P302]. Anyone who tells you this is a one-way disruption has not looked at 2025.

Does it help the real economy?

Evidence for: academic work has found that access to BDC financing causally raises borrower employment growth and patenting[P303] Reported. In the UK, the state-backed British Business Bank has itself committed capital to private credit funds explicitly to fill a mid-market financing gap[P304] Confirmed. That is a government agency agreeing the gap is real.

Evidence against: private credit enables higher leverage in buyouts, which transfers risk to employees and other creditors. And amend-and-extend may keep companies alive that should be restructured, the "zombie" critique, with the rise in PIK from 5.4% to 9.8% of loans as circumstantial support[P69].

6.3 For the firm doing the lending

Why has every large asset manager rushed into this? Follow the economics from Part 5.1.

  • Fees are far higher than in public markets. A management fee on gross assets plus a 20% incentive fee, against perhaps 50 basis points on a public bond fund.
  • The capital is locked up. A closed-end fund's investors cannot leave, so fee income is contracted for years. In a business where public-market fees are compressing, that predictability is worth enormous amounts.
  • It scales. Once the origination team exists, lending more money does not require proportionally more people.
  • It is defensible. Origination relationships and workout capability are genuinely hard to copy, in a way that buying an index is not.
The honest framing of the manager's incentive

Every structural feature that makes private credit attractive to a manager also creates a tension with the investor. Fees on gross assets reward leverage. Fees on committed capital reward raising money over deploying it well. Self-reported valuations feed the fee calculation. Locked-up capital means an unhappy investor cannot easily leave.

None of this implies bad faith, and the industry has produced real returns. But if you want one question that demonstrates you are thinking rather than reciting, it is: "what is this manager paid on, and what behaviour does that reward?" It is the right question to ask about any asset manager, and it is sharper here than almost anywhere else.
