Part 14

Gaps, and what to
check yourself

What this research could not establish, and which claims are thinner than they look. Included because a document about an opaque asset class that pretended to be complete would be missing its own point.

14.1 Claims to handle with care

ClaimThe problemWhat to do
That USS is buying evergreen funds Search summaries of the Financial Times report name it. No reliable fetchable source corroborated it, and the trade outlets read in full name no institution[P203]. The FT was unreachable. Do not name the institution. The structural claim is well supported; the name is not.
Recovery rate of 33% One Federal Reserve study[P120]. Industry sources have historically claimed much higher figures. It has not been reconciled. Attribute it explicitly. It is a strong point precisely because it is contested.
The add-back figures The 29% and 55% figures come from a study whose primary text was paywalled[P68]. Say "studies have found add-backs of roughly half of reported earnings" rather than quoting precisely.
Spread levels Direct lending spreads are not published on an exchange. Every figure is a vendor or adviser estimate, and they differ[P301]. Use them for direction of travel, not as precise levels.
Market size and forecasts Covered in Part 2.3. Forecasts to 2030 are firms' projections, several with an interest in the answer. Always attribute a forecast to whoever made it.
AUM league table figures Not like-for-like across firms[P150]. Use for ranking and scale only.
Pay ranges Two US data points, base salary only, no UK disclosure[P343]. Do not treat as a market rate.

14.2 What could not be established

  • The original FT article. The site was unreachable. Everything about the institutional-into-evergreen story here comes from corroborating trade coverage.
  • Any regulator commentary specifically labelling "retailisation" as a risk. Sought, not found at citable quality. Regulators discuss liquidity mismatch and valuation; the retail framing appears mostly in press.
  • Asia-Pacific private credit is essentially unresearched here. The document is US and Europe weighted, and the UK material is thinner than the US material.
  • A clean year-by-year return series comparing direct lending against syndicated loans, high yield and equities. The index figures quoted are point estimates from summaries, not a built series.
  • The exact numeric thresholds in the 2013 leveraged lending guidance. Its existence, date, issuing agencies and December 2025 withdrawal are confirmed; the specific leverage limits it set are not.
  • Any 2026 named borrower collapse beyond those in Part 9.1. Absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

14.3 Five things to do next, in order

  1. Read the FSB report of 6 May 2026 on vulnerabilities in private credit. Free, primary, and it is the single best document on the risk debate.
  2. Read the FCA valuation review of March 2025. Short, and it makes Part 5.5 concrete.
  3. Open one BDC quarterly filing, any of the five in Figure 13, and find the fee terms and the PIK disclosure yourself. Nothing builds fluency faster than reading one real document.
  4. Read the Alternative Credit Council's Financing the Economy for the industry's own account, and notice where it differs from the regulators'.
  5. Find and read your FT article properly, so you can name the institutions yourself rather than relying on this document's caveat.
A last thought on why this asset class suits the job you want

Private credit has no price, contested valuations, four different default rates, market-size estimates that vary twofold, and a defence that rests on an assumption about investor behaviour that was tested for the first time in 2026.

That is either a reason to avoid it or a reason it needs people who can explain things honestly. An asset class where the numbers are unambiguous does not need a translator. This one does. If you want an answer to "why private credit?" that is about the work rather than the returns, that is the strongest one available, and it happens to be true.
