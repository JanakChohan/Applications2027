Part 2

Where it came from,
and why

Private credit did not appear because someone invented a clever product. It appeared because regulation made banks bad owners of certain loans, and the loans had to go somewhere. That sentence is the honest version, and it has a benign reading and a critical one.

@@ANCHOR:p2a@@

2.1 The prehistory

Lending privately is not new. Three strands ran for decades before anyone used the phrase.

  • Insurance private placements. Life insurers have bought privately negotiated corporate debt since the middle of the twentieth century. They have long-dated liabilities and no need to trade, which makes them natural holders of loans that never trade. This is the oldest form of the idea, and Part 8.1 shows it returning in force.
  • Mezzanine funds, 1980s and 1990s. Funds lending the risky layer between bank debt and equity in buyouts. The first recognisably modern private credit strategy.
  • The BDC. The Small Business Investment Incentive Act of 1980, signed on 21 October 1980, created the business development company: a vehicle designed to channel capital to smaller American companies[P4] Confirmed. A BDC must distribute most of its income and faces statutory limits on borrowing. That structure, forty-five years old, is the wrapper inside which much of today's retail private credit sits.
One change that mattered more than it sounds

The Small Business Credit Availability Act, signed 23 March 2018, relaxed BDC leverage limits from 1:1 to 2:1, by lowering the required asset coverage from 200% to 150%[P5] Confirmed.

In plain English: a BDC could suddenly borrow twice as much against the same investor equity. That doubles the loans a given pool of capital can make, and it multiplies both returns and risk. Much of the growth in retail-facing private credit after 2018 rests on this one statutory change, and very few people mentioning "the growth of private credit" know it happened.

2.2 The real origin: banks walked away

After 2008, regulators made banks hold far more capital against risky loans. That made mid-sized corporate lending a poor use of a bank's balance sheet. The demand for loans did not disappear, so the supply moved.

Why the loans moved, in one paragraph

A bank funds itself largely with deposits, which can be withdrawn at any time, and it must hold regulatory capital against the risk of its loans. A credit fund funds itself with money committed for years and holds no regulatory capital in the banking sense. For a loan that will not be sold and may be volatile in value, the fund is the structurally better owner: it cannot be forced to sell by a run, and it is not penalised for holding risk. That is the benign argument, and it is genuinely strong Inferred.

2.3 How big it is, and why nobody agrees

You will see private credit described as a $1.5 trillion market and as a $3.5 trillion market in the same week. Both figures are honest. Understanding why is a genuine signal of competence.

How to talk about the size without being wrong

Never give one number flatly. Say: "estimates run from roughly $1.5 trillion on a narrow definition of corporate lending to about $3.5 trillion if you include asset-based, real estate and infrastructure credit, and part of the spread is dry powder that has not been lent yet." That is accurate, it shows you know the definitions are contested, and it is far more impressive than a confident single figure.

On direction of travel, Moody's has projected roughly $4 trillion by 2030[P11] Reported. Attribute forecasts to whoever made them.

2.4 The zero-rate era, and what its end revealed

For a decade after 2008, interest rates were near zero and investors could not find yield in bonds. Private credit offered more, and money poured in. Then rates rose sharply from 2022, and because these loans are mostly floating rate, two things happened at once:

  • Lenders' income rose automatically. Yields went up without the lender doing anything. Returns looked excellent.
  • Borrowers' interest bills rose by exactly the same amount. The companies had been leveraged on the assumption of cheap money.

The data shows the strain. The share of middle-market borrowers in one dataset unable to cover interest, tax and working capital from their own cash rose from roughly 25% to 30% in 2021 to a peak near 45% in 2023, easing somewhat through 2025[P12] Confirmed. Over the same period, headline borrowing rose from about four times earnings to five or six times, and closer to seven once earnings adjustments are stripped out[P12].

The thing to understand about floating rate

"Floating rate protects you from interest rate risk" is true for the lender and only for the lender. The risk does not vanish. It converts from interest rate risk into credit risk, because the borrower now has a bigger bill. A lender who says floating rate makes the loan safe has moved the risk rather than removed it, and noticing that is one of the more genuinely analytical observations available in this asset class.

2.5 So why does it exist? Both answers

The benign answer

Bank regulation is designed for institutions funded by deposits that can run. Long-term illiquid loans are a bad match for that funding and a good match for pension and insurance money that has no need to sell. Moving those loans from banks to funds puts risk where it can be held patiently, and gets mid-sized companies financed that banks abandoned. The system is safer, not less safe, because the holder cannot be run on.

The critical answer

Nothing about the loans changed. The same credit risk moved from an institution that is supervised, capitalised and stress-tested to one that is none of those things, and which is often financed by the very banks the risk supposedly left. Regulators cannot see it properly, valuations are self-reported, and the growth happened in a period of unusually low defaults. This is regulatory arbitrage with a better story attached.

Both are serious. The most defensible position is that the benign account is largely right about the structure and the critical account is largely right about the visibility: patient capital really is a better owner of illiquid loans, and regulators really cannot see what they would need to see to know whether it has been done prudently.
