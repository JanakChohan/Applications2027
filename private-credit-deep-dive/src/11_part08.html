8.1 Insurance: the quiet revolution

The most important structural story in private credit is not about lending at all. It is about who owns the money. Private credit managers have been buying and partnering with life and annuity insurers.

Why an annuity company is the perfect partner for a private lender

An annuity is a promise to pay someone an income for decades. So an annuity writer has long-dated, predictable liabilities and cannot be run on in the way a bank can. It needs assets that pay a reliable yield over many years and it does not care about daily prices.

That is an almost perfect match for private credit. And it transforms the manager's own business: instead of earning a fee on someone else's money, which can be withdrawn, the firm owns a permanent balance sheet and earns the spread between what it pays annuity holders and what its loans yield. Fee income is cyclical. Spread income on permanent capital is not.

If you want to understand why Apollo trades as a different kind of company from a traditional asset manager, this is the reason.

The concern, stated fairly. Insurance regulators have noticed. US state insurance regulators, through the NAIC, published an issue brief on private credit in April 2026[P171] Confirmed, and have been working on capital treatment for structured credit that insurers hold. The worry is straightforward: retirement incomes are now backed by assets whose values are estimated rather than observed, and sometimes by assets originated by an affiliate of the insurer's own owner. That last point, affiliated origination, is the one to watch.

8.2 Banks: from rivals to partners

The standard narrative is that private credit took business from banks. What actually happened next is more interesting: they joined up. Named arrangements include JPMorgan committing balance sheet to direct lending, Citi with Apollo, Wells Fargo with Centerbridge, Barclays with AGL, and Société Générale with Brookfield[P172] Reported.

Banks also lend to private credit funds, through subscription lines, NAV facilities and back leverage. The Financial Stability Board's report of 6 May 2026 put bank lending to private credit funds at around $220bn on member-reported data, against commercial estimates of $270bn to $500bn, and noted this is less than 0.5% of bank assets[P173] Confirmed.

Hold both halves of this

That "less than 0.5% of bank assets" figure is genuinely reassuring about scale, and you should cite it when someone claims private credit is about to break the banking system.

But the FSB's own point is that the exposure is concentrated in a handful of large banks and is hard to measure, which is why their own estimate and commercial estimates differ by more than twofold. A risk that is small in aggregate, concentrated in a few places, and imprecisely measured is exactly the shape of risk that surprises people. Both things are true.

8.3 Retailisation, and the inversion you spotted

Private credit was built for institutions. It was then packaged for wealthy individuals. And now, in the development you found, institutions are buying the version built for individuals. That is worth understanding properly, because it inverts a forty-year hierarchy.

Then the inversion

What is reported: institutional investors have begun allocating to the evergreen vehicles that firms such as Blackstone and KKR built for wealthy individuals. And KKR has raised the share of a deal its affiliated evergreen vehicles may take from a long-standing 7.5% cap to around 20% in certain cases, including in relation to an $8bn European fund. Its K-Series private equity evergreen funds have attracted nearly $12bn since launching in 2023, with the wider K-Series suite across strategies reported at roughly $32bn to $35bn[P202] Reported.

Source uncertain — one detail I could not stand behind

Search summaries of the original Financial Times report name the UK's Universities Superannuation Scheme as one of the institutions. This research could not corroborate that in any fetchable reliable source; the trade outlets that were read in full name no institution, and the only source naming USS is a low-quality blog[P203]. The FT itself was unreachable.

Treat the USS detail as unverified. The structural claim, that institutions are moving into evergreen vehicles, is well corroborated. The specific name is not. If you want to use it, read the FT piece yourself.

Why institutions would want the "retail" product

This is the part that makes the story analytically interesting rather than merely odd. Four drivers:

  • No capital calls. In a closed-end fund you commit money and wait to be asked for it, holding cash in the meantime. An evergreen fund is invested from day one.
  • No J-curve. Closed-end funds show negative early returns because fees are charged before gains arrive. Evergreen credit vehicles pay income immediately.
  • Distributions have slowed. Private equity has struggled to sell companies, so cash is not coming back. Investors need liquidity from somewhere.
  • The arithmetic of the drag. Partners Group has published a calculation that a closed-end fund needs roughly a 20% internal rate of return to match an evergreen fund delivering 11%, once the cost of holding uninvested cash is counted[P204] Reported.
That 20% versus 11% figure is the best single number in this section

It comes from a manager with an interest in the answer, so attribute it rather than state it. But it captures the whole argument in one line: a headline IRR on a closed-end fund is not what the investor actually earns, because it ignores the money sitting idle waiting to be called. If you can explain why those two numbers are comparable, you understand something most graduates do not: the difference between a fund's return and an investor's return.

And the frontier: pensions and ETFs

  • US retirement accounts. Executive Order 14330, "Democratizing Access to Alternative Assets for 401(k) Investors", was signed on 7 August 2025, directing the Department of Labor to re-examine its guidance[P205] Confirmed.
  • The UK. The Mansion House Accord commits workplace pension providers to private markets allocations, which is how private credit reaches UK savers who never chose it. Part of the reason a firm like Fidelity built an LTAF.
  • The ETF experiment. A private-credit-containing ETF launched on 26 February 2025, targeting 10% to 35% in private credit, and held around $812m by 17 September 2026[P206] Confirmed. Putting untraded assets in a daily-traded wrapper is the most aggressive version of this trend and the most contested.

8.4 Where the growth is going next

Two directions, both already visible in Part 3.

Asset-based finance. Named by 66% of practitioners as the top growth area[P52]. Lending against receivables, equipment, royalties and consumer loans rather than against a company's earnings. The logic is diversification: these cash flows do not all depend on corporate profits.

AI infrastructure. Private credit has become a major financier of datacentres. Morgan Stanley has projected it supplying around $800bn of a $1.5 trillion financing gap through 2028[P107] Reported.

The observation that ties Part 8 together

Notice what these trends have in common. Insurance capital, evergreen vehicles, workplace pensions and ETFs are all ways of finding longer-dated or stickier money, or of reaching a new kind of investor. The growth constraint in private credit has not been finding loans. It has been finding capital patient enough to hold them.

Which raises the question the next part is about: what happens when the capital turns out to be less patient than the assets require?
