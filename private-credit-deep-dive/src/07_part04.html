4.1 The capital structure, and what "senior secured" buys you

Every company funded with a mix of debt and equity has an order of repayment. If things go wrong, money is distributed strictly down the ladder. Nobody on a lower rung gets anything until the rung above is paid in full.

4.2 Unitranche, the signature instrument

Instead of a company arranging a senior loan from one lender and a junior loan from another, with two sets of documents and an inter-creditor fight waiting to happen, a single lender provides one loan at one blended rate.

Behind the scenes, the lenders privately divide it through an agreement among lenders, or AAL. One group takes the safer "first-out" piece at a lower rate; another takes the "last-out" piece at a higher rate and absorbs losses first. The borrower never sees this, and deals with one counterparty.

The spread cycle, which tells you how competitive the market is

Spreads on direct lending moved from roughly S+600 to 650 in 2022-23, down to about S+475 by early 2026, then back out to around S+502 by August 2026[P301] Reported.

Read that as a story. Spreads fell because too much money chased too few deals: competition, not improving credit. Then they widened again in 2026, and the reported cause was not deteriorating borrowers but redemption pressure on the evergreen vehicles described in Part 8.3. That is an unusual and interesting mechanism: the price of corporate loans moved because of the behaviour of retail investors in the funds that hold them.

4.4 Covenants, and the argument about them

A maintenance covenant requires a borrower to keep meeting a financial test, typically quarterly. If it fails, the lender gets a seat at the table early, while there is still value to protect. An incurrence covenant only bites when the company actively does something, such as raising more debt.

Private credit's standard claim is that it kept maintenance covenants while the syndicated market abandoned them. The evidence says: true at the smaller end, eroding at the larger end. Covenant retention is reported at around 98% in the lower middle market, while covenant-lite packages reach as much as 93% of larger private credit loans[P66] Reported.

It matters financially. Lenders with maintenance covenants have been found to recover roughly 10 percentage points more than lenders who can only act once a payment is actually missed[P67] Reported.

The add-back problem, which is where the real leverage hides

Loan size is set as a multiple of EBITDA, a measure of earnings. But EBITDA is negotiated. Borrowers argue for "add-backs": costs they say are one-off or will disappear.

One study of 2022-vintage loans found add-backs equal to more than 29% of projected EBITDA and around 55% of the earnings actually reported over the last twelve months. The same body of work found 95% of deals from 2015 to 2020 missed their first-year earnings projections[P68] Reported.

Read that carefully. If earnings are overstated by half, then a loan described as five times earnings may really be seven times. This is the single most important thing to understand about why headline leverage figures understate risk, and it is why lenders now try to cap add-backs at around 25% of earnings[P68].

PIK: the early warning

Payment in kind means the borrower pays interest by adding to the loan balance rather than in cash. It is legitimate flexibility for a growing company and a red flag when it spreads across a portfolio, because it means cash is not being generated.

The share of BDC loans with a PIK feature rose from about 5.4% in early 2022 to 9.8% by early 2026[P69] Reported. Research cited in the same material links amendment-driven PIK to a one to two percentage point increase in the probability of delinquency in the following quarter, against a base rate of roughly 3%[P69].

4.5 The life of a loan

Sourcing

A private equity sponsor buying a company asks several lenders to bid. Around 70% or more of direct lending is sponsor-backed[P70], because the sponsor brings deal flow, equity underneath the loan, and a repeat relationship.

Screening and diligence

Typically three to six weeks[P71]. Financial, commercial, legal. The lender gets access to information no public bond investor ever sees, which is one of the genuine advantages of the format.

Credit committee

The internal approval body. The decision-making heart of the firm, and where a credit culture is actually visible.

Term sheet and documentation

Pricing, leverage, covenants, add-back definitions, delayed draw commitments. Every argument in 4.4 is settled here, in writing.

Funding and monitoring

The loan is drawn. Then quarterly reporting, covenant testing, and regular contact with management. Maturities are typically five to seven years, though loans are often refinanced after three or four[P72].

Amendment, or trouble

Most stress is resolved by negotiation: a waiver, a maturity extension, a switch to PIK, an equity injection from the sponsor. Refinancings were about 28% of all direct lending activity in 2025[P73].

Exit, or restructuring

Repayment or refinancing in the good case. In the bad case, a restructuring in which lenders may take ownership of the business. Part 9 has a real example where exactly that happened.

The detail that shows you understand the job

Most of the work in private credit is not choosing loans. It is the monitoring and the amendments: the quarterly call, the covenant test, the waiver negotiation. A public bond investor who dislikes a company sells it. A private lender cannot sell, so the only available action is to engage. That is why these firms employ restructuring specialists, and why "workout capability" is a genuine differentiator rather than marketing.
