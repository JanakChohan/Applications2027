What private credit
actually is

Start here. Three ideas, and if you have all three you can follow everything that comes afterwards: what a loan is as an investment, what makes one private, and why that changes everything.

1.1 The definition, and what it is not

Private credit is lending money directly to a company, through a fund rather than a bank, where the loan is negotiated privately between the two sides and never traded on a market.

That is the whole thing. Every complication later is a variation on it.

Pull the sentence apart:

  • Lending, not owning. You are a creditor, not a shareholder. You get interest and your money back. You do not get a share of the upside if the business triples in value. See 1.2, because this is the distinction people get wrong.
  • Directly. One lender, or a small group, negotiating with one borrower. Not hundreds of anonymous buyers of a bond.
  • Through a fund. The money comes from pension schemes, insurers, endowments, sovereign wealth funds and, increasingly, wealthy individuals and ordinary savers. A manager pools it and lends it out.
  • Never traded. There is no screen anywhere showing what the loan is worth. This is the fact that generates both the returns and the anxieties.
Key term · the word "private"

"Private" here does not mean secret or shady. It means not publicly traded. A public bond can be bought and sold by anyone through a broker and has a price that moves every day. A private loan is a bilateral contract that sits on the lender's books until it is repaid. Same underlying idea, completely different plumbing.

What it is not, which is how you place it

ThingWhat it isHow private credit differs
A bank loan A bank lends deposits to a company and keeps the loan. Same economics, different lender. The bank funds itself with deposits that can be withdrawn tomorrow. A credit fund is funded with money committed for years.
A broadly syndicated loan
BSL		 A bank arranges a large loan then sells slices to many institutional buyers. Those slices then trade. A syndicated loan has a market price and many owners. A private loan has neither. This is the closest comparison and the main competitor.
A high yield bond A publicly issued bond from a company with a sub-investment-grade rating. Traded, rated, disclosed. Private credit needs no public rating, no public disclosure, and usually pays a floating rate rather than a fixed coupon.
A CLO
collateralised loan obligation		 A vehicle that buys a portfolio of loans and issues tranches of securities against them. A CLO is a way of financing loans, not a type of loan. CLOs mostly hold syndicated loans, though private credit CLOs now exist.
Private equity Buying and owning whole companies. Often the same borrowers and sometimes the same firms, but the opposite side of the balance sheet. See 1.2.

1.2 Debt versus equity, in one page

If you take one thing from this document, take this. Private credit and private equity are constantly mentioned together, and they are close to opposites in what they promise.

Imagine a company worth £100m, funded with £60m of borrowed money and £40m of shareholders' money. Now ask what each side gets in three different futures.

The consequence, stated properly

Lending has a capped upside and a protected downside. Owning has an unlimited upside and is first in line for losses. So the two disciplines are completely different jobs:

  • A private equity investor asks "how good could this get?" They need a few investments to be spectacular, because most will be ordinary.
  • A private credit investor asks "what could go wrong, and do I still get paid?" They need almost every loan to perform, because no loan can ever return more than it promised. One total loss can wipe out the margin on twenty good loans.

That asymmetry explains almost everything about how credit people behave: the obsession with downside cases, with collateral, with covenants, and with what the business is worth if it has to be sold in a hurry.

The sentence to remember

Equity investors are paid to be right. Credit investors are paid not to be wrong. If you can explain why that sentence is true using Figure 2, you understand the asset class better than most people who mention it.

1.3 The four things that make a loan "private"

Four attributes travel together. Each one has a benefit and a cost, and the pairs are the reason every argument about this asset class exists.

AttributeWhat it meansWhat it buysWhat it costs
Bilaterally negotiatedThe lender and borrower agree terms face to face, rather than terms being set by a market. Tailored structures, real covenants, and a lender who knows the business. Slower, and it only works at a size one lender can handle.
Not tradedNo exchange, no screen, no daily price. No forced selling in a panic. The lender can hold through volatility. Nobody knows what it is really worth. Value is an estimate. See Part 5.5.
Held to maturityThe fund intends to hold the loan until repayment. Income is predictable and contractual. The investor's money is locked up, which is the origin of every liquidity problem in Part 9.
Usually floating rateInterest resets with a reference rate such as SOFR or SONIA, rather than being fixed. When rates rise, income rises. Little interest-rate risk for the lender. When rates rise, the borrower's costs rise too, exactly when conditions may be hardest.
The one to sit with

Row two is the important one, and the one where the honest and the marketing answers diverge.

Being untraded genuinely protects a lender from being forced to sell at a bad price. It also means the reported value of the portfolio is produced by the same firm that is paid on the basis of it. Both things are true at once. Someone who only says the first is selling. Someone who only says the second is not paying attention. Part 5.5 works through the mechanics properly.
