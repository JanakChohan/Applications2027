Part 12

Glossary

78 terms. If you can define forty of these, you can hold a conversation with a private credit investor. If you can define all of them, you can hold one with their client.

Add-back
An adjustment that increases reported EBITDA for costs a company argues are one-off or will disappear. Heavily negotiated, because the more add-backs are allowed, the more the company can borrow against the same real earnings.
Agreement among lenders (AAL)
The private contract that splits a unitranche loan into a safer first-out piece and a riskier last-out piece. The borrower sees one loan; the lenders privately divide the risk.
Amend and extend
Renegotiating a loan to push out its maturity rather than letting it default. Sensible workout tool, and also a way a troubled loan can avoid ever appearing in default statistics.
Amortisation
Repaying principal in instalments over the life of a loan, rather than all at maturity. Most direct lending is bullet repayment with little amortisation.
Asset-based finance (ABF)
Lending secured against specific assets or cash flows rather than a whole company: receivables, equipment, royalties, aircraft, consumer loans. The fastest-growing corner of private credit.
AUM
Assets under management. For private credit, read carefully: some figures include undrawn commitments (dry powder) and some do not, which is why market-size estimates differ so much.
BDC
Business development company. A US listed or non-traded vehicle created by legislation in 1980 to channel capital to smaller companies. Must distribute most of its income and faces limits on leverage.
Bilateral
A loan between one lender and one borrower, with no syndication.
Broadly syndicated loan (BSL)
A large loan arranged by a bank and sold in slices to many institutional investors. It trades, so it has a market price. Private credit's main competitor.
Bullet
A loan repaid in a single lump at maturity.
Capital call
A demand from a fund to its investors to send in a portion of the money they committed. The mechanism by which closed-end funds draw capital as deals arise.
Carried interest
The manager's share of profits above a hurdle, traditionally 20% in private equity and often lower in credit.
CLO
Collateralised loan obligation. A vehicle that buys a pool of loans and issues tranches of securities against them. A financing structure, not an asset class.
Club deal
A loan provided by a small group of lenders who know each other, rather than one lender or a broad syndicate.
Cov-lite
A loan without maintenance covenants. Common in the syndicated market. Private credit has historically retained maintenance covenants, though less universally than its marketing suggests.
Covenant
A promise in a loan agreement. A maintenance covenant must be met on a continuing basis and is tested regularly. An incurrence covenant only bites when the company does something specific, such as taking on more debt.
Credit committee
The internal body that approves or rejects a loan. The decision-making heart of a lending firm.
Default
Failure to meet an obligation under the loan agreement. Note that a covenant breach is a default even if every interest payment has been made, which is why covenants matter so much.
Delayed draw term loan (DDTL)
A commitment to lend more later, typically to fund acquisitions. Valuable to a borrower planning to buy other businesses.
Direct lending
The core of private credit: funds lending directly to companies, usually senior secured and floating rate.
Distressed debt
Buying the debt of companies already in serious trouble, usually at a deep discount, often with the aim of converting it into ownership.
DPI
Distributions to paid-in capital. How much cash a fund has actually returned, divided by how much investors have put in. The number that cannot be flattered by a valuation.
Drawdown fund
The traditional closed-end structure: commit for around ten years, capital is called as needed, money is returned as investments are realised, and there are no redemptions.
Dry powder
Capital that investors have committed but the fund has not yet invested.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. The rough proxy for cash generation against which loan size is measured. Its definition is negotiated, which is why add-backs matter.
ELTIF
European Long-Term Investment Fund. The EU structure for holding illiquid assets, reformed as ELTIF 2.0 to widen retail access.
Evergreen fund
A fund with no end date that accepts money continuously and allows limited periodic withdrawals, usually quarterly and typically capped at 5% of value.
First lien
The debt with the first claim on collateral in a default. The safest position in the capital structure.
First-out / last-out
The two halves of a unitranche. The first-out piece is repaid first and earns less; the last-out earns more and absorbs loss first.
Floating rate
Interest that resets with a reference rate. Most private credit is floating, so income rises when rates rise.
Fund finance
Lending to funds themselves rather than to operating companies. Includes subscription lines and NAV loans.
Gate
A limit on how much can be withdrawn from a fund in a given period.
Hurdle rate
The return a fund must exceed before the manager earns performance fees. Typically expressed as an annual percentage.
Illiquidity premium
The extra return an investor expects for accepting that they cannot sell. Whether it is genuinely earned or partly an artefact of not marking to market is a live debate.
Incurrence covenant
A restriction that applies only when a company takes a specific action, such as raising more debt.
Interest coverage ratio
Earnings divided by interest expense. How many times over a company can pay its interest bill. The single most watched stress indicator in private credit.
Interval fund
A US registered fund that must offer to repurchase between 5% and 25% of its shares each period under SEC Rule 23c-3.
IRR
Internal rate of return. An annualised return that accounts for the timing of cash flows. Can be flattered by using borrowed money early to delay calling investor capital.
J-curve
The tendency for a fund's early returns to look negative because fees are charged before investments mature. Less pronounced in credit than in private equity, because credit pays income from the start.
Junior debt
Debt ranking behind senior debt for repayment. Higher return, first to lose after equity.
Leverage
Borrowed money. It appears at three levels in private credit: inside the borrowing company, inside the fund, and sometimes at the investor. Each layer multiplies both return and risk.
Liquidity sleeve
Cash and readily saleable assets a semi-liquid fund holds specifically to meet withdrawals.
LTAF
Long-Term Asset Fund. The UK structure for holding illiquid assets, created by the FCA with a minimum 90-day redemption notice period and dealing no more often than monthly.
Mark to model
Valuing an asset using an internal estimate because no market price exists. Standard practice in private credit and the source of most valuation controversy.
Maturity
When the loan must be repaid.
Mezzanine
Debt sitting between senior debt and equity, often with an equity component. Higher return, higher risk.
Middle market
The band of companies private credit traditionally serves. Definitions vary; commonly companies with EBITDA between roughly $10m and $100m, with an upper middle market above that.
MOIC
Multiple on invested capital. How many times the original investment has been returned, ignoring timing. Reported alongside IRR because each hides something the other reveals.
NAV
Net asset value. What a fund says its holdings are worth, after debt. In private credit this is an estimate, not an observed price.
NAV loan
A loan secured against a fund's portfolio of investments rather than against a single company. Controversial because it can add leverage at the fund level to return cash to investors.
Non-sponsored
A borrower not owned by a private equity firm. Usually harder to source, often better priced, and requires more diligence.
OID
Original issue discount. Lending £98 for a loan that repays £100, which raises the effective yield. A standard way of adding return at the front.
Originate
To source, structure and make a loan yourself, rather than buying one someone else made. Origination capability is the main thing that separates private credit managers.
PIK
Payment in kind. Interest paid by increasing the loan balance rather than in cash. Useful flexibility for a growing borrower, and a warning sign when it rises across a portfolio.
PIK toggle
A provision letting a borrower choose to pay interest in kind rather than cash, usually at a higher rate.
Private placement
A security sold directly to a small number of investors rather than publicly offered. Insurance companies have done this for decades, and it is a forerunner of modern private credit.
Pro rata
Proportionate. When withdrawal requests exceed a fund's cap, each investor typically receives the same proportion of what they asked for.
Recovery rate
How much a lender gets back on a defaulted loan, as a percentage of what was owed. Just as important as the default rate, and less often quoted.
Reference rate
The floating benchmark a loan is priced over: SOFR in the US, SONIA in sterling, EURIBOR in euros.
RVPI
Residual value to paid-in capital. The value of what a fund still holds, relative to what investors contributed. The unrealised half of TVPI.
Second lien
Debt ranking behind first lien on the same collateral.
Senior secured
Debt with the first claim on repayment and specific assets pledged against it. The bulk of direct lending.
SOFR
Secured Overnight Financing Rate. The main US floating reference rate, which replaced US dollar LIBOR.
Special situations
Investing in complex or stressed circumstances that do not fit standard categories. Sits between direct lending and distressed.
Sponsor
A private equity firm that owns the borrowing company. Sponsor-backed lending is the majority of direct lending, because the sponsor supplies equity, governance and a repeat relationship.
Spread
The margin over the reference rate. Quoted as, for example, SOFR plus 500 basis points.
SRT
Significant risk transfer. A bank buys protection on a portfolio of its loans, transferring credit risk to an investor while keeping the loans. A fast-growing overlap between banks and private credit.
Subordinated
Ranking behind other debt for repayment.
Subscription line
A short-term loan to a fund secured against investors' unfunded commitments. Smooths cash flow, and flatters IRR by delaying capital calls.
Syndication
Selling parts of a loan to other lenders.
Tranche
A slice of a financing with its own terms and ranking.
TVPI
Total value to paid-in capital. Distributions plus remaining value, over capital contributed. The headline multiple.
Unitranche
A single blended loan that replaces separate senior and junior tranches. The signature private credit instrument: one lender, one document, one price, with the risk split privately behind the scenes.
Venture debt
Lending to young, loss-making companies, usually alongside venture capital equity and often with warrants attached.
Vintage year
The year a fund started investing. Vintages are compared with each other because market conditions dominate outcomes.
Waterfall
The order in which money from a company or a fund is distributed. Senior debt first, then junior, then equity.
Workout
The process of dealing with a loan that has gone wrong: renegotiating, restructuring, or enforcing security.
Yield to maturity
The total annualised return on a loan if held to repayment, including spread, fees and any discount.
