Part 3

The strategy map

"Private credit" covers a dozen different businesses with different risks and different returns. Knowing the map is the fastest way to stop sounding like someone who read one article.

3.1 The core: corporate direct lending

This is what most people mean by private credit, and it is where most of the money is. A fund lends to an operating company, usually senior secured, usually floating rate, usually held to maturity.

One structural fact worth carrying: a law firm's review of the loans it documented found that 86% were first lien or unitranche[P51] Confirmed. Private credit is overwhelmingly a senior secured business, not an exotic one. That matters when you meet the caricature of private credit as reckless.

Why NAV lending attracts criticism

A NAV loan is secured against a private equity fund's whole portfolio. Funds have used them to return cash to investors when they cannot sell companies. The objection: investors receive a distribution that looks like a realisation but is actually borrowed money, secured on assets they still own, adding leverage on top of leverage already inside each company.

Defenders say it is ordinary portfolio financing and often cheaper than selling a good asset into a bad market. The market is estimated at around $150bn with vendor projections of $600bn to $700bn by 2030[P55] Reported. If you want one topic that marks you out as reading past the headlines, this is a strong candidate.

3.3 Where the growth actually is

3.4 Significant risk transfer, briefly

Worth understanding because it is the clearest example of banks and private credit becoming partners rather than rivals. A bank keeps a portfolio of loans on its balance sheet but buys protection against the first slice of losses from an investor. The bank keeps the customer relationship and frees up regulatory capital; the investor earns a high return for taking concentrated first-loss risk.

The question this raises, which you should hold a view on

If a bank keeps a loan but sells the first losses to a credit fund, has risk genuinely left the banking system, or has it simply been moved somewhere with less visibility and then partly financed by banks again? Regulators ask this in every report on the subject. There is a reasonable answer on both sides, and Part 9 sets them out.
