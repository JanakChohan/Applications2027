Chapter 1

The short version

Seven ideas. If you understand these, you understand the business better than almost any other candidate.

  1. AlphaSights is a broker, not a publisher. It earns money when a client and an expert actually speak. Its accounts say revenue is counted “on completion of a transaction”. Audited
  2. So everything is built to make calls happen. The teams, the pay, the technology and the training all push in one direction: more good calls, faster.
  3. It hunts for experts. Older rivals fish in a pond. AlphaSights goes out and finds the ideal person for each project. The original model was to search a fixed database of people who had signed up.
  4. One team does the whole job. The same Associates find the experts, look after the client and grow the account. Rivals split this between separate teams.
  5. Costs move with sales. Experts are paid only when they are used. A large part of staff pay depends on results. The accounts describe a “considerable degree of flexibility” in the cost base. Audited
  6. The result is unusual profit. About 38 cents of every dollar is kept before interest, tax and write-downs. GLG, the firm that invented the industry, kept about 22 cents the last time it published figures. Audited
  7. The model is now changing. AlphaSights is adding a library and AI tools on top of calls. That moves it from pure broker towards “platform”.
The one-sentence version

“AlphaSights is a broker for expert knowledge that hunts for the right person each time, runs each project with one accountable team, and gets paid when the conversation happens.”

Chapter 2

What is really being sold

In one line

On paper the product is a one-hour call. In reality the client is buying four things at once: the search, the check, the legal protection and the speed.

The expert’s knowledge is not the product

This is the first idea to get straight. AlphaSights does not own the knowledge. The expert does. Anyone could, in theory, find that expert and call them.

So what does the client pay about $1,000 an hour for? It pays for everything that happens before the call.

What is boughtWhat it meansWhy the client cannot do it
The searchWorking out who would know the answer, then finding and persuading them.It takes days of work and hundreds of messages.
The checkSpeaking to the person first to confirm they really know the subject.A job title does not prove knowledge.
The protectionCompliance checks so that no secret information is shared.One mistake can mean a criminal case for an investor.
The speedAll of the above, done in hours.The deal deadline will not wait.

A useful comparison: a headhunter for one-hour jobs

A headhunter finds a person for a permanent job and takes a fee. AlphaSights finds a person for a one-hour job and takes a fee. The skills are similar: research, cold approaches, persuasion and judgement about people.

The difference is volume and speed. A headhunter fills a few roles a month. AlphaSights sets up something like 2,000 conversations every working day. My reading

How I got that: revenue of $652.6 million divided by a typical industry price of about $1,200 a call gives roughly half a million calls a year. AlphaSights does not publish its prices or call numbers, so treat this as a rough size, not a fact.

A marketplace, but run by hand

Business people call this a two-sided marketplace. One side has a need (clients). The other side has a supply (experts). The firm in the middle matches them.

Uber and Airbnb are two-sided marketplaces too. But they are self-service: an app does the matching. AlphaSights is a managed marketplace. Trained people do the matching by hand, helped by software.

Every client question is different. The stakes are high. That is why the hand-run model wins here, and why clients accept the price.

The revenue formula

In one line

Revenue is four numbers multiplied together. AlphaSights can grow by pushing up any one of them.

Which lever matters most?

The accounts answer this. They list three reasons revenue grew 12% in 2025, in this order: Audited

  1. “growth of existing clients”
  2. “an expanded platform offering”
  3. “the continued acquisition of new clients”

Existing clients come first. So most growth comes from the Projects and Calls levers: doing more with firms that already buy. That is cheaper than finding new clients, because the trust already exists.

The hidden race inside the Calls lever

Here is something most candidates never learn. A client often sends the same request to two or three expert networks at once. The networks then race.

The client takes calls with the best experts it is shown first. A network that replies late, or with weaker experts, earns nothing for its work.

What this means
  • Speed is not a nice extra. It decides who gets paid.
  • Quality matters just as much. A fast but poor match loses to a slightly slower, perfect one.
  • The work on a lost project is wasted cost. So the firm that wins more races earns far more profit from the same effort.

This is why AlphaSights is obsessed with speed and with how well each expert fits. My reading

Chapter 4

How and when clients pay

In one line

Some clients pay after each call. Others pay in advance for a bundle. Either way, AlphaSights only counts the money as revenue when the call is done.

What the accounts say

The accounts include a short section on exactly when revenue is counted. In plain English it says three things. Audited

The ruleWhat it means
Revenue is counted “on completion of a transaction”.The call has to happen. Work done on a project that leads to no call earns nothing.
Work that is done but not yet billed is still counted.If a call happens on 30 December and the bill goes out in January, the revenue belongs to December.
Money paid in advance waits. It becomes revenue when the service is delivered, “or on expiry of the term”.Some clients pay up front. If they do not use all they paid for before the contract ends, AlphaSights keeps the money as revenue anyway.

The two ways a client can pay

Pay as you go

The client takes a call. AlphaSights sends a bill. The client pays, on average, about 54 days later. My reading

Pay in advance

The client commits to a yearly amount, often as “credits”. Each call uses up credits. Rare experts and extras can use more. Industry estimate

The 54 days is my working: $96.2 million was owed by clients at the end of 2025, against $652.6 million of yearly revenue.

Why pre-payment is good for AlphaSights

  • Cash arrives early. The firm holds the client’s money before doing the work.
  • The client is tied in. A client that has already paid will use AlphaSights before a rival.
  • Unused credits still count. When a contract ends, anything unspent becomes revenue with no cost attached.

Clients almost always pay

At the end of 2025, clients owed AlphaSights $96.2 million. The firm set aside only $250,000 in case some never pay. That is about a quarter of one per cent. Audited

The clients are large, wealthy institutions. This is a quiet strength of the model: almost no money is lost to bad debts.

Why this matters for understanding the firm

GLG, the oldest rival, told investors that 90% of its revenue came from subscription contracts. Audited A subscription firm is paid for giving access. AlphaSights’ revenue is tied to completed interactions. That one difference explains a lot about its culture: the whole firm is tuned to make conversations happen. My reading

Why “costs that move with sales” matters so much

Think about two shops. One pays a huge fixed rent whether customers come or not. The other only pays for stock when a customer buys it. In a bad month, the first shop loses money. The second shop is fine.

AlphaSights is like the second shop. If clients book fewer calls, it pays fewer expert fees and less performance pay. The directors say this directly: the firm has a “considerable degree of flexibility in its cost base” and could “withstand a substantive deterioration in business performance”. Audited

It needs almost no equipment

  • All of its long-term assets, such as software and office fittings, add up to $71 million. That is small next to $653 million of yearly sales. Audited
  • It spent $18.0 million building software in 2025, up from $14.6 million. That is under 3% of revenue. Audited
  • Interest paid on borrowing was only $186,000. The firm has almost no debt. Audited

A business like this is called asset-light. It grows by hiring and training people, not by building things.

Chapter 6

The people model

In one line

AlphaSights’ real “factory” is its system for hiring graduates, training them fast and paying them for results. This is the part of the business model you would be joining.

Why people are the product

A car maker’s advantage sits in its factory. AlphaSights has no factory. Its advantage sits in how well thousands of young professionals can find and persuade experts, again and again, under time pressure.

So the way the firm hires, trains, pays and promotes is not a side issue. It is the business model.

The four parts of the people model

The honest trade-off

This model asks a lot of people. It is fast, target-driven and full of rejection. Not everyone stays. That has two effects on the business.

  • The firm must keep hiring and training. This is why it invests so much in graduate recruitment, including Insight Days like yours.
  • It trains its own competitors. Former staff founded Dialectica, Tegus, Arbolus and others. Industry estimate The method is valuable enough that people leave to copy it.
Why this is worth saying in the interview

It shows you understand why the Insight Days exist at all. “I can see that the Associate team is really the engine of the business model, so finding the right people early must matter a great deal.” That is a mature observation for a student to make.

The flywheel

In one line

Each project leaves something behind that makes the next project easier. Over 18 years and 3 million interactions, that adds up to a serious head start.

A flywheel is a heavy wheel that is hard to start but, once spinning, keeps itself going. In business, it means a loop where success feeds more success.

But because it recruits fresh experts for every project, it has been building its database as a by-product of paid work for 18 years. It now has both: a large database and the habit of hunting. Chapter 8

Six choices that make it different

In one line

AlphaSights is different because of six deliberate choices. Each one has a reason, a benefit and a price.

A firm’s strategy is the set of choices it makes that its rivals did not. For each choice below you get four things: what it is, why they chose it, what it gives them, and what it costs them.

Choice 1: Hunt for experts instead of fishing in a pond

Why they chose itIn 2008 AlphaSights had no database. It had to hunt. It then made a virtue of it. A former employee wrote that the firm “focused on their custom recruiting in all marketing” to stand out from networks that “had a database”. Industry estimate The timing helped too: LinkedIn was growing fast, so for the first time almost any professional in the world could be found online.
What it gives themA closer match to the question. Fresher knowledge. Less reliance on “professional experts” who take many calls and add little. AlphaSights’ own line is that experts are “found for your specific brief, not pulled from a self-signup marketplace”. Company says
What it costs themMuch more work for each project. It needs a large, well-trained team. It only pays off if the hunt is very fast.

Choice 2: One team owns the whole project

Why they chose itThe founders had been consultants: the customer. They knew that what a client says in the first conversation is often lost by the time a researcher starts work. My reading
What it gives themSpeed, because nothing waits for a hand-over. Better matches. Clear responsibility: one team wins or loses. Fewer staff for the same revenue.
What it costs themA much harder job for each Associate, who must research, sell and manage clients all at once. Heavy dependence on hiring and training the right people.

Choice 3: Get paid when the conversation happens

What it isRevenue is tied to completed interactions, even where clients pay in advance. Audited GLG chose mainly yearly subscriptions. Tegus chose to sell a library and price its calls close to cost.
Why they chose itIt is the simplest promise to a client: you pay for what you use. It was also easy to start with. A new firm in 2008 could not ask clients for a large yearly fee before it had proved itself. My reading
What it gives themThe firm and the client want the same thing: useful calls. Staff pay can be tied to the same number. Revenue grows naturally as clients get busier.
What it costs themRevenue is less predictable than a subscription. When deal-making slows, calls slow and so does income. This is the main reason growth swings between 10% and 41% from year to year.

Choice 4: Service first, with technology behind it

What it isClients deal with people. Software makes those people faster. AlphaSense and Tegus made the opposite choice: clients deal with software, and people support it.
Why they chose itThe clients are senior, busy and working on unique questions. They want someone to take the problem away, not another tool to learn. My reading
What it gives themA premium price. Close client relationships. Answers to very unusual questions that no library contains.
What it costs themIt needs many staff, so it is harder to grow than software. It is now being tested by AI. This is why the firm is adding its own platform (see chapter 11).

Choice 5: Grow from within, led by the founders

What it isThe two founders are still Co-CEOs and the only two directors. Audited I found no record of AlphaSights buying another company. It opened each of its nine offices itself. AlphaSense, by contrast, has raised over $1 billion from investors and bought Tegus for a reported $930 million. Industry estimate
Why they chose itThe business makes more cash than it needs, so it never had to buy growth or borrow for it. Both founders had already built and sold companies, so they knew how to do it themselves.
What it gives themOne culture and one method in every office. No debt to repay. Freedom to think long term. The accounts talk of the directors’ “focus on the group’s long-term health”. Audited
What it costs themSlower entry into new areas. It built its library years after rivals had theirs.

Reminder from Part 1: some websites report an outside investment in 2024. I could not confirm it, so do not rely on it.

Choice 6: Treat compliance as a selling point

What it isA large in-house legal and compliance team, reported at over 60 people, with checks on every expert and call. Industry estimate
Why they chose itAround 2010 and 2011, insider trading cases hit US expert networks and frightened investors. European networks were less affected and grew. Industry estimate A young London firm with strict rules was well placed to win nervous clients.
What it gives themBefore an investment fund can use any network, its own compliance team must approve it. Passing that test is slow and hard. Once approved, a network is difficult to replace. My reading
What it costs themReal money, and sometimes a lost call when an expert fails a check.
The thread that ties all six together

Every choice serves the same customer need: the right person, fast, with no risk. Hunting gives the right person. One team gives speed. Compliance removes risk. Paying per interaction keeps everyone focused on it. That consistency is what makes it a strategy and not just a list of features.

The Tegus idea, and why it worries the brokers

Tegus was founded by former AlphaSights staff. It turned the model upside down. It charged about $300 to $400 for a call, close to what it cost. Industry estimate In return, the client let Tegus keep the transcript.

Tegus then sold access to all its transcripts as a subscription. One call could be sold again and again to many readers. A broker sells each call once.

Why that is a threat

If a client can read ten past calls on a topic for a fixed fee, it may book fewer new calls. Analysts say libraries have already squeezed call profits among investor clients. Industry estimate

Why it is not the end of calls

A library only holds answers to questions someone has already asked. A deal team with a new question about a small private company still needs a live expert.

AlphaSights’ answer is to do both: keep the best call service, and build its own library (AlphaNow) so clients do not have to go elsewhere for that.

Why is AlphaSights so much more profitable?

Nobody outside the two firms can prove the exact causes. These are the most likely ones. My reading

  1. Fewer people for the same work. One team doing three jobs needs fewer staff than three teams.
  2. Pay that follows results. A large share of pay is only earned when revenue is earned.
  3. Winning more of the races. Faster, better matches mean less wasted work on lost projects.
  4. No debt and no purchases. Almost no interest to pay, and no cost of buying other companies.
  5. Technology doing more. The accounts link the rising margin to the platform and “sustained technology investments”. Audited

The long view

GLG once had about half of this market. Analysts now put it at about a quarter. Industry estimate In those same years AlphaSights grew from £148 million of revenue in 2019 to $653 million in 2025. Audited

That is the strongest proof of all. The newer model did not just survive next to the old one. It took its place at the top.

Where the model is heading: from broker to platform

AlphaSights is adding a second engine on top of the first.

Engine 1: the broker (today’s core)
  • Paid per interaction
  • People do the work
  • Each call is sold once
Engine 2: the platform (being built)
  • Library, AI summaries, AlphaGPT, AI Interviewer
  • Software does more of the work
  • Content can be sold many times

The evidence that this is real

  • The accounts name the “expanded platform offering” as a cause of growth and of the higher margin. Audited
  • Spending on building software rose from $14.6 million to $18.0 million in a year. Audited
  • The firm’s stated plan is to “further expand its platform offering”. Audited

Why they are doing it

  • To defend. If clients want a library, better they use AlphaSights’ than a rival’s.
  • To earn more from the same work. A transcript that already exists costs almost nothing to sell again.
  • To steady the income. Subscriptions keep paying when deal-making is slow.
  • To free up Associates. If AI handles summaries and simple questions, people can focus on the hard searches.
What this means for the Associate role

The job is likely to shift away from routine tasks and towards judgement: understanding what the client really needs, persuading rare experts, and managing relationships. Those are the skills the Insight Days advert lists. My reading

Chapter 12

Using this in your interview

In one line

You will not be asked for this level of detail. Use one or two points to show you understand how the business works, then stop.

A word of caution

Eve is in Talent Acquisition and the call is 15 minutes. A candidate who lectures about profit margins will not come across well. Pick the insight that fits the question, say it simply, and move on. Depth shows in how clearly you explain, not in how many numbers you quote.

If you are asked “What makes AlphaSights different?”

A 45-second answer

“Three things stood out to me. First, AlphaSights looks for the right expert for each project instead of relying only on a fixed database, which goes back to how it started in 2008. Second, one Associate team owns the whole project, from the client’s request to the call, where some competitors split that between teams. I think that is why it is known for speed. Third, it earns money when the conversation actually happens, so the whole firm is focused on making useful calls happen. Put together, those choices seem to be why it has grown to be one of the two largest firms in the industry.”

Five insights, each in one sentence

Choose the one that fits the conversation.

  1. On the model: “It is really a broker: it is paid when the client and the expert speak, so speed and fit decide everything.”
  2. On the difference: “Starting with no database forced it to hunt for experts, and that became its advantage.”
  3. On the people: “The Associate team is the engine of the business, which is why the firm invests so much in finding graduates.”
  4. On the numbers: “Its costs move with its sales, which helps explain a margin of nearly 40%.”
  5. On the future: “It is adding a library and AI tools, so it is moving from arranging calls towards being a platform for expert knowledge.”

Questions that show real understanding

  • “I read that one Associate team handles the whole project. How do new Associates learn to balance the client side and the expert side?”
  • “As the platform grows, with the library and AI tools, how is the Associate’s day changing?”
  • “What separates an Associate who is good at finding experts from one who is exceptional?”
