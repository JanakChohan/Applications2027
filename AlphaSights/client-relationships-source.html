Chapter 1

The short version

Six ideas that explain what a client relationship means at AlphaSights.

  1. A client is two things at once. It is a firm, such as a private equity fund. It is also a person at that firm who sends you a request. You serve the person. The firm pays the bill.
  2. The relationship is made of many small jobs. There is no single big contract to win. Trust is built one project at a time, often several times a week with the same person.
  3. It is never guaranteed. Most clients use more than one expert network. They can send the next request to a rival. So the relationship has to be earned again every time.
  4. “Client service” is not customer support. At AlphaSights it means running the client’s project, owning the result and carrying a revenue target. Company says
  5. You own client contact from the start. The firm says Associates “take on direct client responsibility and own commercial results from the outset”. Company says
  6. Relationships are where the growth comes from. The accounts list “growth of existing clients” first among the reasons revenue rose. Audited
The one-sentence version

“At AlphaSights a client relationship is trust built through many fast, accurate deliveries, with people who could easily go elsewhere, so every project is a chance to win or lose the next one.”

Chapter 2

What “client” means here

In one line

The word “client” covers three levels: the person who asks, the team they sit in, and the firm that pays. Each level wants something different.

The person behind the word

When someone at AlphaSights says “my client”, they usually mean a specific human being. Picture this person.

A typical client contact
  • Two to five years into their career at a private equity firm, hedge fund or consultancy.
  • Very bright, very busy and under pressure from their own boss.
  • Has been told to understand an unfamiliar industry by the end of the week.
  • Sends a short email, often between meetings, and expects a fast reply.
  • Has two or three other expert networks they could email instead.

This is my picture of the typical contact, built from how the firm and the industry describe their clients. My reading

That person is often not much older than a new Associate. So the relationship is frequently one young professional helping another young professional look good in front of their boss. That is a helpful way to think about it.

One more person you rarely meet: compliance

At investment firms, a compliance officer often has to approve a network before anyone can use it. On some projects they approve each expert before a call can be booked. Industry estimate

You may never speak to this person as an Associate. But they are part of the client. A relationship with an investor is partly a relationship with their rule book.

The three levels

What each level cares about

LevelWhat they wantWhat worries them
The userThe right expert, fast, with no hassle. To look good to their boss.Wasting an hour on a poor call. Missing their deadline.
The teamEnough good calls to make a confident decision on the deal or project.Gaps in what they know. Running out of time.
The firmA supplier that is safe, reliable and worth the money across all its teams.A legal or compliance problem. Paying too much.

A relationship is only really strong when all three levels are happy. A delighted user cannot keep using you if the firm’s compliance team has not approved you.

Chapter 3

What “client service” means here

In one line

In most companies “customer service” means answering complaints. At AlphaSights “Client Service” is the team that does the firm’s main work and earns its revenue.

Do not be misled by the name

This is an easy mistake to make. The words sound like a help desk. They are not. The Client Service team is the core of the business. It is the largest team, it runs every project, and it is where the money is made.

What “client-first” means in practice

It is a phrase every company uses. Here it has real consequences.

  • The client’s clock is your clock. Work follows client demand. One guide says Associates must be “ready to respond to high-priority requests at all times” within the working day.
  • Plans change without warning. The firm warns that projects “often change in scope or pivot entirely on short notice”, or are cancelled. Company says
  • You adapt. The client does not. If they change the question at 4pm, you start again at 4pm, cheerfully.

The three jobs inside “client service”

UnderstandWork out what the client really needs, which is often more than what they wrote.
DeliverFind the experts, present them well and make the calls happen on time.
DeepenLeave the client keen to send the next request to you.

Chapter 4

One project through the client’s eyes

In one line

A relationship is not built in meetings and lunches. It is built in the small moments of a live project: how fast you reply, how well you listened, how honest you were.

Here is a made-up project, told from the client’s side. Meet Priya. She is an associate at a private equity firm. Her firm may buy a chain of vet clinics. Her boss wants a view by Friday.

The moments that mattered

MomentWhat the Associate didWhat Priya concluded
The first replyAnswered in minutes, not hours.“They are on it.”
The clarifying callAsked why, not just what.“They understand my problem.”
The first profilesSent fewer, better-matched people.“They do not waste my time.”
The honest updateAdmitted a gap and offered a way round it.“I can believe what they tell me.”
The check-inAsked for feedback after the first call.“They care whether it worked.”
The change of planRestarted without complaint.“They make my life easier.”
The lesson

None of these moments needed special knowledge of vets or private equity. They needed speed, curiosity, honesty and care. That is why AlphaSights hires for those qualities and teaches the rest.

Chapter 5

Why trust is re-earned every time

In one line

A client is rarely “yours”. Most use several expert networks. What you really compete for is your share of the client’s requests.

Clients keep more than one network

Industry guides say investment firms and consultancies almost never rely on a single network. They keep accounts with several. Industry estimate They do this for good reasons.

  • No one network can find every kind of expert.
  • Having a back-up protects them when one network is slow.
  • Competition keeps prices and service sharp.

One guide describes large funds opening accounts with three or four networks, comparing them for a few months, and then sending most work to the best one or two. Industry estimate

The idea of “share of wallet”

Business people use the phrase share of wallet. It means: of all the money this client spends on this kind of service, how much comes to us?

What this means for how you behave

If clients were locked in

You could be slow on a quiet day. A weak match would not matter much. The client would still be there tomorrow.

Because they are not

Every request is a small contest. A slow or careless reply sends the next request to a rival. A great one brings more.

This is why the relationship is best seen as a running score, not a signed deal. It is also why growth mostly comes from existing clients: winning a bigger share from firms that already use you. Audited

But good relationships do last

Do not read this as “clients are disloyal”. In this industry, trusted relationships are very long. GLG told investors that 22 of its top 25 clients had been clients for over ten years. Audited

Clients stay because switching has a cost. A network they trust already knows how they work, has passed their compliance checks and has a record of delivering. The loyalty is real. It simply has to keep being deserved.

Chapter 6

What builds trust and what breaks it

In one line

Think of the relationship as a bank account. Every good moment is a small deposit. Every mistake is a withdrawal, and withdrawals are bigger.

Five habits of a strong client relationship

1. Listen for the real question

Clients write short briefs. “Former managers at vet chains” is what they ask for. “Can this company keep its vets?” is what they need. The Associate who finds the second question finds better experts. A good habit is to ask: “What decision is this helping you make?”

2. Be fast, and be seen to be fast

A quick “received, working on it” costs ten seconds and removes the client’s worry. Remember that a rival may have the same email.

3. Choose quality over quantity

Three experts who fit perfectly beat ten who roughly fit. Every weak profile you send teaches the client to trust your judgement a little less.

4. Tell the truth early

Sometimes the perfect expert cannot be found. Say so quickly and offer the nearest alternative. Clients forgive a gap. They do not forgive a surprise on deadline day.

5. Protect the client

Clients often do not want an expert to know who they are, or which company they are studying. A deal can be secret. Guarding that carefully is part of the relationship. So is following every compliance rule, every time. My reading

“Thought partner”

One business school case study described AlphaSights’ client service team as acting as a “thought partner”: someone who tries to understand what the client is solving, not just take an order. It also noted that the team checks in often and that clients rate each expert after a call. One example of adapting

To a private equity associate

“Two strong matches attached. Both free tomorrow before 10am. More coming by 2pm.”

Short. Assumes they know the process. Leads with times.

To a corporate strategy manager

“I have found two people who ran this exact function at your competitors. Here is what each can cover, and how the call will work.”

Warmer. Explains more. Guides the next step.

This is the “adaptability” and “empathy” the firm lists in its values. It is not acting. It is noticing what the other person needs.

Chapter 10

The other relationship: experts

In one line

An Associate serves two groups at once. How you treat experts decides what you can deliver to clients.

The expert is a relationship too. One guide describes Associates as building “relationships with both clients and industry experts”. Company says

The clientThe expert
What they wantThe right knowledge, fastRespect, clear instructions, fair pay, an interesting talk
How you win themSpeed, fit, honestyA clear, polite approach and a smooth process
If it goes wrongThey email a rival networkThey ignore your next message, and maybe every network’s

Why this matters for clients

  • A well-treated expert says yes again. That makes the next client project faster.
  • A well-prepared expert gives a better call. The client judges you on that call.
  • An expert who feels pestered stops replying. Then you have nothing to offer the client.
  • The directors say so too. The accounts speak of strengthening relationships with “clients, knowledge holders and other suppliers”. Audited “Knowledge holders” means experts.
The balancing act

Sometimes the two pull apart. The client wants a call tomorrow at 7am. The expert can only do next week. The skill is finding an answer that keeps both content. This is the “negotiation” in the Insight Days advert.

Chapter 11

Using this in your interview

In one line

Show that you know client service here means owning a result for a demanding person, and back it with a real story of your own.

If you are asked “What does client service mean to you?”

A 40-second answer

“To me it means taking ownership of someone else’s problem. From what I have read, at AlphaSights that is quite literal: an Associate is the client’s main contact on a project and is responsible for the result. I think good service comes down to a few things. Really understanding what the person needs, not just what they asked for. Being fast and keeping them informed. And being honest when something is not possible. Since clients can use other networks, I imagine you have to earn their trust again on every project.”

Your own story

You will probably be asked about your experiences. Pick a time you looked after a customer, a team-mate or anyone who relied on you. Use the STAR structure from Part 1. Then link it to one idea from this pack.

If your story is about...Link it to this idea
A demanding customer in a part-time jobStaying calm and adapting to what the person needs
Chasing a sponsor or organising an eventPersuading busy people and following up
Fixing a mistake you madeTelling the truth early and putting it right
Tutoring or helping someone learnListening for the real problem behind the question
Juggling several commitmentsBeing organised when many people depend on you

Three practice scenarios

Your Insight Days include a client brief simulation. Situations like these are likely to come up. There is no single right answer. What matters is your thinking.

Scenario 1: The brief is vague

A client writes: “Need experts on electric vehicle charging in Europe. Calls this week.”

  • Weak response: Start searching straight away and send whoever you find.
  • Strong response: Reply at once to confirm you are on it. Ask one or two sharp questions. Which countries? Charging at home or on motorways? What decision are they making? Then search.
Scenario 2: You cannot deliver in time

The client wants a former executive of one specific company by tomorrow. Nobody has replied.

  • Weak response: Stay quiet and hope someone answers.
  • Strong response: Tell the client today. Explain what you have tried. Offer the nearest alternatives, such as someone from a direct rival, or the same person next week.
Scenario 3: The client is unhappy

After a call, the client says: “That expert knew nothing about what I needed.”

  • Weak response: Defend the expert, or apologise and move on.
  • Strong response: Thank them for saying so. Ask what was missing. Take responsibility. Use the answer to find a better match quickly, and raise the question of the charge with your manager.
The pattern in all three

Respond fast. Ask before you assume. Be honest. Offer a way forward. If you remember only that, you can handle almost any client scenario they give you.

Questions to ask

  • “How soon does a new Associate start speaking to clients directly?”
  • “What does a really strong client relationship look like on your teams?”
  • “In the client brief simulation, what separates a good response from a great one?”
