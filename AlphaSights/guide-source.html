Chapter 2

AlphaSights in 60 seconds

In one line

AlphaSights is a matchmaker. On one side are people who need knowledge. On the other side are people who have it. AlphaSights finds the right match quickly and charges for the introduction.

A simple way to picture it

Think of the “phone a friend” lifeline on a quiz show. You have a hard question. You call the one person most likely to know the answer.

Now imagine you are an investor about to spend £300 million buying a company. You have a hard question. But you do not have a friend who knows. AlphaSights finds that person for you, anywhere in the world, and puts them on the phone.

The firm describes itself like this

  • Its tagline is “Knowledge on demand”. Company says
  • Its accounts say it “unlocks human knowledge to power progress for professionals, businesses and society”. Audited
  • The official legal description of its activity is “the provision of business information services to corporate clients”. Audited

What kind of company is it?

The industry is called expert networks. The work is called primary research. That means getting information straight from a person, instead of reading a report someone else wrote.

It is...It is not...
A service business that sells access to people’s knowledgeA bank, an investment fund or a consultancy. It does not invest money or give advice.
A middleman between clients and expertsThe expert itself. AlphaSights staff do not answer the client’s question. They find the person who can.
A fast, sales-driven, people-heavy business supported by technologyA pure software company, although it is building more software each year.
Do not mix these two up

AlphaSights and AlphaSense are different companies and are rivals. AlphaSense is a US research software firm. The names are so close that AlphaSights is suing AlphaSense over it (see chapter 12). Getting this wrong in the call would be a bad slip.

Chapter 3

The problem it solves

In one line

Clients must make huge decisions, quickly, about subjects they do not know well. The knowledge they need is inside people’s heads, not on the internet.

A worked example

Example (made up, but typical)

A private equity firm is thinking about buying a chain of dental clinics in Germany for €400 million. It has three weeks to decide.

The team can read the company’s financial reports. But the reports cannot answer questions like these:

  • Do patients stay loyal, or do they switch to cheaper clinics?
  • Is it hard to hire dentists in Germany right now?
  • What do rival chains think of this company?
  • Are new rules coming that would hurt profits?

The people who know are a former regional manager of a rival chain, a dentist who left the company, and a supplier who sells to the whole industry. The investors do not know these people. They have no time to find them.

AlphaSights finds them, checks them, and books the calls within days.

The legal risk, in plain English

This part matters a lot in this industry. Experts are allowed to share their general knowledge and experience. They are not allowed to share secrets.

The key term is MNPI. It stands for “material non-public information”. It means secret information that could move a company’s share price. Trading shares using it is called insider trading. It is a crime.

AlphaSights’ rules for experts come down to four points: Industry estimate

  • Do not discuss the details of your current employer.
  • Do not share confidential information or MNPI.
  • Do not take part if you have a conflict of interest.
  • Keep the conversation itself confidential.

The firm reportedly has more than 60 legal and compliance staff. Industry estimate For a client, this protection is a big part of what they are paying for.

Why the Associate job is harder than it looks

Chapter 6

How it makes money

In one line

Revenue = number of calls × price per call. AlphaSights charges the client more than it pays the expert. The gap pays for its staff and leaves a large profit.

One call, in numbers

AlphaSights does not publish its prices. Large clients negotiate their own rates. But the industry as a whole works roughly like this: Industry estimate

StepTypical amount
The client pays the expert network for a one-hour call$1,000 to $1,400
The network pays a mid-level expert for that hour$200 to $500
What is left for the network, before its own costsAbout $600 to $1,000

Very senior or rare experts, such as former chief executives or specialist doctors, can cost over $1,000 an hour. The client then pays more too.

That gap is not all profit. It has to pay for the Associates who ran the project, the compliance team, the technology and the offices.

How clients are billed

  • Per call. This is AlphaSights’ core model. The more calls a client takes, the more it pays. Industry estimate
  • Pre-paid packages. Across the industry, many clients pay up front for a bundle of call “credits”, often $50,000 to $100,000 a year to start. A rare expert may cost more than one credit. Industry estimate
  • Subscriptions. Library and software products are usually sold as ongoing access instead of one call at a time.

The revenue sources, biggest first

Why I am confident that calls are the biggest earner
  • For most of its life, AlphaSights sold only calls. The library and AI tools are recent additions.
  • Associates’ performance pay is tied to the number of client-to-expert interactions they set up each month. Company says A firm pays people for what earns it money.
  • The firm says it has delivered more than 3 million client-to-expert interactions. Company says

If Eve asks what the biggest revenue source is, say “expert calls, as far as I can tell from public information”. That is honest and correct.

Where each $100 of revenue goes

Why the business model is so attractive

  • No stock, no factories. It sells time and access. It needs people, laptops and phones.
  • It mostly pays experts only when a call happens. So its biggest direct cost rises and falls with its sales. That lowers the risk.
  • Clients come back. Every new deal or project creates new questions. The accounts list “growth of existing clients” first among the reasons revenue rose. Audited
  • Each call builds an asset. Every expert recruited and every transcript created can be useful again later.
  • It funds itself. The firm ended 2025 with $72.3 million in cash. Audited

Why AlphaSights chose these clients

This is a good question to think about, because it shows you understand the business and not just the list.

  1. The stakes are huge. When a decision is worth hundreds of millions, a call costing about $1,000 is tiny. Price is not the main worry. Being wrong is.

The big idea

Expert networks already existed in 2008. The biggest was GLG, founded in New York in 1998. The older firms kept a fixed database of experts who had signed up.

AlphaSights started with no database at all. So it had to go out and find new experts for every single project. That weakness became its strength. Clients got experts who were a closer fit, because they were chosen for that exact question.

Chapter 12

Commercial awareness

In one line

AlphaSights does well when its clients are busy making deals and starting projects. Its biggest long-term question is what AI does to the value of an expert call.

Commercial awareness means understanding what in the outside world helps or hurts a business. For AlphaSights there are six forces to know.

1. Deal activity

When investors buy and sell more companies, they need more research, so they book more calls. When deals slow down, call volumes fall.

  • Global mergers and acquisitions reached about $3.9 trillion in the first nine months of 2026. That is up 28% and the highest for that period since 2001. Industry estimate
  • But the third quarter fell about 41% from the quarter before, to roughly $993 billion, as borrowing costs rose. Industry estimate
  • Private equity firms are holding about $1.7 trillion they have raised but not yet spent. This is nicknamed “dry powder”. They are under pressure to invest it. Industry estimate

Why it matters: high interest rates make borrowing expensive. Private equity uses a lot of borrowed money. So high rates mean fewer deals, and fewer deals mean fewer calls.

2. Artificial intelligence

This is the biggest topic in the industry. It cuts both ways.

The threat
  • AI can answer basic questions that once needed a call.
  • Libraries let clients reuse old calls cheaply. This has squeezed call profits in parts of the market.
  • AI could do parts of an Associate’s work.
  • New AI-first rivals are raising money.
The opportunity
  • AI only knows what has been written down. Experts know what has not. That gets more valuable.
  • AI makes AlphaSights faster and cheaper to run.
  • New products: AlphaGPT, AI summaries, AI Interviewer.
  • AI companies themselves need experts.

AlphaSights’ rising margin suggests that, so far, technology is helping it more than hurting it. My reading

A strong commercial point to make

“The thing I find most interesting is AI. On the surface it looks like a threat, because an AI tool can answer simple questions. But AI only knows what is already written down. The value of AlphaSights is knowledge that is still in people’s heads. So I think demand for real experts could actually rise, and the firms that win will be the ones that combine both, which seems to be what AlphaSights is doing with its platform.”

3. The shift from calls to content

Rivals are betting that clients want a searchable library and software, not just calls. AlphaSense paid about $930 million for Tegus in 2024 to get its transcript library, and launched a new AI research agent in October 2026. AlphaSights has responded by building AlphaNow and AlphaGPT.

4. Rules and compliance

  • Insider trading. Around 2010 and 2011, staff and experts at a US network called Primary Global Research were convicted of passing secret company information to hedge funds. The whole industry tightened its rules afterwards. Trust is everything in this business.
  • China. In 2023, Chinese police raided the expert network Capvision as part of an anti-spying campaign. AlphaSights has an office in Shanghai, so politics is a real risk for it.

5. Competition and price pressure

More networks are chasing the same clients and the same experts. Popular experts get tired of being contacted by many firms. Cheaper rivals push prices down.

6. Currency movements

AlphaSights reports in US dollars but pays many costs in other currencies, such as pounds. Its accounts list exchange rates as a main financial risk. Audited

“Deals” and “mandates”: an honest note

You asked about recent deals or mandates the firm has won. Banks and law firms announce those. AlphaSights does not, for two reasons.

  • Its client work is confidential. A private equity firm does not want the world to know which company it is researching.
  • It does not win one big contract at a time. It wins thousands of small projects from repeat clients.

So if you are asked about recent developments, talk about the growth in its accounts, its new AI products and the AlphaSense case. Do not go looking for “deals”.

Chapter 13

Your interview: questions and answers

In one line

Fifteen minutes. Three topics: how AlphaSights works, why you applied, and your experiences. Short, clear answers win.

What to expect

  • Eve works in Talent Acquisition, not Client Service. She is checking that you understand the firm, want to be there and can communicate clearly.
  • In 15 minutes there is time for about five or six questions. Aim for answers of 45 to 60 seconds.
  • The invite says there is no expectation to have your camera on. Turning it on anyway usually helps you connect.
  • “Outstanding communication skills” is in the advert. How you speak is being judged as much as what you say.

Likely questions, with an answer plan

1. What do you know about AlphaSights? What does it do?

Use the 30-second answer from chapter 1. Then add one detail that shows depth, such as the three client types or the 37% margin.

2. Who are AlphaSights’ clients?

Private equity firms, hedge funds, strategy consultancies, investment banks, venture capital firms and large corporations. Then say why: “What they share is big decisions, tight deadlines and a need for knowledge that is not public.”

3. How does AlphaSights make money?

“Clients pay for each interaction with an expert, mainly one-hour calls. AlphaSights pays the expert for their time and earns the difference. It is also building a platform with a research library and AI tools.”

4. Why did you apply for the Insight Days?

Give two or three real reasons. Strong options:

  • You want to test a commercial, client-facing career by doing it, through the client brief simulation.
  • The role mixes things you enjoy: learning new industries quickly, persuading people, working to a target.
  • You are drawn to the early responsibility and promotion based on performance.

Link each reason to something true about you.

5. Why AlphaSights and not another firm?

Pick points only AlphaSights can claim: still led by its founders, one of the two largest in its industry, built on finding fresh experts for each project, Associates own the whole project.

6. What does an Associate do? What would you find hardest?

Walk through the five steps in Diagram 2. For the hardest part, be honest. For example: “Persuading a senior stranger to give up an hour, and staying positive when most people do not reply.” Then say how you would handle it.

7. Tell me about a time you worked under pressure, showed resilience, persuaded someone, or failed.

Use the STAR structure. Keep it to one minute.

  • Situation: one sentence of background.
  • Task: what you had to achieve.
  • Action: what you did. Spend most of your time here. Say “I”, not “we”.
  • Result: what happened, with a number if you can, and what you learned.
8. Walk me through your CV. Tell me about yourself.

Three parts in 60 seconds: what you study, your two best experiences, and why that leads you to this.

Chapter 14

Jargon buster

Plain-English meanings for the terms you may hear.

Expert network
A company that connects clients with people who have specialist knowledge, for a fee.
Primary research
Getting information directly from a person. The opposite is secondary research: reading reports and articles.
Expert (or advisor)
Someone with first-hand experience of an industry, such as a former manager, a customer or a supplier.
Interaction
AlphaSights’ word for one connection between a client and an expert, usually a call.
Private equity (PE)
Firms that buy whole companies, improve them and sell them later.
Hedge fund
An investment firm that buys and sells shares and other assets, often quickly.
Venture capital (VC)
Firms that invest in young start-ups.
Strategy consultancy
A firm that advises companies on big decisions. Examples: McKinsey, Bain, BCG.
Due diligence
The careful checking an investor does before buying a company.
M&A
Mergers and acquisitions. Companies buying or combining with other companies.
Dry powder
Money that investors have raised but not yet spent.
MNPI
Material non-public information. Secret information that could move a share price. Illegal to trade on.
Compliance
Making sure the firm and its experts follow the law and the rules.
Transcript
A written record of what was said on a call.
Revenue (or turnover)
All the money a company brings in from sales.
Profit before tax
What is left after all costs, before the tax bill.
Margin
Profit as a share of revenue. A 37% margin means $37 of profit for every $100 of sales.
B2B
Business to business. Selling to companies, not to the public.
Mandate
A formal job that a client hires a bank or adviser to do. Not a term used much at AlphaSights.
Custom recruiting
Finding new experts specially for each project.
