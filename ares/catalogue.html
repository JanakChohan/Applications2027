Part 1

What a fund actually is

Thirty seconds of groundwork that makes everything after it readable.

A fund is a pot of money with rules. Investors put money in. A manager decides what to buy with it. A legal document — the limited partnership agreement or prospectus — sets out what the manager may buy, what they get paid, how long it lasts, and how investors get their money back. That is the whole concept.

Three things follow, and they explain why there are so many funds:

  • A fund is a separate legal entity, not an account at Ares. If Ares disappeared tomorrow, the fund and its assets would still exist. That separation is the point.
  • One fund does one thing. Investors buy a specific exposure, so a fund that lends to US companies cannot suddenly buy Japanese warehouses. To offer a second strategy, you need a second fund.
  • Different investors need different containers. A US pension fund, a European individual and a Japanese insurer face different tax and regulatory rules. The same strategy may be offered through three different legal wrappers to reach all three.

That is why Ares runs over 300 funds in Credit alone, and why the catalogue looks intimidating at first. It is not 300 ideas. It is roughly a dozen strategies, each offered in several containers, each refreshed every few years.

Part 2

Two shapes: funds that end, and funds that don't

The first question to ask about any fund. It changes everything else.

Shape A  Closed-end / drawdown

Has a fixed life, usually about ten years. Investors commit money which is called in instalments, invested, then returned as assets are sold. Then the fund closes forever.

Spot it by: a Roman numeral. ACE VI, SDL III, ASOF II. Numerals exist because the fund dies and you must raise a successor. Numeral = finite life.

Shape B  Open-ended / evergreen

Has no end date. Money comes in monthly, stays invested, and is reinvested indefinitely. Investors exit via a periodic tender, usually capped at about 5% per quarter.

Spot it by: no numeral, and usually the words "open-ended", "perpetual", or a ticker. ARCC, ASIF, the non-traded REITs. No numeral = probably perpetual.

The shortcut that works almost every time

If an Ares fund has a Roman numeral after its name, it ends. If it doesn't, it probably doesn't. ACE VI will wind up and be replaced by ACE VII. ARCC has run since 2004 and has no planned end. This single rule lets you classify most of the catalogue on sight — and it is a genuinely useful thing to be able to do in front of someone.

Part 3

The container types

The legal wrappers. Each exists because a particular investor could not otherwise buy in.

ContainerWhat it is, in plain termsWhy it exists — and the Ares example
Commingled fund
the default		A private partnership. Many investors pool money; the manager invests it. Not listed, not public, minimums in the millions.The standard institutional vehicle. Ares: SDL III, ACE VI, ASOF II, Pathfinder III.
BDC
business development company		A US-regulated company that lends to mid-sized American businesses. Must distribute most of its income to shareholders.A 1980 US law created BDCs specifically so ordinary people could invest in private companies. Ares: ARCC (listed), ASIF (non-traded).
REIT
real estate investment trust		The same idea for property. Owns buildings, distributes rental income, receives tax advantages for doing so.Lets an individual own a share of a warehouse portfolio. Ares: two US non-traded REITs, a Tokyo-listed J-REIT, and ACRE (NYSE-listed).
Interval fundA registered fund that must offer to buy back shares at set intervals, by regulation — typically 5% of net assets per quarter.The repurchase window is mandated rather than discretionary, which is a meaningful investor protection. Ares: CADC, run through the CION Ares Management joint venture.
CLO
collateralised loan obligation		A vehicle holding a pool of loans, financed in layers of differing risk. Safer layers get paid first.An efficient way to finance a large loan portfolio. Ares manages over 100 such vehicles; they produce roughly 2–3% of total management fees.
SMA
separately managed account		A portfolio run for one investor, built to their specification, rather than a pooled fund.Large institutions want control, transparency and bespoke terms. $139.6bn — 30% of Ares' institutional AUM.
Co-investment vehicleA side pot letting an investor put extra money into one specific deal, alongside the main fund.Usually offered at reduced or zero fees, as a reward to large investors. Ares: "Ares PE Co-Invest" appears in the subsidiary list.
ELTIF
European Long-Term Investment Fund		An EU wrapper designed so individual Europeans can access private assets across borders.Without it, selling a private credit fund to retail investors in multiple EU states is impractical. Ares: the Open-Ended European Direct Lending ELTIF.
SPAC
special purpose acquisition company		A listed shell that raises money first and finds a company to merge with later.Largely a 2020–21 phenomenon. Ares: AAC II became Kodiak AI (Nasdaq: KDK) in September 2025.
Feeder fundA fund whose main job is to invest into another fund, usually to serve a different investor base or jurisdiction.Ares: AUSIF — formerly ADSIF, renamed in May 2026 — invests roughly 80% into ASIF.

Three patterns in this table

1. The oldest vehicles have the best returns. ARCC (2004) at 12.0% and the J-REIT (2012) at 13.3% have compounded through multiple cycles uninterrupted. That is the core argument for perpetual structures.

2. The retail product is cheaper than the listed one. ARCC charges 1.50% on total assets with a 20% Part I fee; ASIF charges 1.25% on NAV with 12.5%. Most people assume the opposite.

3. "Evergreen" is not the same as "retail". Of Ares' $223.3bn of perpetual capital, only about a third sits in wealth vehicles. The rest is institutional — private commingled funds, listed vehicles and separately managed accounts, many for insurers.

Part 9

One-page reference card

If you memorise one page of this document, make it this one.

The six funds to know by name

  1. ARCC — the listed BDC, 2004, $35.9bn, ~31% of Credit Group fees. The crown jewel.
  2. ASIF — the non-traded BDC, 2023, $24.3bn. The wealth flagship, and the one that pro-rated tenders in 2026.
  3. SDL III — US senior direct lending, 2023, ~$33.6bn total capital base. Among the largest direct lending funds ever raised.
  4. ACE VI — European direct lending, 2022, $24.7bn. The European moat.
  5. Pathfinder III — asset-based finance, closed $8.5bn in June 2026, oversubscribed against a $6.5bn target.
  6. APMF — secondaries, evergreen, 14.2% net since 2022. Best return of the perpetual set.

The five rules for reading any fund

  1. Roman numeral = it ends. No numeral = probably perpetual.
  2. Always ask gross or net. SDL III levered: 24.6% gross, 17.3% net.
  3. Always ask levered or unlevered. Same fund, 12.9% versus 24.6% gross.
  4. Always ask which currency class. SDL III: 9.6% net in USD, 10.3% in GBP.
  5. Check the vintage. A 2023 fund is barely realised; judging it on IRR alone is premature.
