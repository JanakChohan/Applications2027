Part 1

The problem these funds exist to solve

Start here. Everything else is engineering built on top of one awkward mismatch.

Private assets — loans to companies, warehouses, data centres — take years to pay off. People who want to invest in them increasingly want their money back on reasonable notice. Those two facts do not fit together, and an evergreen fund is the structure built to make them fit.

Evergreen fund. A fund with no end date. Money comes in continuously, stays invested, and is reinvested indefinitely. Investors can ask to leave periodically, but only a limited amount per period. Also called perpetual, semi-liquid, or open-ended. These words are used almost interchangeably.

1.1 Why the old structure excluded most people

For forty years, private markets ran on one model: the drawdown fund. You promised £10m, the manager called it in pieces over five years, invested it, sold the assets over the following five, and gave you your money back. Then the fund closed and you did it again with the next one.

That model works fine if you are a £50bn pension scheme. It is close to impossible if you are a dentist with £200,000 to invest, for four reasons:

J-curve. The shape of a drawdown fund's return chart over time. It dips below zero early (fees are charged, investments have not yet appreciated) then rises as assets are sold. Drawn out, it looks like the letter J.

Part 3

How one actually works

The machinery, piece by piece. This is the part worth genuinely understanding.

3.1 What is inside the fund

An evergreen fund is not one portfolio. It is two, deliberately, and the split is the central design decision the manager makes.

3.2 Getting in: net asset value

NAV (net asset value). The value of everything the fund owns, minus what it owes, divided by the number of shares. It is the price at which you buy in and the price at which you are bought out.

For a stock market fund, NAV is easy — the market prices everything every second. For a fund holding private loans, there is no market price. The fund must estimate what each loan is worth, typically using discounted cash flow and comparable market yields, reviewed by independent valuation firms and the auditor. Most private credit evergreen funds strike NAV monthly.

This matters enormously, and we return to it in Part 8: every investor buys and sells at a price the manager calculated.

3.3 Getting out: the tender offer

This is the heart of the structure. Each quarter, the fund offers to buy back a limited number of shares. The standard cap is 5% of shares outstanding per quarter — roughly 20% a year if used fully.

The one sentence that captures the whole structure

An evergreen fund is a liquidity transformation engine. It takes assets that take three to seven years to repay and turns them into a product offering quarterly, partial, rationed liquidity — managed with a cash sleeve, a credit line and a cap. Every argument about these funds is ultimately an argument about whether that transformation is honestly explained and prudently sized.

Worth knowing · why Ares runs an ELTIF specifically

Ares has $84.7bn of European direct lending — one of the two or three genuinely scaled European platforms. An ELTIF is the only efficient way to sell that strategy to European individual investors across borders. The wrapper exists to match the distribution opportunity to the asset Ares already has, which is the general rule: managers do not build evergreen funds because the structure is elegant, they build them because a specific channel cannot buy the drawdown version.

Part 7

The 2026 stress test

The structure's first real examination. Here is what happened and what it actually proved.

Evergreen funds grew up in a benign decade. 2026 was the first year they were properly tested — and the results are more interesting than either the cheerleaders or the critics will tell you.

7.1 What triggered it

  • Two fraud-driven collapses in late 2025. Tricolor pledged the same auto-loan collateral to multiple lenders. First Brands failed with $11.6bn of liabilities amid allegations of fabricated receivables. Neither was a credit-cycle event, but both shook confidence.
  • The AI thesis. Investors decided artificial intelligence might destroy the mid-market software companies that private credit funds lend to. Ares shares fell roughly 32% in Q1 2026.
  • The result: a wave of redemption requests across the industry's non-traded vehicles.

7.2 What happened, across the industry

7.3 What the detail showed

Evidence the structure held

  • Over 95% of ASIF investors did not request redemption at all.
  • Requests from the US private wealth channel were only 2.4% of shares — below the cap — and fell more than 35% quarter on quarter.
  • Nearly half of all requests came from smaller non-US institutions and family offices representing under 1% of the fund's 20,000+ shareholders.
  • No fund was forced into a fire sale. The caps did their job.
  • Ares still raised a record $36.4bn in Q2 2026 and closed Pathfinder III oversubscribed at $8.5bn against a $6.5bn target.

Evidence the critics have a point

  • Two consecutive quarters of pro-ration at ASIF. Investors who wanted out got 43.1% in Q1, then had to queue again.
  • The promise of "quarterly liquidity" proved to be quarterly partial liquidity precisely when people wanted it most.
  • Industry-wide, non-traded BDC fundraising fell 82% year on year in Q2 2026, to $2bn.
  • US evergreen private credit net assets fell about $1bn in Q1 2026 — the segment Ares leads is the one losing momentum, while private equity and venture evergreen funds gained.
  • In April 2026, House Financial Services Committee Democrats sent inquiries to Ares and six peers covering marketing, valuation, fees, leverage and BDC handling.
The honest verdict

The structure worked mechanically and failed reputationally. No fund collapsed, no fire sale occurred, the caps protected remaining investors exactly as designed. But a large number of people discovered, at the worst moment, that "semi-liquid" meant something more restrictive than they had understood. That gap — between the mechanism and the expectation — is the single biggest unresolved issue in this corner of the industry, and it is a communication problem before it is a structural one.

Part 8

The criticisms, taken seriously

Five real objections. You should be able to state each one better than a sceptic would.

#The criticismThe argument againstThe defence — and what remains true
1Liquidity mismatch The fund promises quarterly liquidity while holding assets that take years to repay. Critics argue that when markets turn, the tension resolves one way: the fund gates. The cap is disclosed up front and protects remaining investors from forced selling. Still true: the marketing emphasises the liquidity far more prominently than the cap, and 2026 proved the cap binds in exactly the conditions people most want out.
2NAV staleness Private assets are valued by periodic appraisal, not by a market. The reported NAV may lag reality — so investors buy and sell at a price the manager calculated. Valuations use discounted cash flow and market-comparable yields, with independent third-party review and annual audit. Still true: there is genuine judgement involved, and a lagging NAV advantages whoever transacts first. This is why clear explanation of valuation method is the top trust driver in LP surveys.
3Cash drag The liquidity sleeve — perhaps 5–20% of the fund — sits in cash and short-dated credit yielding far less than private loans. Investors pay private-market fees on public-market assets. The sleeve is what makes any liquidity possible; it is the cost of the wrapper. Still true: an evergreen fund will generally trail an equivalent drawdown fund over a full cycle, and investors should expect that trade.
4Fees on unrealised marks Performance fees can be charged on gains that exist only as valuations, not as cash. The manager both calculates the mark and is paid on it. Hurdles, high-water marks and loss carry-forwards limit this, and Part I fees at Ares are charged on net investment income — actual earnings — rather than appreciation. Still true: the conflict is structural and cannot be fully designed away, only disclosed and governed.
5Suitability Are individual investors equipped to judge an illiquid, complex, hard-to-value product? Regulators are asking exactly this. Suitability standards, minimums and adviser gatekeeping exist. ASIF's stated standards, for instance, require minimum income and net-worth thresholds. Still true: the April 2026 congressional inquiries indicate the question is live, and distribution standards may tighten.
How to handle these in an interview

Do not defend the structure reflexively. The strongest thing you can do is state the criticism accurately and then add the nuance — "the liquidity mismatch is real, and 2026 showed the cap binds exactly when people want out; what the data also showed is that over 95% of ASIF holders didn't ask to leave and the US wealth channel's requests were under the cap at 2.4%." That is a person who understands the product, not a person selling it. Someone who claims there are no downsides will not be believed by anyone who works in the industry.

Part 9

What this means for the role

Why evergreen funds make Client Solutions a harder and better job than it was a decade ago.

The shift from drawdown to evergreen changes the client-facing job in four concrete ways. These are worth understanding because they are the actual texture of the work.

1 The relationship never ends

A drawdown fund has a beginning, middle and end — you raise it, then you raise the next one. An evergreen fund has a client who can leave every ninety days, forever. There is no "closed" moment. Retention stops being a periodic campaign and becomes a continuous obligation.

2 Flows are the scoreboard

The relevant metric becomes net flow — subscriptions minus redemptions — reported monthly. Ares' wealth channel generated $3.9bn of gross equity commitments in Q2 2026, up 15%. You would see that number constantly, and your work would move it in both directions.

3 You explain the same thing to two audiences

Ares runs the same direct lending strategy for a sovereign wealth fund via a bespoke mandate and for an individual via ASIF. The asset is identical; the vocabulary, the regulatory wrapper and the concerns are completely different. Very few seats teach you both.

4 The hardest conversation is now routine

Explaining pro-ration to someone who wanted their money is not an edge case in 2026 — it is a recurring task. Doing it accurately, without defensiveness, while explaining why the cap protects them, is the most transferable skill the structure produces.

Say this — if evergreen funds come up

"What interests me about the evergreen shift is that it changes what client service is. In a drawdown fund an unhappy investor is locked in until the wind-down. In a perpetual vehicle they can file a redemption request every quarter, so satisfaction turns into a revenue variable you can actually measure. Ares has about $223bn of perpetual capital and earns 93% of management fees from perpetual or long-dated vehicles, so that dynamic sits right at the centre of the firm. And 2026 tested it properly — ASIF pro-rated two tenders, which was uncomfortable, but over ninety-five percent of holders didn't ask to leave and the US wealth channel came in under the cap. I'd rather learn the job in that environment."

9.1 Twelve terms, defined

TermPlain meaning
Evergreen / perpetualA fund with no end date. Money stays and is reinvested indefinitely.
Semi-liquidThe same thing, named after what it offers investors: some liquidity, not full liquidity.
NAVNet asset value. What the fund owns minus what it owes, per share. The buy and sell price.
Tender offerThe fund's periodic offer to buy back shares. Usually quarterly, usually capped at 5%.
Pro-rationScaling everyone's request down by the same factor when requests exceed the cap.
GatingInformal term for limiting redemptions. Usually describes pro-ration in practice.
Liquidity sleeveThe portion held in cash and liquid assets to fund redemptions. Roughly 5–20%.
Cash dragThe return lost because the sleeve earns less than the private assets.
Early repurchase deductionA penalty — commonly 2% — on shares sold within a year. ASIF repurchases these at 98% of NAV.
Part I feeA quarterly fee on net investment income above a hurdle. Ares counts it as a management fee because it recurs.

The six things to carry away

  1. An evergreen fund has no end date. Money comes in monthly at NAV; it leaves via a quarterly tender capped at about 5%.
  2. It is a liquidity transformation engine — illiquid assets, rationed liquidity, managed with a cash sleeve and a cap.
  3. When requests exceed the cap, everyone is scaled down. ASIF honoured 43.1% of each Q1 2026 request, paying $523.5m. The cap protects those who stayed.
  4. "Evergreen" is not "retail". Only about a third of Ares' $223.3bn of perpetual capital sits in wealth vehicles; the rest is institutional, including insurers.
  5. It is the business, not a side business. 93% of Ares' management fees come from perpetual or long-dated vehicles.
  6. The real criticisms are liquidity mismatch, NAV staleness and cash drag. State them accurately — you will be more credible than someone who claims there are none.
