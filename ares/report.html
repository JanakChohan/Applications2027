Part 1

The industry from zero

What an alternative asset manager actually is, who pays it, and how it earns money.

Start with the only sentence that matters: Ares is not an investor of its own money. It is a manager of other people's money, and it gets paid a percentage for managing it. Almost everything else about the firm — its strategy, its structure, and the entire reason your role exists — follows from that one fact.

1.1 The four players

Every private markets transaction has the same four characters. Learn these four words and 80% of the jargon collapses.

The one-line business model

Ares raises money from asset owners, puts it into funds, lends or invests it, and charges a percentage fee on the money it manages. It earns that fee whether or not the fund has made a gain yet. That is why the industry obsesses over one number — AUM, assets under management — and why a sales job here is a revenue job, not a support job.

1.2 Why does this industry exist at all?

Because banks retreated. After the 2008 financial crisis, regulation made it expensive for banks to hold risky loans to mid-sized companies on their balance sheets. Those companies still needed to borrow. At the same time, pension funds and insurers faced a decade of very low interest rates and could not hit their return targets in government bonds.

Private credit is the bridge between those two problems: money that needs yield, meeting borrowers that need capital, with a manager in the middle doing the underwriting the bank used to do. Ares' own 10-K describes its direct lending business as serving companies "increasingly underserved by traditional bank lenders." Ares FY2025 10-K, Item 1.

1.3 The two shapes of fund — and why this matters more than anything else

This is the single most important technical distinction in the report. Nearly every strategic decision Ares has made in the last six years comes back to it.

Shape A  The drawdown fund

The traditional model. Investors commit (say) $100m but don't hand it over. Ares "calls" the money down as it finds deals. The fund invests for ~5 years, harvests for ~5, then dies and returns the cash.

The problem for Ares: the fee dies with the fund. Every few years you must go back and raise the successor fund — Fund IV, Fund V, Fund VI — or your revenue falls off a cliff. This is a treadmill.

Shape B  Perpetual capital

The modern model. The vehicle has no end date. Money comes in, stays in, and is reinvested indefinitely. Investors can ask to leave, but usually only a limited percentage per quarter.

Why Ares wants this: the fee never expires. Ares defines it precisely — capital that has "an indefinite term, is not in liquidation, and for which there is no immediate requirement to return invested capital." FY2025 10-K glossary. This is an annuity.

Edge · the number that reframes the firm

93% of Ares' management fees in 2025 came from perpetual capital or long-dated funds (funds with a contractual life of five years or more at inception). It was 95% in 2024, 2023, 2022 and 2021. FY2021–FY2025 10-Ks, "Management Fees By Type".

Read that again. Roughly nineteen out of every twenty fee dollars Ares earns are contractually locked in for years or forever. This is the answer to "how are they surviving the fundraising slowdown" — and almost no other candidate will have it.

Edge · the embedded growth argument

At 31 December 2025 Ares had $78.8bn of AUM available for future deployment plus $4.3bn of development assets not yet stabilised, which it states could "collectively generate approximately $730.4 million in potential incremental annual management fees, which represents a 23% embedded growth rate in our 2025 base management fees." In plain English: Ares could stop raising a single new dollar tomorrow and still grow management fees by roughly a quarter simply by deploying what it already holds.

Part 2

What Ares actually is

Founded 1997. A credit house that grew into a four-legged platform — and never stopped being a credit house.

2.1 The origin story, and why it still shapes the firm

Ares was founded in 1997 in Los Angeles by Antony (Tony) Ressler, John Kissick and Bennett Rosenthal. Ressler had co-founded Apollo Management in 1990 and ran its capital markets activities — high-yield bonds, leveraged loans, distressed debt — until leaving to start Ares. Rosenthal came from Merrill Lynch's global leveraged finance group. For its first five years Ares operated as effectively the West Coast credit affiliate of Apollo.

Two things follow from that history and both are worth saying out loud in an interview:

  • Ares is a credit firm by DNA, not by pivot. Blackstone and KKR are private equity firms that later added credit. Ares started in leveraged finance. Its 10-K says every investment group employs "a disciplined, credit-oriented investment philosophy" — including the private equity and real estate teams.
  • It is not a New York firm. Headquarters remain Los Angeles. Culturally this matters: Ares' stated values are "collaborative, responsible, entrepreneurial, self-aware and trustworthy," and the 10-K explicitly notes there is no centralised investment committee — committees are deliberately built with overlapping membership across groups.

Ares covers 520+ private equity sponsors in the US and 435+ in Europe. That is the real moat, and it is not a marketing claim — it is disclosed in the 10-K. Part 3

Follow the money: the insurance engine

From a retired person's monthly cheque, through an insurer, into an Ares fund, out to a company, and back again.

You asked for this specifically, and you were right to. Insurance is the most important and least understood part of Ares' story — and it is the part that most candidates will get factually wrong, because they will assume Ares works the way Apollo does. It does not.

3.1 Why insurers are the perfect client for private credit

An insurer that sells annuities — a product where you hand over a lump sum and they promise you a fixed income for life — has a very specific problem. They have made a promise that runs for thirty or forty years, and they must hold assets that generate enough income to fund it. They need:

  • Long duration — assets that pay out over decades, to match the liability.
  • Predictable income — not capital gains; contractual coupons.
  • Yield above government bonds — the spread between what they earn and what they promised is their profit.
  • Investment-grade-equivalent credit quality — because regulators charge capital against risky assets.
  • They do not need liquidity — nobody can demand their annuity back early.

That is an almost perfect description of a directly originated senior secured loan. Insurers can accept illiquidity — which is exactly what private credit charges a premium for. Roughly $1 trillion of the ~$6 trillion held by US life and annuity insurers is now in private credit.

Careful · the mistake most candidates will make

Ares does not run the Apollo model, and saying it does will expose you.

Apollo owns Athene. KKR owns Global Atlantic outright. Those firms put an insurance company's balance sheet on their own books: they capture the investment spread as well as a fee, but they also carry the insurance risk, the capital requirements and the ratings-agency scrutiny.

Ares deliberately went the other way. Its partner insurer, Aspida, is capitalised largely by third-party money — in January 2025 Ares announced it had raised over $2.3bn of equity commitments for Aspida "from third parties, Ares and affiliates of Ares," on top of $700m of credit facilities. Ares is treated by the Bermuda Monetary Authority as the "shareholder controller" of Aspida Re, and acts as its "dedicated investment manager, capital solutions and corporate development partner" — but the economics are structured to keep the balance sheet risk largely off Ares.

Edge · Ares' own words, and the phrase to use

The 2025 Annual Shareholder Letter, signed by Michael Arougheti and Antony Ressler, states: "With more than $150 billion in available capital matched with our asset-light balance sheet approach, we believe we are very well positioned for growth in the year ahead." "Asset-light" is the firm's own self-description. Use that phrase. It signals you have read the shareholder letter, not the website.

The 2025 shift is subtler than an acquisition. Ares has started operating assets, not just financing them, and it has given the operating businesses their own brands:

Marq Logistics

The consolidated global brand for Ares' industrial property platform, formed after GCP. Ares owns, develops and operates warehouses across the Americas, Europe and APAC — it is not merely a landlord-by-proxy.

Ada Infrastructure

The in-house data centre development and operating platform, staffed with people from hyperscalers. As of Q2 2026 it has approximately 1 gigawatt of compute capacity across seven large campuses. Ares raised its first data centre development fund at $2.4bn in 2025.

Why this matters commercially: an integrated owner-operator captures development profit, operating margin and the management fee, rather than just the fee. For a client-facing role, it is also a differentiated story to sell — you are not offering exposure to data centres, you are offering exposure to a team that builds and runs them.

The market right now — and why it is still a good time to join

An honest read of the private credit slowdown, what it is doing to Ares, and the argument for going in anyway.

You asked for macro news on fundraising, and specifically whether it is still a good time to want this role. The honest answer is yes, but not for the reason most people give — and being able to discuss the stress credibly will do more for you than pretending it isn't there.

9.1 What actually happened

Private credit had a decade-long boom. In late 2025 two bankruptcies punctured the confidence:

  • Tricolor, a subprime auto lender, filed in September 2025 after investigators found it had pledged the same auto-loan collateral to multiple lenders. Lenders were owed more than $900m.

Neither was a credit-cycle event. Both were fraud. Cambridge Associates' assessment is that they are idiosyncratic rather than systemic. Honesty notes — read before you quote anything
  • The relationship management headcount series has a definitional break. FY2019–20 disclosed a combined "strategy and relationship management teams" figure (~120, ~140); FY2021 onward narrowed to relationship management alone. The 55→200+ comparison (FY2022→FY2025) is like-for-like; do not compare it to the 2019 number.
  • The word-frequency analysis in 4.2 is my own count of the full text of each 10-K, not an Ares disclosure. It is reproducible but it is an interpretation, so present it as an observation rather than a fact.
  • The CV-versus-answers weighting in 12.2 is an inferred model, not published by Ares or Dartmouth Partners.
  • Peer AUM figures are approximate and measured on different bases between firms — Brookfield reports fee-bearing capital, others report total AUM. Use them for order of magnitude, not precision.
  • The question ranking in 12.3 assumes three standard archetypes because the actual questions were not available. Verify against the real form.
  • Fund performance figures are as at 31 December 2025 and are historical. Past performance is not indicative of future results.

If you remember only five things

  1. 93% of management fees come from perpetual or long-dated capital. That is why Ares is resilient — not an insurance balance sheet.
  2. Ares calls itself "asset-light." It is the largest credit-concentrated manager that has not bought an insurer. Apollo owns Athene; KKR owns Global Atlantic; Ares owns neither.
  3. The sales team went from 55+ to 200+ in three years, plus 175+ in wealth. The firm's answer to a hard fundraising market was more distribution.
  4. "Nearly half of our global distribution partners currently offer just one Ares product." That sentence from the shareholder letter is the commercial rationale for your job.
  5. Ares raised a record $36.4bn in Q2 2026 while industry non-traded BDC fundraising fell 82% — but it also pro-rated two consecutive ASIF tenders. Hold both facts at once.
