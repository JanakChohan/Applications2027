Part 1

What the team is — in one page

The structural fact first, because everything technical follows from it.

Client Solutions is the team that owns the relationship between Ares and the people whose money it manages. It is not a support function attached to the investment teams. It is a separate, firm-wide group — and where it sits in the org chart determines what you would learn.

The structural fact. Ares reports in five buckets: four investment groups (Credit, Real Assets, Secondaries, Private Equity) plus the Operations Management Group. Client Solutions, Investor Relations and wealth distribution sit in the OMG, and the 10-K states the OMG's "revenues and expenses are not allocated to our operating segments."

In plain terms: the team sits underneath all four investment groups at once, not inside one. A salesperson at a firm organised the other way learns one asset class deeply. Here, an investor might buy senior secured loans to US mid-market companies, a Japanese logistics portfolio, a stake in somebody else's private equity fund bought second-hand, or a loan secured on music royalties — and the same person covers all of it.

Part 2

Anatomy of the product: the fee stack

You cannot sell a fund you cannot price. This is the actual mechanism.

Every conversation you would have eventually reaches the same question: what am I paying, on what, and when? Ares' fees are not a single number. They are a stack of four components that behave differently. Here is the whole thing, using Ares' own published schedules.

Catch-up, explained once. A hurdle means the manager earns nothing until the fund returns a minimum. A catch-up then lets the manager collect its full percentage from the first dollar once the hurdle is cleared, rather than only on the excess. ARCC's Part I fee has a 20% rate, a 1.75%-per-quarter hurdle and a 20% catch-up. Without the catch-up the manager would earn materially less; with it, crossing the hurdle is a cliff rather than a ramp.
Edge · catch-up fees are a different thing with the same name

Confusingly, Ares also uses "catch-up fees" to mean something else entirely: fees "charged retroactively on limited partner commitments to a fund following the initial close", so that investors who join later are treated equally with those who came in first. On earnings calls Ares flags when a quarter contains no catch-up fees — because their presence or absence distorts quarter-on-quarter fee growth. If you can distinguish a catch-up provision (performance) from catch-up fees (late closers), you are already using the vocabulary correctly.

Part 3

The five numbers you must be able to defend

This is the technical heart of the job. Each one has a trap in it.

An investor will not ask "is this a good fund?". They will ask about a specific number, and the question is almost always testing whether you know the number's weakness. Here are the five, each explained from zero, each with the trap named.

3.1 Gross versus net

Gross return is what the investments earned. Net return is what the investor actually received, after management fees, carried interest, fund expenses and credit facility interest.

The gap is the cost of the manager. For SDL III Levered, Ares' 2023-vintage US senior direct lending fund, the gross IRR was 24.6% and the net IRR 17.3% — a spread of 730 basis points.

3.2 Levered versus unlevered — the same fund, two different answers

This is the one that genuinely surprises people. Ares offers many credit funds in two sleeves. The unlevered sleeve holds the loans outright. The levered sleeve borrows against them to buy more. Same manager, same loans, same vintage — very different returns.

The trap

Note the net MoIC is 1.1x for all four. Leverage has transformed the IRR while barely moving the multiple. That is because IRR is time-sensitive and MoIC is not — borrowing lets you deploy sooner and return cash faster, which flatters an annualised rate far more than a multiple. If someone quotes you a direct lending IRR without saying whether the sleeve is levered, the number is close to meaningless. This single distinction will put you ahead of most people in the building's first-year analyst cohort.

3.3 IRR versus MoIC

MoIC (multiple of invested capital) is simply money out divided by money in. 1.4x means you got £1.40 back for every £1. It ignores time entirely. IRR is the annualised rate of return, and it is extremely sensitive to when cash moves.

A fund returning 1.4x over three years has a far higher IRR than one returning 1.4x over eight years, despite identical multiples. An investor who cares about compounding asks for IRR; one who cares about total value asks for MoIC. Good managers publish both, and Ares does.

3.4 The subscription line — the most technical thing in this report

A subscription credit facility is a loan the fund takes, secured against investors' unfunded commitments. Instead of calling cash from investors to buy an asset, the fund borrows, buys, and calls the cash later.

This is administratively convenient and completely standard. It also mechanically inflates the fund's IRR, because IRR measures returns from the moment investors' money leaves their account — and the facility delays that moment.

Edge · Ares discloses this against itself, in its own 10-K

Ares states, in the footnotes to every fund performance table: "Early in the life of a fund, the gross fund-level MoICs would generally have been lower had such fund called capital from its limited partners instead of utilizing the credit facility." The same sentence appears for net MoIC, gross IRR and net IRR — sixteen times across the FY2025 10-K.

Why this matters: the Institutional Limited Partners Association (ILPA) — the body representing investors — has issued guidance since 2017 asking managers to report net IRR both with and without the facility, precisely because it distorts comparisons. ILPA notes there is still "not an agreed upon methodology" for doing so. A sophisticated investor will ask this question, and knowing that Ares already flags it is exactly the kind of preparation that separates a good answer from a flustered one.

3.5 Currency sleeves — the detail hiding in a footnote

SDL III accepts commitments in US dollars, sterling and yen. The headline 12.9% gross / 9.6% net is for dollar investors. For sterling investors the same fund returned 13.8% gross and 10.3% net.

Same fund, same assets, same manager, different answer depending on which currency you committed in — driven by hedging costs and interest rate differentials between currencies. If a UK pension scheme asks "what did SDL III return?", the correct response begins with a question: in which currency are you committed? That is the technical reflex this job builds.

Part 5

The question bank

Ten real questions, with the technical answer and the number behind it.

This is the closest thing to a transcript of the job. Each question is one a genuine institutional investor or wealth platform asks. The answers use Ares' disclosed figures.

1. "What is your non-accrual rate, and how do you define it?"
Why they ask · the single most important health metric in credit

A non-accrual is a loan where the borrower has stopped paying and the lender stops booking the interest as income. It is the first hard evidence of credit deterioration. ARCC's non-accruals at amortised cost were 1.8% at end-2025, 2.1% in Q1 2026 and 2.4% in Q2 2026 — three consecutive increases — against roughly 1.4% at fair value and a historical average near 3% since the financial crisis. The honest answer names the trend and the benchmark. Note "at cost" and "at fair value" give different numbers; always say which.

2. "How much of your income is PIK?"
Why they ask · non-cash income can disguise stress

PIK means payment-in-kind: instead of paying interest in cash, the borrower adds it to the loan balance. Sometimes that is a deliberate structure for a growing company; sometimes it is a struggling borrower being granted relief. Across the fifteen largest listed BDCs, PIK ran at about 8.2%–8.3% of total interest income through 2026, the lowest since Q4 2023. Concentration is uneven — Prospect Capital at 35%, FS KKR 27%, Blackstone Secured Lending 20%. Ares does not appear among the highest. A Lincoln International estimate put ~11% of private credit portfolios as carrying PIK, of which ~6% was distress-driven.

3. "Is that IRR with or without the subscription line?"
Why they ask · the sophisticated investor’s test question

See 3.4. Ares' own footnotes state returns "would generally have been lower" without the facility. ILPA has asked since 2017 for net IRR reported both ways, and acknowledges there is no agreed methodology. The right answer is not a number, it is transparency about method — which is exactly what ILPA asks for.

4. "What is the loan-to-value on the portfolio?"
Why they ask · it measures the cushion beneath the loan

LTV is the loan divided by the enterprise value of the borrower. At 40% LTV, the company's value would have to fall by 60% before the lender loses a penny — the equity owner absorbs losses first. Ares' software book runs at roughly 40% LTV with about 10% EBITDA growth and a weighted average remaining maturity near three years.

5. "Given AI, what is your software exposure?"
Why they ask · the defining market question of 2026

Software is about 6% of total AUM and under 8% of private credit AUM. Ares commissioned a nine-week review by a top-three consultancy: 86% of the software portfolio low risk, 13% medium, 1% high, putting medium-to-high at under 2% of US and European direct lending AUM and under 1% firm-wide. Supporting evidence from traded markets: loans to core operational software are down ~2% year-to-date while content-generation and productivity software loans are down ~24% and trade below 65.

6. "Why did you pro-rate the tender, and will it happen again?"
Why they ask · they could not get their money out

ASIF received repurchase requests of 11.6% of shares in Q1 2026 against a 5% cap — 43.1% of each request was honoured, paying out $523.5m — then 14.4% in Q2. The context that matters: requests from the US private wealth channel were only 2.4% of shares, down more than 35% quarter-on-quarter, and nearly half of all requests came from smaller non-US institutions and family offices representing under 1% of the fund's 20,000+ shareholders. Management's stress case: the full 5% cap every quarter for a year with zero inflows would cost about 1% of fee-paying AUM.

7. "What happens to the fee after the investment period?"
Why they ask · total cost of ownership over the fund’s life

It usually falls twice. An opportunistic credit fund charges 1.50% during the investment period, then steps down to 1.00%–1.25%, and the base changes from committed capital to invested capital or the cost of remaining unrealised investments. For corporate private equity the step-down is to 0.75%–1.00%, triggered by the earlier of the investment period ending or a successor fund activating.

8. "Who else is in the fund, and how concentrated is it?"
Why they ask · co-investor quality and exit risk

An investor wants to know they are not the only one, and that no single holder could force a liquidation. Ares raised 2025 commitments from over 540 institutional investors, 235 of them new, across 190+ vehicles, with 2,850+ direct institutional relationships. This is also where alignment lands: Ares and its employees have over $7.2bn committed, of which $3.6bn is employee money — more than the firm's own $3.4bn.

9. "How do you value assets nobody trades?"
Why they ask · 92% of SDL III’s value is unrealised

This is the number one trust driver in LP surveys — 39% named clear explanation of valuation methods and assumptions, more than any other factor. For private loans, valuation typically rests on discounted cash flow and comparable market yields, reviewed by independent third parties and the fund's auditors. You would not perform the valuation; you would have to explain it — which is why this role teaches you how a mark is actually built.

10. "What did you learn from First Brands and Tricolor?"
Why they ask · testing whether you deflect

Both 2025 collapses were fraud, not credit cycle: Tricolor pledged the same auto-loan collateral to multiple lenders; First Brands collapsed with $11.6bn of liabilities amid allegations of fabricated receivables and double-pledged inventory. Ares' own 10-K concedes the industry faces "increased negative publicity... following certain high profile defaults and bankruptcies" while noting Ares "has not been involved in those particular defaults." The good answer moves to process — collateral verification, lender documentation, being lead or sole lender so you control the terms.

Part 6

Inside a DDQ

The document that wins or loses mandates before anyone meets anyone.

A DDQ — due diligence questionnaire — is a standardised document an investor sends before committing. An RFP — request for proposal — is a competitive tender, often run by an investment consultant on behalf of a pension scheme. Both are written, scored, and frequently decide the outcome before a single meeting happens.

Ares has a dedicated RFP & Diligence team sitting under the COO of Global Client Solutions. At most firms this work is done badly, late, by a salesperson. Building a standing team for it is a deliberate choice — and as an intern it is one of the places you would most plausibly do real work, because the questions are factual and the sources are internal.

Edge · why the DDQ is the best technical teacher in the building

A DDQ forces you to source every claim. You cannot write "strong performance" — you must write the net IRR, name the vintage, state the sleeve and footnote the methodology. Working on DDQs is how client-facing people in this industry actually learn the technical material, far more than from pitch decks. If you are asked in interview what you would want to work on, naming the DDQ and RFP process is a genuinely sophisticated answer, and almost nobody gives it.

Waterfall, since it appears above. The order in which fund profits are split. European-style: the manager earns carried interest only after investors get back all their capital plus a preferred return. American-style: carry is paid deal by deal, as each investment is realised. European is more investor-friendly, and Ares' credit funds predominantly use it — the 10-K labels ASOF, Pathfinder and other funds "European".
Part 7

The semi-liquid machine

How a private-credit fund gives individual investors monthly access to illiquid loans.

The wealth channel runs on a structure most people — including most finance students — cannot explain. It is the engineering problem at the centre of the industry's growth, and it is why 2026 has been difficult.

The problem: the underlying assets are loans that take years to repay. The investors are individuals who expect to get money back on request. Those two facts are incompatible, and the semi-liquid structure is the compromise.

The technical point worth internalising

A semi-liquid fund is a liquidity transformation engine. It converts assets that take 3–7 years to repay into a product offering quarterly partial liquidity, and it manages the mismatch with a cap. Every argument about private credit and retail investors — including the questions from the House Financial Services Committee in April 2026 — is ultimately an argument about whether that transformation is honestly explained. Being the person who explains it honestly is the job.

Part 10

Your 1,000-character answer

"What do you hope to learn whilst interning at Ares?"

The strategy for this specific question

A 1,000-character limit is roughly 170–180 words — tight. That rules out listing four things. The winning structure is: one technical thing you want to master, one commercial thing you want to understand, and one sentence on why Ares specifically.

The common failure is naming skills ("communication, teamwork, commercial awareness"). Name a specific technical capability instead — it proves you already know what the work involves.

Draft — 938 characters including spaces

Two things, mainly.

First, how to explain a private credit return accurately. Reading Ares' fund disclosures, SDL III's levered sleeve shows a 24.6% gross and 17.3% net IRR, but the net MoIC is only 1.1x, so most of that is leverage and deployment timing. Sterling-committed investors see different figures again. I want to get to the point where I can explain that to someone without oversimplifying it or hiding behind it. Working on DDQs would be the fastest way, because they force you to source every claim.

Second, how investors actually decide. With roughly 79% of institutional fundraising coming from existing investors, retention isn't a service function at Ares, it's the growth engine. I want to understand what makes someone commit to a second product.

I'd also rather learn this in a hard year. Explaining a pro-rated tender to a worried client is harder than explaining a good quarter, and I suspect it teaches you more.

Why this works

  • Opens with a real number from a real fund — proves you read filings, not the website.
  • The MoIC-versus-IRR point is genuinely technical and almost nobody will make it.
  • The currency sleeve detail is a footnote-level find.
  • Links learning to the commercial model (79%), not to personal development.
  • Naming the DDQ is the sophisticated touch — see Part 6. Almost nobody says it.
  • The closing line shows you know what 2026 is actually like and are choosing it.
  • Says "learn", not "bring" — answers the question asked.

What to avoid

  • "I hope to learn from industry-leading professionals" — says nothing.
  • Listing more than two or three learning goals; the limit punishes breadth.
  • Technical terms you cannot explain if asked — every number here you must be able to unpack.
  • Claiming Ares is unaffected by the downturn. It pro-rated two tenders.
  • Repeating your "Why Ares?" answer. If that one used perpetual capital and the 93% figure, keep this one on different ground — as this draft does.
Before you submit

Check the counter on the actual form. Character limits vary in whether they count spaces and line breaks. This draft is 938 characters with spaces, leaving 62 to spare — but if the form counts differently, the easiest cut is the DDQ sentence, which costs 84 characters. Also be ready to be asked about SDL III in interview. If you put a fund in your application, you own it: know it is 2023 vintage, US senior direct lending, roughly 38% deployed, 92% unrealised.
