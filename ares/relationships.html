Part 1

Retention is three different things

And conflating them is the fastest way to sound like you've only read the marketing.

When Ares says 79% of its institutional fundraising came from existing investors, that single number is hiding three separate commercial motions. They need different skills, happen on different timescales, and are worth different amounts. If you can separate them in an interview, you are immediately ahead.

The definition to carry in your head

Retention is not "clients being happy." It is the share of this year's revenue that did not require a new relationship to be created. That is why it is a business model rather than a service standard — and why a firm will spend heavily on it.

Part 2

Why a kept client is worth more than a won one

The economics, with actual numbers.

In most businesses, retention is cheaper than acquisition. In asset management the gap is far wider than usual, for three structural reasons that are specific to how this industry works.

2.1 A commitment is an annuity, not a sale

If you sell someone a car, you book the revenue once. If an investor commits £100m to an Ares fund, Ares earns a management fee on it every year, for the life of the vehicle. At Ares' blended effective rate of roughly 1%, that commitment is worth around £1m a year — and in a perpetual vehicle there is no end date at which it stops.

2.2 The second product costs almost nothing to sell

Winning a new institutional investor is slow and expensive: a consultant screen, an RFP, operational due diligence on the firm's systems and controls, investment committee approval at the client, legal negotiation. That process can run nine to eighteen months and may involve dozens of people on both sides.

Selling a second product to that same investor skips nearly all of it. The operational diligence is done. The legal relationship exists. The trust is established. This is why the cross-sell is the single most profitable motion in the business — and why the 2025 Annual Shareholder Letter flags it as an explicit priority:

Edge · the sentence that defines the opportunity

"We ended 2025 with eight semi-liquid products in the wealth channel with at least one Ares product on more than 80 global platforms... Nearly half of our global distribution partners currently offer just one Ares product, highlighting the substantial opportunity to broaden our footprint within established partnerships and unlock incremental growth."
Ares 2025 Annual Shareholder Letter.

2.3 Retention is what makes the fee base defensible

Ares earns 93% of its management fees from perpetual capital or long-dated funds. But "perpetual" does not mean "trapped" — investors in perpetual wealth vehicles can request redemptions each quarter, and in 2026 they did. The permanence of the fee base is therefore not purely legal. It is partly behavioural, and the behaviour is managed by the client teams.

The connection most candidates miss

The 93% statistic is usually quoted as proof of structural resilience. It is really a joint product of structure and service. The fund documents stop money leaving quickly. Only the relationship stops it leaving at all. Saying that out loud connects the firm's headline resilience statistic directly to the job you are applying for.

Careful · the trap in the 79%

A high "percentage from existing investors" can mean loyalty or stagnation. The reason Ares' number is genuinely impressive is evidence #2: relationships went from 1,090 to 2,850 over the same window. Always pair the two. "Seventy-nine percent from existing investors, while the relationship count went from about eleven hundred to twenty-eight fifty" is a complete argument. The first half alone is not, and a sharp interviewer will push on exactly that.

Part 4

The machine that produces retention

Retention is not a culture. It is roughly 375 people and four named functions.

This is the part that answers "how is it shown in the firm". Ares has built specific, named, staffed infrastructure whose only purpose is keeping and deepening client relationships. Most of it is disclosed; some of it only appears in leadership biographies.

Edge · the detail that shows how seriously they take this

Ares has a dedicated RFP & Diligence team. At most firms, responding to a request for proposal or a due diligence questionnaire is something a salesperson does badly at 11pm. A DDQ can run to hundreds of questions on everything from valuation policy to cybersecurity to key-person provisions, and a weak answer can end a mandate before any human conversation happens. Building a standing team for it is an operational statement: Ares treats the written, procedural part of client service as a competitive weapon rather than an overhead. If you want one concrete thing to admire about how they run this function, it is this.

Part 5

The disclosure test

I read all five firms' 10-Ks side by side. Only one quantifies its client function.

There is no public league table of client service quality in private markets — I looked, and it does not exist. So I ran a different test, which is arguably better: what does each firm choose to tell its own shareholders about its client function in its annual report? A 10-K is audited, legally binding and tightly edited. What a firm puts in it is what it thinks is material.

Careful · state this precisely or it becomes an overclaim

The honest version is: "In the 10-K specifically, Ares quantifies its client-facing function in a way its four largest listed peers do not." It is not "Ares has the biggest client team" or "peers don't invest in client service" — neither follows.

Two things keep this fair. First, peers disclose elsewhere: Blackstone's Q1 2026 BCRED shareholder letter describes a private wealth platform of 345 professionals — materially larger than Ares' 175+ AWMS team, against roughly $324bn of private wealth AUM. Second, my method is a text search; a differently-worded disclosure could have been missed. Present it as "when I read the filings side by side, what struck me was..." rather than as an audited finding.

Edge · what the difference actually signals

Disclosure is a choice about what management wants to be judged on. Ares puts a retention percentage, a relationship count and a team headcount into a document its CEO signs — which means it has chosen to be held to them, and will have to explain if they deteriorate. Blue Owl, by contrast, mentions investor relationships mostly where firms are required to list what could go wrong. That is a real difference in posture, and it is the sort of observation you can only make by having opened all five documents.

5.1 Where competitors are genuinely stronger

A comparison that only flatters Ares is not credible. Two honest points:

Blackstone: scale and brand in wealth

345 private wealth professionals and roughly $324bn of private wealth AUM, versus Ares' 175+ and $108.4bn across publicly-traded and perpetual wealth vehicles. Blackstone is the better-known consumer brand and reaches more advisers. If pure wealth distribution scale is the measure, Ares is not the leader.

Blue Owl: individual-investor concentration

Roughly 40% of Blue Owl's $300bn+ comes from individuals, against Ares' 17% in wealth and listed vehicles. Blue Owl is further down the retail path. The flip side showed in 2026: Blue Owl's funds saw the heaviest redemption requests of the peer set (22% at OCIC, 41% at its tech fund, both capped at 5%).

The fair summary: Ares is not the biggest in wealth distribution, and does not claim to be. It is the most institutional of the large alternative managers — 76% of AUM from direct institutional relationships, 30% of that in bespoke SMAs — and it is the one that publishes the evidence.

Part 6

What investors actually say they want

Because "clients value trust" is an assumption until somebody surveys them.

The 2026 SS&C Intralinks LP survey asked institutional investors directly what would improve their relationships with managers, and what builds trust. The answers are strikingly operational — they are about communication quality, not performance.

Careful · sourcing on this part is weaker than the rest

These percentages come from secondary coverage and briefing summaries of the survey, not the primary report, which I could not obtain. Other figures circulating — such as a claim that 92% of institutional LPs say reporting quality influences re-ups — are attributed to Preqin by vendor blogs without a verifiable link, so I have left them out. Use the Intralinks figures as "one 2026 LP survey found..." rather than as hard fact, and do not cite the 92% figure at all.

6.1 The tension worth noticing

Two credible sources point in opposite directions, and holding both is a sign of genuine understanding rather than confusion.

Ares' 10-K says consolidation

"Several large institutional investors have announced a desire to consolidate their investments in a more limited number of managers. We expect that this will cause competition in our industry to intensify."

The LP survey says expansion

Nearly three-quarters of institutional LPs said they plan to increase the number of GP relationships they hold over the next twelve months.

Both can be true, and the reconciliation is the interesting bit. Large, sophisticated investors are concentrating their core allocations into fewer, deeper relationships — which favours scaled managers who can take an SMA mandate. Meanwhile the broader LP universe is still widening its roster, often into newer or more specialised strategies.

Either way the conclusion for Ares is the same: if investors concentrate, you must be one of the few they keep; if they expand, you must be one of the ones they add. Both outcomes are won by the client team, which is a reasonable one-sentence answer to "why does this function matter strategically?"

Part 7

Whether you would actually enjoy this

You said you value trust and relationships. Here is the honest version of what that gets you.

It would be easy to tell you this job is a perfect match for someone who likes building trust. Mostly it is. But the match is better than the cliché in some ways and worse in others, and knowing both is what lets you talk about it credibly rather than enthusiastically.

7.1 Where the fit is real

Relationships here are measured in decades

ARCC launched in 2004 and still contributes about 31% of Credit Group management fees. The European direct lending ladder runs ACE IV (2018) to ACE V (2020) to ACE VI (2022), each larger than the last. Some of the investors in ACE VI were in ACE IV. You would be joining relationships already a decade old and expected to last another. If you like compounding trust rather than closing transactions, that is the actual job.

Depth is structurally rewarded

30% of institutional AUM sits in SMAs — bespoke mandates built around one client's constraints. You cannot win one on a pitch; it requires knowing a client's regulatory position, risk appetite and internal politics well enough to design around them. That is a relationship product, and the firm has $139.6bn of them.

The firm is honest with its clients under pressure

When ASIF's tenders were pro-rated, Ares disclosed the uncomfortable detail: how much was requested, how much was honoured, which investor segments were leaving. It also commissioned a nine-week external review of AI risk in its software book and published the findings. If you value trust, the test is how a firm communicates when the news is bad — and the evidence here is reasonable.

Breadth, because the function is horizontal

Client Solutions and IR sit in the Operations Management Group, across all four investment groups rather than inside one. You would learn credit, real assets, secondaries and private equity because a client might buy any of them — rather than becoming the person who sells one sleeve.

7.2 Where it will be harder than you expect

Most of the "relationship" is actually accuracy

The romantic version of this job is dinners and conferences. The real version, especially in the first two years, is capital account statements, capital call notices, DDQ responses and quarterly reporting against deadlines set by fund documents that cannot move. Trust in this industry is built mostly through being reliably correct, not through being personable. The LP survey bears this out — the top trust driver was explaining valuation methods clearly, not rapport.

Your hardest relationships are internal

You will need time from portfolio managers who do not report to you, whose time is the scarcest resource in the building, to answer a client question. Getting that time repeatedly, without exhausting goodwill, is the job. If you enjoy relationships only when they are warm and reciprocal, the internal half of this will be a surprise.

You will deliver news you did not cause

Someone had to tell ASIF investors their redemption request had been cut to 43% of what they asked for. That person did not make the decision and could not change it. Private credit in 2026 means more of these conversations, not fewer.

The honest summary

If what you like about relationships is being trusted over a long period, this is one of the better seats in finance — the time horizons are genuinely long and the firm has built real infrastructure around them. If what you like is the social side of relationships, you will find the first two years more procedural than you imagined. The candidates who do well here tend to be the ones who find satisfaction in being the person who always has the right number.

7.3 How to say this without sounding like everyone else

"I'm drawn to the relationship side because of how long the relationships last here. Ares Capital launched in 2004 and still produces about a third of Credit Group management fees. The European direct lending series went from fund four to fund six with the same investors re-upping and committing more each time. And thirty percent of institutional assets sit in separately managed accounts, which you can't win from a pitch — they're built around one client's specific constraints. What I've also realised reading the LP surveys is that trust in this industry is mostly technical: the single biggest driver investors named was clear explanation of valuation methods. So it's less about rapport than I'd assumed, and more about being the person who can explain a number accurately. That suits how I like to work."

The five things to take from this

  1. Retention is three motions, not one — the re-up, the cross-sell, and simply not redeeming. The 79% covers the first two.
  2. Pair the 79% with the relationship count (1,090 → 2,850). Alone, the 79% could just as easily signal stagnation.
  3. Ares has roughly 375 disclosed client-facing people, plus product managers, IR professionals, a dedicated RFP & Diligence team and a standing Conferences & Events team.
  4. Of the five big listed managers, only Ares quantifies this function in its 10-K — headcount, relationship count and a retention percentage. The others mostly describe it qualitatively or in risk factors.
  5. Trust in this industry is technical. The top driver LPs named was clear explanation of valuation methods — which makes this a precision job before it is a people job.
