Why you would want it: seven honest reasons

  1. It converts straight into a job. Many internships end with "we'll be in touch". This one is designed to end with a desk.
  2. You sit in the middle of the markets. A broker hears what dozens of banks and funds are doing, all day. Few seats in finance see that much.

My view: an edge you can use Most finance students prepare for investment banking. Far fewer understand broking. So the bar for sounding informed here is lower, and you can clear it by a mile. That is my opinion, not a statistic, but it is why this pack is worth an hour.

Who this is not for

A good teacher tells you the downsides too. Be honest with yourself about these.

  • Early starts. You are in before the market opens.
  • A loud, fast floor. If you need quiet to think, it will be tiring.
  • Rejection, daily. Most calls do not become trades.
  • Variable pay. A quiet market or a lost client hits your income.
  • Social stamina. Relationships are built in person as well as on the phone.
  • Technology is changing the job. Simple trades are moving to screens. The brokers who thrive will be the ones who add something a screen cannot.
Quick self-check Do you enjoy talking to people more than working alone? Do you bounce back fast from a "no"? Do you like competing and being measured? Are you good with numbers at speed? Would people describe you as trustworthy with a secret? Four or five yeses means this role fits you.
Chapter 2

The role: what a broker actually does

You said you know nothing about the role. Perfect. We start from zero and build it up.

The job in one sentence

In plain English A broker finds two people who want opposite things, gets them to agree a price, and takes a small fee from each. That is the whole job. Everything else is how you get good at it.

Think of three jobs rolled into one:

  • A matchmaker. You know who is looking for what.
  • An auctioneer. You call out prices and nudge both sides towards a deal.
  • A news desk. You tell your clients what is going on in the market, without ever revealing anyone's secrets.

The language of the desk

Brokers speak in shorthand because speed matters. Here are the basics. Learn these and the floor will make sense on day one.

WordWhat it means
BidThe price someone is willing to pay to buy.
OfferThe price someone is willing to accept to sell.
The spreadThe gap between the bid and the offer. The broker's job is to close that gap.
SizeHow much they want to trade.
"Mine""I buy at your offer."
"Yours""I sell at your bid."
"Done"The trade is agreed. A broker's favourite word.
Working an orderQuietly looking for the other side of a client's trade.

A ten-second example A trader tells you: "I'll pay 20." That is a bid. Another says: "I'll sell at 22." That is an offer. The market is "20 at 22". You go back to the buyer: "Could you pay 21?" You go to the seller: "Would you take 21?" Both say yes. Done. You just earned two commissions by closing a gap of two.

What you would do as an intern

The advert confirms rotations across desks, workshops, off-site events, socials and a charity event. Beyond that, based on how internships like this usually run:

  • Shadow brokers. Sit beside them and watch how they handle clients.
  • Learn the products. One market at a time as you rotate.
  • Follow the news. Expect to be asked what is moving and why.
  • Meet people. Every broker you impress is a possible vote for you at the end.
What gets interns a desk offer You are not judged on knowing everything. You are judged on whether a desk wants you sitting with them every day for years. That means energy, curiosity, reliability, and being easy to like. Turn up early. Ask good questions. Remember names. Follow up on what you were told.

The career ladder

How to read this diagram Read left to right. The big jump is from the second box to the third. A trainee helps other people's clients. A broker has their own. Everything in the first couple of years is about earning that step.

What makes a great broker

  • Memory. Who wanted what, at what price, last Tuesday.
  • Speed with numbers. Quick mental arithmetic under pressure.
  • Listening. Hearing what a client did not quite say.
  • Discretion. You hold everybody's secrets. Leak one and you are finished.
  • Thick skin. "No" is not personal.
  • Honesty. Admit a mistake in seconds, not hours.
Chapter 3

The business model, properly

Now the fun part. We are going to open up the engine. A business model is just the answer to five questions. Who pays? For what? How are they charged? What does it cost to deliver? And what stops a rival taking it away?

The picture: a toll bridge

How to read this diagram Two sides need to reach each other. TP ICAP is the bridge between them. It charges a small toll each time someone crosses. It does not own what crosses, so it does not care whether the cargo is worth more or less tomorrow. It only cares how much traffic there is.

The five questions, answered

QuestionTP ICAP's answer
Who pays?Big institutions: banks, funds, energy firms. Usually both sides of a trade pay.
For what?Finding the other side, fast, anonymously, at a fair price. Plus price data.
How are they charged?A small commission per trade, which is about 92% of revenue. A subscription for data, which is about 8%.
What does it cost?Mostly people. About 63p of each £1 goes on front office costs. About 23p on support. About 15p is left as profit.
What protects it?The network, the relationships, the licences, and the data. We will take each in turn.

Four things that make this model clever

1. It needs very little capital

A bank needs billions set aside to absorb losses on loans and trades. A shop needs stock. TP ICAP holds neither. It has no warehouse and no trading book. So the profit it makes turns into cash it can actually spend.

The proof: over the last three years it turned an average of 113% of its earnings into free cash. That cash funded about £660 million of dividends and buybacks approved since 2023.

2. Its biggest cost shrinks when revenue shrinks

Brokers are paid largely out of what they bring in. When trading is slow, pay falls. The firm says front office costs are "variable with revenue". That is a built-in shock absorber.

3. Profit grows faster than revenue

Support costs, such as technology and offices, stay roughly fixed. So when revenue rises, most of the extra flows through to profit. This is called operating leverage.

How to read this diagram Three bars showing growth. Revenue grew 11%. Support costs barely moved. So profit grew 22%, twice as fast as revenue. The division's profit margin rose from 18.4% to 20.3%. This is the single best piece of evidence that the model is working.

4. It sells its own by-product

Every trade a broker arranges creates a price. In private markets, that price is valuable information that outsiders cannot see. TP ICAP collects those prices and sells them through Parameta Solutions.

The proof that this is real: inside the group, Parameta pays the broking divisions for the data they produce. In 2025 it paid Global Broking £25 million and Energy & Commodities £3 million. A broker doing their normal job is creating a second product without lifting a finger.

The four walls that protect it

Investors call these moats, like the water around a castle.

MoatWhy a rival cannot easily copy it
The networkA broker is only useful if it can find the other side. The biggest network finds it most often. A new broker with five clients cannot match anybody.
RelationshipsA trader trusts a broker they have dealt with for years. Trust cannot be bought off the shelf. Chapter 4 covers this.
LicencesRunning a trading venue needs approval from regulators in every major country. That takes years and costs a great deal.
DataOnly someone sitting in the middle of the trades can see the prices. A pure data company cannot create them.

What makes it different from its direct rivals

All three big brokers do the same core job. The difference is the bet each has made about the future. This table is my reading of their public strategies.

FirmIts betWhat it has done about it
TP ICAP"Broking alone is too bumpy. Add steadier income and new kinds of client."Bought Liquidnet to reach investors. Built Parameta to sell data. Formed a bond trading business part-owned by nine banks.
BGC"Go electronic, get bigger fast, and take on the exchanges."Built the Fenics electronic platforms. Launched FMX, its own futures exchange. Bought a large energy broker in 2025.
Tradition"Stay a focused broker and do it well."No big data or investor arm. Grew revenue 11% in 2025 from broking itself.

Why did TP ICAP choose its bet?

  1. Its old customers were shrinking. After the 2008 crisis, banks were forced to take less risk. Fewer bank traders means fewer customers for an interdealer broker.
  2. Broking revenue swings with the market. A quiet year hurts. Subscriptions do not swing.
  3. Stock markets pay more for steady income. A pound of predictable data profit is valued more highly than a pound of bumpy commission. This is why the firm explored listing Parameta separately in the US.
The honest weak spots The model is not perfect. It still depends on trading volumes. The brokers keep a large share of what they earn, so they capture much of the value. And electronic platforms threaten to cut out the middleman on simple trades. A strong candidate can name these calmly.

Steal these ideas for your own business

You said you want to apply this, not just learn it. Here are four lessons that work at any size.

1. Be the bridgeCan you sit between two groups who need each other, and charge for the introduction? You carry no stock and little risk.
2. Make costs move with salesPay commission rather than fixed wages where you can. Bad months then hurt far less.
3. Sell your by-productWhat does your business create by accident? Data, contacts, know-how? Someone may pay for it.
4. Win one small network firstA marketplace is useless when thin. Become the busiest place in one tiny niche, then widen.
Chapter 4

Client relationships: the real product

Here is the most important idea in this whole pack. At TP ICAP, the relationship is the product. The firm has no factory and no patent. What it owns is thousands of traders who pick up the phone to its brokers first.

Proof: the accountants put a price on it

A fact almost nobody will mention TP ICAP's 2025 balance sheet carries £366 million of "customer relationships" as an asset. It came mainly from buying ICAP and Liquidnet. The accounts spread the cost of those relationships over 2 to 20 years. In plain English: when TP ICAP bought those firms, a large part of what it paid for was the clients' loyalty, and it expects some of that loyalty to last two decades.

Three levels of relationship

How to read this diagram Read from the bottom up. The foundation is one human trusting another. Above that, whole teams deal with each other. At the top, the two companies are tied together by contracts and technology. The lower levels are warm but fragile. The top level is cold but sticky. A strong client relationship has all three.

Level 1: what a trader wants from a broker

This is general knowledge of how the market works. Think of it as five currencies a broker pays the client in.

CurrencyWhat it means in practice
Discretion"Nobody will ever learn from you what I am trying to do." The number one rule.
Information"Tell me what is happening out there." Useful colour, without names.
Speed"When I need it done, you get it done now."
Straightness"You give me the real price. If you make a mistake, you tell me at once."
Results when it is hard"Anyone can find a buyer on a calm day. You find one on the bad day."

And one more that matters more than people admit: being pleasant to deal with. A trader may speak to their broker dozens of times a day. Nobody wants that to be a chore.

How a relationship grows

How to read this diagram Start at the bottom rung and climb. Each step up means more trust and more business. The top rung, first call, is the goal: when a trader has something big, yours is the number they dial. A junior broker's first years are spent climbing this ladder with one client after another.

Why the firm treats relationships as both treasure and risk

Here is the tension at the heart of every broking firm. The relationship lives mostly at Level 1, between two people. So if the broker leaves, the client may follow.

You can see the firm managing this risk in its own accounts:

  • It pays brokers up front to stay. The accounts describe "broker contract payments": money paid in advance under employment contracts, which must be repaid if the broker leaves early.
  • It pays part of bonuses in shares. Eligible brokers receive some of their bonus through a share plan, which ties them to the firm's future.
  • It has felt the pain. The firm said its oil business suffered "significant competitor disruption" at the end of 2024. It then spent heavily on hiring and keeping energy brokers. That division's profit fell from £27 million to £12 million in the first half of 2026.

How TP ICAP makes relationships stickier

The smart move is to add Level 3 glue, so the client is tied to the firm and not only to one person. This is my reading of the strategy.

GlueHow it works
Fusion on the client's deskOnce a trader uses TP ICAP's screen every day, switching brokers means changing their tools too.
Data subscriptionsThe same bank that trades through the brokers also pays Parameta for prices. Two ties instead of one.
Clients as co-ownersNine banks own 30% of TP ICAP's new credit trading business. Owners want their own venue to succeed.
Two brandsA bank can deal with a Tullett Prebon desk and an ICAP desk. Two separate relationship networks under one roof.
BreadthA client can use TP ICAP for rates, currencies, credit, equities, energy and data. Each extra product deepens the tie.

The same idea in the other divisions

  • Liquidnet runs a community. More than 1,100 investment firms are members. The more members, the more likely each one finds a match. Its sales traders look after members, and the First Mate AI tool helps them spot matches.
  • Parameta runs on renewals. Its income is 98% subscriptions. The firm reported higher retention rates in 2026 and its largest ever contract with a new US investor client.

The rules that keep relationships clean

Because brokers hold secrets and deal with the same people for years, the job is tightly regulated. In general across the industry:

  • Phone lines and chats are recorded.
  • Client information is confidential. You never tell one client what another is doing.
  • Gifts and entertainment are logged and limited.
  • Trades must be genuine. Nothing is arranged just to generate a fee.

This is why Accountability is the first of the firm's three values. Integrity is not a poster on the wall. It is what the whole product rests on.

What this means for you

The real test in your video and your internship The reviewer is silently asking one question: "Would a client enjoy speaking to this person fifty times a day?" So be warm. Be clear. Be brief. Sound like someone people would trust with a secret. That matters more than any statistic you quote.
Steal this for your own business Draw the ladder for your own customers. Who is on rung 2? Who is on rung 4? What one thing would move each customer up a rung? And ask: if my best salesperson left tomorrow, would the customers stay? If not, add some Level 3 glue.
