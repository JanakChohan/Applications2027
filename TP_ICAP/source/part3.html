Chapter 2

The toolkit: seven ideas all four desks share

Before the four markets, a small toolkit. Learn these seven ideas once and they work everywhere.

1. The basis point

Interest rates move in tiny steps. Saying "nought point nought one per cent" is slow. So the market uses the basis point. People write it "bp" and say "bip".

Memorise this one line 1 basis point = 0.01%. So 100 basis points = 1%. A rate going from 4.00% to 4.25% has risen 25 basis points.

2. Bid, offer and mid

  • Bid: the price a buyer will pay.
  • Offer: the price a seller will accept.
  • Mid: halfway between. Bid 20, offer 22, mid 21.

3. Notional

The headline size of a contract. A "£100 million swap" has a notional of £100 million. Nobody hands over £100 million. It is just the number the payments are worked out from.

4. Cash versus derivative

  • Cash: you buy the real thing. A share. A bond. Some dollars.
  • Derivative: you sign a contract whose value follows the real thing, without owning it.

5. The four kinds of derivative

TypeIn plain EnglishEveryday comparison
ForwardAgree a price today for a deal that happens on a future date. Private, between two parties.Ordering a car today at today's price, for delivery in three months.
FutureThe same idea, but standardised and traded on an exchange.The same order, on a standard form, through a central marketplace.
SwapTwo parties exchange one stream of payments for another, over months or years.You pay my fixed phone bill. I pay your pay-as-you-go bill.
OptionPay a fee now for the right, but not the duty, to buy or sell later at a set price.A deposit that holds a house at an agreed price. You can walk away.

6. Hedging versus speculating

  • Hedging: doing a trade to remove a risk you already have. Skyline Air fixing its fuel cost.
  • Speculating: doing a trade to profit from a view. A hedge fund betting rates will fall.

Both create trades. A broker serves both and takes neither side.

7. Volatility

How much prices are jumping around. Calm market, low volatility. Wild market, high volatility. People shorten it to "vol". It matters twice over: it sets the price of options, and it decides how busy a broking desk is.

Chapter 3

Rates: the price of money over time

This is the big one. Nearly half of Global Broking. If you understand Rates, the other three desks become much easier, because they are all built on top of it.

What "rates" means

"Rates" is short for interest rates. An interest rate is the price of borrowing money. Borrow £100 for a year at 5%, pay back £105.

Three facts turn that simple idea into a giant market:

  1. There is not one interest rate. There are many. Borrowing for 2 years costs a different rate from borrowing for 10 or 30.
  2. They change every second, as people change their minds about inflation and what the central bank will do.
  3. Everyone is exposed. Every mortgage, pension, loan and government budget depends on them.

Where rates come from

The central bank sets the rate for very short-term money. In the UK that is the Bank of England. Its rate was raised to 4% in November 2026 according to news reports. In the US it is the Federal Reserve, often called "the Fed".

The market then decides every longer rate, by guessing where the central bank will go next. Draw all those rates on one chart and you get the most important picture in finance.

How to read this diagram Left to right is how long you borrow for. Up is a higher interest rate. The solid blue line is the usual shape: lenders want more reward for tying money up longer. The dashed grey line is an inverted curve, where short-term borrowing costs more than long-term. That usually means the market expects rates to be cut, and it has often appeared before recessions.

The two main products

Product 1: government bonds

A bond is a loan you can sell on to someone else. A government borrows £100 from you, pays you interest every year, and repays the £100 at the end. In between, you can sell that bond to anyone, at whatever price the market offers.

Here comes the rule that confuses everybody. Let's kill it properly.

How to read this diagram, and why it is true A seesaw. One end goes up, the other must go down. Why? Say you own a bond paying £4 a year. Then rates rise, and brand new bonds pay £5 a year. Nobody will pay full price for your £4 bond any more. Its price must drop until it is as good a deal as the new one. Rates up, old bond prices down. Always.

Yield just means the return you earn on a bond at today's price. Bond traders talk in yields, not prices.

Product 2: interest rate swaps

This is TP ICAP's heartland. A swap is an agreement to exchange interest payments.

How to read this diagram Two sides, one contract in the middle. Side A pays the same 4% every year. Side B pays whatever the short-term rate turns out to be. If rates rise above 4%, Side A wins, because it locked in a lower cost. If rates fall, Side B wins. The "swap rate" is the fixed rate the market will agree to today. That number is what Rates brokers shout all day.

The floating rates have names. You will hear them constantly: SONIA for pounds, SOFR for dollars, €STR for euros. They replaced an older benchmark called LIBOR.

The rest of the Rates family

TP ICAP's website lists these Rates products: interest rate swaps, interest rate options, government bonds, repurchase agreements, basis swaps and inflation products.

  • Interest rate options: the right to lock in a rate later. Insurance against rates moving.
  • Inflation products: swaps where one side pays whatever inflation turns out to be. Pension funds use these heavily.
  • Basis swaps: swapping one floating rate for another.
  • Repurchase agreements, or "repos": borrowing cash overnight and handing over a bond as security.

Who trades Rates, and why

WhoWhy they need it

Pension funds and insurersThey owe pensions for decades. They need assets that behave the same way as those promises.

Hedge fundsTo bet on what central banks will do next.

What broking Rates looks like day to day

  • Desks are split by currency and product. A sterling swaps desk. A euro swaps desk. An inflation desk. An options desk.
  • Prices are quoted as rates. "Ten-year sterling, 4.20 bid, 4.21 offered."
  • The diary rules the day. Central bank meetings, inflation figures and US jobs numbers cause sudden bursts. In between it can be quiet.
  • Many trades are "curve trades". A client bets on the gap between two points on the yield curve. The most famous is "2s10s": the 10-year rate minus the 2-year rate.
  • After the trade, most swaps are handed to a clearing house, which stands between the two banks so neither has to worry about the other failing.
Which bit is too awkward for a screen? A standard 10-year swap in a normal size trades happily on a screen. But a very large trade, a trade with odd dates, an inflation swap, or a package of several swaps at once needs someone to quietly find the other side. That is the Rates broker's job.

Chapter 4

FX and Money Markets: the price of one money in another

FX stands for foreign exchange. You have already traded FX. Every time you changed pounds into euros for a holiday, that was an FX trade. Now imagine doing it in billions.

How a currency price works

Currencies come in pairs. One price tells you how much of the second currency buys one unit of the first.

Reading a currency price GBP/USD at 1.3000 means one pound buys 1.30 dollars. If it goes to 1.3100, the pound got stronger. If it drops to 1.2900, the pound got weaker.

London is the world capital of this market. About 38% of all currency trading on earth happens in the UK, worth around $4.7 trillion a day.

The FX products, from simple to clever

ProductWhat it isWho mostly trades it
SpotSwap currencies now. In practice it settles two working days later.Mostly on electronic screens. Little voice broking.
ForwardAgree today's rate for an exchange on a future date.Companies and funds locking in a rate.
FX swapSwap currencies today and agree to swap them back later. Really a way of borrowing one currency using another.Banks managing their cash. The most traded FX product in the world.
Non-deliverable forward (NDF)A forward for a currency you cannot freely move in and out of a country. Only the profit or loss is paid, in dollars.Anyone dealing with emerging markets, such as India, Korea or Brazil.
FX optionThe right, not the duty, to exchange at a set rate later.Hedgers who want protection but also want to keep the upside.

TP ICAP's website lists its FX products as: FX options, forward FX, non-deliverable forwards and emerging market interest rate swaps, across major and emerging market currencies. Notice what is missing: spot. That tells you where the broker adds value.

Which bit is too awkward for a screen? Spot trading in big currencies is almost entirely electronic. So FX brokers concentrate on forwards, emerging market currencies and options, where prices are harder to find and local knowledge counts.

The idea that links FX to Rates

Here is a lovely piece of logic. A forward rate is not a forecast. It is today's rate adjusted for the difference in interest rates between the two countries.

How to read this diagram, and why it is true Left box is today. Right box is the price for a deal one year from now. The middle box is the reason they differ. If you hold dollars for a year you earn 1% more interest than someone holding pounds. The forward price has to cancel that advantage out, or there would be free money. So the forward sits about 1% away from today's price. This is why FX and interest rates are welded together.

What "Money Markets" means

Money markets are where institutions borrow and lend cash for short periods: overnight, a week, three months, up to a year.

Every bank ends each day with too much cash or too little. The money market is where they fix that. It is the plumbing of the financial system. It sits beside FX because an FX swap is, underneath, a short-term loan in another currency.

What broking FX looks like day to day

  • It never really closes. Trading passes from Asia to London to New York, five days a week.
  • Desks are split by region and product. Major currency forwards. Emerging markets. Options.
  • News moves it instantly. A central bank surprise, an election, a war.
  • Prices are quoted in pips. For forwards, brokers quote "points": how many pips the forward is away from spot.
  • The 4pm London "fix" is a daily reference price many funds use. Activity bunches around it.

Chapter 5

Equities: not what you think it is

You said: "I know equities is stocks. You buy them on the market. But what does Global Broking mean in equities?" Brilliant question. It is exactly the right thing to be confused about.

The answer

In plain English Buying and selling ordinary shares happens on public exchanges, by computer, in fractions of a second. Nobody needs a voice broker for that. So Global Broking's equities desks mostly do something else: equity derivatives. These are contracts whose value follows shares or stock indices, rather than the shares themselves.

The firm said its 2025 equities growth was "primarily notable in derivative products", driven by clients wanting to "hedge and risk manage portfolios". Its regulated UK venue lists equity swaps, options and forwards.

So where do big blocks of actual shares get traded? That is Liquidnet, a different division. Do not mix the two up.

First, the basics

  • A share is a slice of ownership in a company. "Equity" and "stock" mean the same.
  • An index is a basket of shares used as a scoreboard. The FTSE 100 tracks the 100 biggest companies listed in London. The S&P 500 tracks 500 large US companies.

The main product: options

An option is a contract that gives you a right. There are two kinds.

TypeWhat it gives youWhen you would want it
CallThe right to buy at a set price.You think the price will rise.
PutThe right to sell at a set price.You fear the price will fall. This is insurance.

Three words describe every option:

  • Strike: the set price you can buy or sell at.
  • Expiry: the date the right runs out.
  • Premium: what you pay for the option up front.
How to read this diagram Left to right is where the share price ends up. Above the middle line is profit, below is loss. If the share finishes below 100, your right to buy at 100 is worthless, and you lose the 5 you paid. That is the flat part. Above 100 you start winning it back. At 105, the orange dot, you break even: strike plus premium. Beyond that, the profit has no ceiling. Small fixed loss, large possible gain. That shape is why options exist.

Why volatility is everything here

What should an option cost? It depends mostly on how wild the share is expected to be. A calm share rarely moves far, so the option is cheap. A wild share might do anything, so the option is expensive.

So option traders do not really trade prices. They trade volatility. An equity derivatives broker will quote an option "in vol", as a percentage. There is even a famous index of expected stock market volatility called the VIX, nicknamed the fear gauge.

Two words you will hear

  • Delta: how much the option's price moves when the share moves by 1. A delta of 0.5 means the option moves about half as much as the share.
  • Vega: how much the option's price moves when volatility changes.

The rest of the family

  • Equity swaps: one side receives the full return of a share or index without owning it, and pays a financing cost in exchange.
  • Dividend products: contracts on how much companies will pay out in dividends.
  • Strategies: options combined in packages. A straddle is a call and a put together: a bet that the price moves a lot in either direction.
Which bit is too awkward for a screen? A small option trade goes straight onto an exchange screen. But a huge one, or a package of three or four options traded together, would move the market if shown openly. A broker privately finds the other side and agrees one price for the whole package. The finished trade is then reported to the exchange. That negotiation is the equity derivatives broker's job.

What broking Equities looks like day to day

  • Desks are split by product and region. Index options. Single-stock options. Swaps. Europe, US, Asia.
  • Clients are banks' derivatives desks, market-making firms and hedge funds.
  • Busy times: company results seasons, big falls in the market, and monthly expiry days.
  • You talk about where an index is, what volatility is doing, and which strategies clients are asking for.

Chapter 6

Credit: the price of trust

Credit is the market for lending to companies, and for the risk that they do not pay it back. If Rates is about the price of safe borrowing, Credit is about the extra you charge for risky borrowing.

The core idea: the credit spread

When the UK government borrows, lenders are confident of being repaid. When Skyline Air borrows, they are less sure. So Skyline has to pay more. That extra is the credit spread.

How to read this diagram The whole bar is the 6% interest a company pays on its bond. The blue part is what even a safe borrower pays. The orange part is the reward lenders demand for the risk. Credit traders care almost entirely about the orange part. When people trust a company more, the orange part shrinks. That is called spreads "tightening". When they worry, it grows. That is "widening".

The two main products

Product 1: corporate bonds

The same idea as a government bond, but issued by a company. You lend, you receive interest, you get your money back at the end, unless the company fails.

Credit ratings grade how safe a borrower is. Agencies such as Moody's and S&P give the grades.

GroupGradesWhat it means
Investment gradeAAA down to BBBConsidered safe. Lower spread.
High yieldBB and belowRiskier. Higher spread. Sometimes called "junk".

Product 2: credit default swaps

A credit default swap, or CDS, is insurance against a company failing to repay. You pay a yearly fee. If the company defaults, the seller of the insurance covers your loss.

The fee is quoted in basis points per year. "Skyline CDS at 150" means insuring £10 million of Skyline's debt costs 1.5% of £10 million, which is £150,000 a year.

There are also CDS indices: one contract that insures a whole basket of companies. TP ICAP's regulated UK venue lists CDS indices plus CDS on single companies and on governments.

Why Credit needs humans more than any other desk

Here is something that surprises people. A company has one share but may have dozens of different bonds, each with its own interest rate and end date. Most of those bonds do not trade on any given day.

Which bit is too awkward for a screen? Nearly all of it. With thousands of bonds and few of them trading daily, there is often no price on a screen at all. Someone has to know who owns the bond, who wants it, and what it is worth. Credit is the most relationship-driven of the four desks.

This is also why TP ICAP is pushing into credit. It bought Neptune Networks, which gathers data from banks on which bonds they want to buy or sell. In June 2026 it launched RealQ, a platform connecting those banks with investors.

What broking Credit looks like day to day

  • Desks are split by type of borrower. Investment grade. High yield. Banks and insurers. Emerging markets. CDS.
  • News is company by company. Results, a rating change, a takeover, a profit warning.
  • New bonds matter. When a company sells a new bond, trading in its older bonds picks up.
  • The typical question: "Where can I find a buyer for 10 million of this bond?"

Chapter 7

How everything links together

You asked the best question of all: how does it all connect? Here is the map. Once you see it, the four desks stop being four subjects and become one.

How to read this diagram Start at the dark box on top. That is the safe interest rate, the foundation. The three arrows show that each other market starts from that number and adds something. FX compares two of them. Credit adds a spread. Equities adds a bigger reward for bigger risk. Move the top box, and all three below it move.

The five links, one at a time

  1. Rates to FX. If UK rates rise compared with US rates, pounds become more rewarding to hold. Money flows in. The pound tends to rise.
  2. Rates to Credit. A company's borrowing cost is the safe rate plus its spread. If the safe rate rises, every company's cost rises with it.
  3. Rates to Equities. A share is worth the profits it will earn in future. Higher rates make future money worth less today. So higher rates tend to push share prices down.
  4. Credit to Equities. They are two views of the same company. Lenders get paid first. Shareholders get what is left. When a company is in trouble, its spread widens and its share price falls.
  5. Fear links everything. On a scared day, credit spreads widen, shares fall, volatility jumps, and money runs to safe currencies like the dollar.

Whichever desk you land on, the job underneath is the same. You talk to clients all day. You find the other side. You get told "no" a lot. You are measured on revenue. The product changes. The job does not.

Chapter 9

What you need to know, and what they might ask

Nobody expects an intern to be an expert. They expect you to know the basics cold, be quick with numbers, and be curious. Here is exactly where the bar sits.

The baseline: twelve things to know cold

  1. A basis point is 0.01%.
  2. Bid is what a buyer pays. Offer is what a seller accepts.
  3. When yields rise, bond prices fall.
  4. A swap exchanges fixed interest payments for floating ones.
  5. The yield curve shows the interest rate for each length of loan.
  6. GBP/USD at 1.30 means one pound buys 1.30 dollars.
  7. A forward fixes today's rate for a future date. Its price reflects the interest rate gap.
  8. A call is the right to buy. A put is the right to sell.
  9. Options get more expensive when volatility rises.
  10. A credit spread is the extra yield a risky borrower pays over a safe one.
  11. A CDS is insurance against a borrower defaulting.
  12. A broker arranges trades and takes no risk. A trader takes the risk.

Questions they might ask, with answers

Anonymous candidate reports for past TP ICAP broker interviews mention "why brokerage", quick mental maths, the difference between a trader and a broker, voice versus electronic broking, and what technology the firm uses. Some later stages included a numerical test, a trading simulation and a group presentation. Treat that as a guide. These are more likely in a manager interview than in your three-question video.

QuestionA good short answer
What is the difference between a broker and a trader?"A trader buys and sells with their firm's money and carries the risk. A broker sits between traders, finds the other side, and earns commission without taking risk."
Voice versus electronic broking?"Electronic suits simple, frequent trades on a screen. Voice suits large or complex trades that need negotiation. TP ICAP combines them in a hybrid model through its Fusion platform."
What technology does the firm use?"Fusion, its electronic platform across asset classes. It also has AI tools such as First Mate, and RealQ in credit."
What happens to bond prices when interest rates rise?"They fall. New bonds pay more, so older bonds paying less must drop in price to stay attractive."
What is an interest rate swap? Who uses one?"Two parties exchange fixed interest payments for floating ones. A company with a floating-rate loan might use one to fix its costs."
What is the yield curve?"A line showing the interest rate for each length of borrowing. Normally it slopes up. When it inverts, the market expects rates to fall."
Why is a forward FX rate different from spot?"Because of the gap between the two countries' interest rates. It is not a forecast."
What is the difference between a call and a put?"A call is the right to buy at a set price. A put is the right to sell. A put works like insurance."
What is a credit spread?"The extra yield a company pays over a safe government bond, for the risk it might not repay."
If equities trade on an exchange, why does Global Broking have an equities desk?"Because it mainly brokers equity derivatives. Large or multi-part option trades are negotiated privately, then reported to the exchange."
