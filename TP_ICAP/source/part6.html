What is a broker?

Part 6. The word, the job, and how a TP ICAP broker differs from an insurance broker, a stockbroker, a salesperson and a relationship manager

"Broker" is one of the most confusing words in business. An insurance broker, a mortgage broker, a stockbroker and a TP ICAP broker all share the name, and do very different jobs. Today we untangle it, so that when someone asks "what is a broker?" you answer in one breath, and when they ask "why broking?" you know exactly what you are choosing.

The whole pack in one sentence A broker arranges a deal between two other parties and is paid a fee for it, without owning the thing being traded. Every kind of broker fits that sentence. What changes is who the customers are, whose side the broker is on, and what gets traded.

Chapter 1

What a broker is

Forget finance for a minute. A broker is one of the oldest jobs there is. Wherever two people could do a deal but cannot easily find or trust each other, someone steps into the middle.

How to read this diagram Three boxes. The two white boxes are the people doing the actual deal. The dark box is the broker. The broker is not one of the two parties. When the deal is done, the thing traded passes between the white boxes. The broker keeps only the fee.

The three tests

If you are ever unsure whether someone is really a broker, ask three questions.

Is it somebody else's deal? A broker arranges a deal between two other parties. A shop selling its own stock is not a broker.

Are they paid for arranging, not for owning? A broker earns a fee or commission when the deal happens. They do not profit from the thing going up in value.

Do they avoid taking the risk? If the price moves tomorrow, the broker neither gains nor loses. The two parties carry that.

Three yeses, and you are looking at a broker.

What the broker actually adds

Why would anyone pay a middleman? Because the broker brings four things the two parties lack.

The broker bringsIn plain English
Knowledge of whoThe broker knows who is out there. You do not have to search.
Knowledge of priceThe broker sees many deals, so knows what is fair.
TrustBoth sides know the broker. They may not know each other.
NegotiationThe broker carries messages back and forth until both say yes.

The five questions that separate one broker from another

Every kind of broker passes the three tests. They differ on these five questions. Keep them in mind for the next chapter.

  1. Who are the customers? Ordinary people, or giant institutions?
  2. Whose side is the broker on? The buyer's, the seller's, or neither?
  3. What gets arranged? A policy, a loan, shares, a swap?
  4. Who pays the broker? One side, or both?
  5. Does the broker give advice? Or only find and connect?
Where the word comes from "Broker" has been an English word for more than 600 years. Its exact origin is uncertain. One popular theory links it to wine sellers who "broached", or tapped, a barrel and sold the wine on in small amounts. Whatever the truth, the meaning settled long ago: a go-between in trade.
One more confusing word "Brokerage" means two things. It can mean the firm, as in "a brokerage". Or it can mean the fee, as in "we charge brokerage on each trade". On a broking floor it almost always means the fee.

Three of them in more detail

The insurance broker

You need to insure a factory. There are dozens of insurers. An insurance broker knows them all, works out what cover you need, and finds the best policy. The broker works for you, and usually gives advice.

There is a London twist worth knowing. Large and unusual risks are insured at Lloyd's of London, where brokers walk the floor negotiating face to face with underwriters. That is a wholesale, relationship-driven market, and in spirit it is a close cousin of interdealer broking.

The mortgage broker and the finance broker

The same idea, for loans. You want to borrow. The broker searches lenders for the best deal and handles the paperwork. Again the broker is on the borrower's side and gives advice. "Finance broker" is the wider term, covering business loans and equipment finance too.

The stockbroker

This is the one people picture. A stockbroker buys and sells shares on the stock exchange for an investor. There are three levels of service:

  • Execution only: you decide, they carry out the order. Investing apps are this.
  • Advisory: they suggest what to buy. You decide.
  • Discretionary: they decide for you and manage your money.
Careful: the film version When people hear "broker" they often picture a film scene: someone cold-calling the public to push shares. That is a caricature of a retail stockbroker. A TP ICAP broker does not deal with the public, does not recommend investments, and does not push a product. If you say "stockbroker" in your interview, you have described the wrong job.

Chapter 3

Broker against other jobs

You asked how a broker compares with a salesman and a customer relationship manager. This is the right question, because broking borrows from both. Let's line them up.

Broker against salesperson

The job advert calls broking a "high pressured sales" environment. So is a broker just a salesperson? Partly yes, and the difference is the interesting bit.

A salespersonA TP ICAP broker
What they sellTheir company's product: software, cars, advertisingNo product. They sell access to the other side, and their own service
SidesOne customer. The company is the other sideTwo customers. The firm is on neither side
PriceSet by the company, with some room to discountNot theirs to set. Discovered between the two clients
CustomersOften new ones. Much time spent finding themA small circle of the same traders, every day
The "close"Days, weeks or monthsSeconds. Many times a day
What they sharePicking up the phone. Persuading. Hearing "no". Being measured on revenue. Being paid on results.
In plain English A salesperson says: "Buy my product." A broker says: "I've found someone who wants what you have. Shall we get it done?" The energy is the same. The thing being sold is completely different. A broker can never win by pushing, because pushing one client hurts the other, and both are customers.

Broker against relationship manager

A customer relationship manager, also called an account manager or relationship manager, looks after existing customers. They keep them happy, sort out problems and encourage them to stay and buy more.

The phrase also names a kind of software, usually shortened to CRM, which stores customer records. Here we mean the job.

In plain English A relationship manager looks after the relationship. A broker trades on it, many times a day. Every single call tests whether the client still trusts you.

The recipe

How to read this diagram Four white boxes, each one ingredient borrowed from another job. The dark box is the result. The fourth ingredient, fairness, is the one that salespeople and relationship managers do not need. It is what makes broking its own profession.

A closer everyday comparison than you might expect

If you want one familiar job that feels most like interdealer broking, try a good recruiter. A recruiter has two sets of clients, companies and candidates. They hold confidential information from both. They are paid only when a match is made. Their whole value is who they know and whether both sides trust them.

The differences: a recruiter is paid by one side, a match takes weeks, and they place a person rather than close a trade. But the instinct is the same.

Chapter 4

A TP ICAP broker, specifically

Now we can say exactly what a broker is here, and you will see why each word matters.

The definition A TP ICAP broker is a neutral go-between for the world's largest financial institutions. They find the other side of very large trades, keep both clients' identities confidential, help the two sides agree a price, and earn commission, without ever taking a position themselves.

Five things that define the job

FeatureWhat it means
1. WholesaleClients are institutions only: banks, hedge funds, asset managers, energy firms. Never the public.
2. NeutralBoth sides of every trade are clients. The broker cannot favour one.
3. No riskThe firm does no proprietary trading. If prices move, the clients gain or lose. The broker does not.
4. ConfidentialThe broker knows what everyone is trying to do and tells no one. This is the heart of the trust.
5. Real timeTrades are found, negotiated and agreed in seconds or minutes, by phone, chat and screen.

Why "interdealer"?

"Inter" means between. "Dealer" means a firm that trades with its own money, usually a bank. So an interdealer broker is a broker between dealers.

That explains the neutrality. An insurance broker can take your side because the insurer is not their client. But when both sides are your clients, and you will speak to both again in ten minutes, the only way to survive is to be fair to each.

TP ICAP is several kinds of broker under one roof

Here is something neat. TP ICAP's accounts list four ways it arranges trades. Each one matches a kind of broker from Chapter 2.

How to read this diagram Four boxes, four ways of being a broker. The top left box is by far the biggest and is the classic interdealer job. The bottom two are closer to "acting for one client". The rest of the firm's revenue, about 8%, is data subscriptions. So when someone asks "what kind of broker is TP ICAP?", the honest answer is "mostly a neutral matchmaker, with other kinds alongside".

What the broker is selling, if there is no product

A salesperson has a product. So what does a broker offer a trader who could ring five other brokers?

  • Liquidity: "I can find you the other side."
  • Information: "Here is what the market is doing right now."
  • Discretion: "Nobody will know it was you."
  • Speed: "It will be done before the price moves."
  • Themselves: "You know I'll be straight with you."

That last one is why the firm's accounts carry £366 million of "customer relationships" as an asset, and why one average broker generated about £752,000 of revenue in 2025. The broker is the product.

Chapter 5

Why broking exists, and whether you want it

Two different questions hide inside "why broking?". One is about the world. One is about you. Be ready for both.

Question one: why does the world need brokers?

Big banks are clever and well connected. They could call each other. They pay a broker because the broker solves five problems.

ProblemHow the broker solves it
Finding the other side takes timeOne call to a broker reaches the whole market.
Showing your hand is dangerousThe broker hides who is buying or selling.
Many products have no public priceThe broker sees everyone's interest, so knows where the price really is.
Big trades move the marketThe broker works them quietly, piece by piece.
Rivals do not like to haggle directlyThe broker carries the negotiation between them.
In plain English On a trade worth hundreds of millions, getting a slightly better price is worth far more than the broker's fee. The broker is paid a little to save the client a lot. As long as that is true, broking exists.

Question two: why would you want it?

Now use everything from Chapter 3. Broking is the right choice if you want a particular mix that the neighbouring jobs do not offer.

If you like this......but not thisThen broking fits because
The drive and scoreboard of salesPushing one product on strangersYou close deals all day, with clients you know, and no product to push.
The trust of relationship managementA slow pace and a vague scoreboardYou build relationships for years, and see the result every day.
Being close to markets, like a traderBetting money and carrying lossesYou see more of the market than any one trader, and take no risk.
The pace of financeLong nights building spreadsheetsThe work is talking to people, not modelling.

An honest self-test

Read each line. Be truthful. There is no shame in a "no".

  • I would rather talk to someone than email them.
  • When someone says no, I want to try again.
  • I like being measured against a number.
  • I can keep a secret, even when sharing it would make me look clever.
  • I can be fair to two people who want opposite things.
  • I enjoy quick sums more than long analysis.
  • I would rather have twenty clients for ten years than two hundred for a week.
What your answers tell you Five or more yeses: broking suits you, and you can say why with conviction.
Mostly yes to the first three, no to the rest: you may be a natural salesperson, and plain sales might suit you better.
Yes to the secret and the fairness, no to the phone and the number: relationship management or advice may fit you more.

What the choice costs

To decide properly you need the other side of the ledger too.

  • Your income depends on market activity and on keeping your clients.
  • You do not own the decision. The client decides. You help it happen. Some people find that frustrating.
  • You will know things you can never say.
  • The simple part of the job is moving to screens. The future belongs to brokers who handle what a screen cannot.
Chapter 6

How to say it

Short answers for every version of the question. Each one is built from this pack, so you can explain any word in it.

"What is a broker?"

15 seconds "A broker arranges a deal between two other parties and earns a fee for it, without owning what is traded or taking the risk."
45 seconds, for TP ICAP "A broker arranges a deal between two other parties and earns a fee, without taking the risk. At TP ICAP that means being a neutral go-between for the largest banks and institutions. When a bank needs to pass on risk, the broker finds another institution to take the other side, keeps both names confidential, and helps them agree a price.

What makes it different from an insurance broker or a stockbroker is that both sides are clients, so the broker has to be fair to each. About 62% of TP ICAP's revenue comes from name passing, where the broker simply introduces the two sides and never holds the position."

"How is that different from a stockbroker or an insurance broker?"

30 seconds "Three ways. The clients: a stockbroker or insurance broker usually serves the public or ordinary businesses, while an interdealer broker serves only large institutions. The side: those brokers act for their customer, while an interdealer broker is neutral because both sides are clients. And advice: they often recommend what to do, whereas an interdealer broker gives market information and the client decides."

"Isn't broking just sales?"

30 seconds "It has the drive of sales: you're on the phone, you hear no, and you're measured on revenue. But a salesperson sells their company's product to one customer. A broker has no product and two customers on every trade. You can't win by pushing, because pushing one client hurts the other. What you're really selling is trust and your ability to find the other side."

"How is it different from being a relationship manager?"

20 seconds "A relationship manager looks after a client over months and usually hands the deal to someone else. A broker builds the same long-term trust, but does the deal personally, many times a day. Every call tests the relationship."

"What is the difference between a broker and a trader?"

15 seconds "A trader buys and sells with their firm's money and carries the risk. A broker sits between traders, finds the other side and earns commission without taking risk. The trader is the broker's client."

"Why broking?"

This version uses the comparison itself as the answer. Few candidates will do that, because few have thought it through.

60 seconds. Put your own story in the gap "Thanks for watching. I want broking because it combines the two things I'm best at, and I worked that out by comparing it with the jobs next to it.

I like what sales involves: [YOUR STORY, one sentence, one number]. But I don't want to push one product at strangers. And I like building long relationships, but I want a scoreboard I can see every day.

Broking is the only role I found that has both. You deal with the same clients for years, and you're neutral between them, so the only thing you can sell is trust. At TP ICAP the average broker generated about £750,000 of revenue last year on exactly that.

That mix of drive, trust and markets, without taking risk, is what I want to do."

The mistakes to avoid

  • Saying "stockbroker". Wrong job.
  • Saying a broker "advises clients on what to buy". An interdealer broker does not advise.
  • Saying a broker "sells products". There is no product.
  • Saying a broker "works for the client" as if there were one. There are two, and the broker is neutral.
  • Saying a broker "trades". The clients trade. The broker arranges.
