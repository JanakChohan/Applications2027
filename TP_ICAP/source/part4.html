Chapter 1

First: whose interest rates does TP ICAP deal in?

You asked: "What companies' interest rates do they specialise in? Is it UK? Would it be US?" Great instinct to ask. Two things to straighten out.

Fix one: it is countries, not companies

In plain English The Rates desk deals in the interest rates of currencies and governments: the pound, the dollar, the euro. What a company pays to borrow belongs to the Credit desk. Part 3 covers that difference.

Fix two: it is not just the UK

The job is in London. But London is where the world's interest rate contracts are traded, not just Britain's. About half of all over-the-counter interest rate derivatives on earth are traded in the UK, and a large share of those are in euros and dollars rather than pounds.

TP ICAP does not publish its Rates revenue by currency. But the evidence points one way:

  • Its swaps platform, i-Swap, covers three currencies: euros, pounds and dollars.
  • Its data arm publishes swap rate indices in euros and dollars.
  • About 63% of the whole group's revenue is in US dollars.
How to read this diagram Each bar is how much was traded in swaps on that currency's interest rate in three months. Longer means more. The orange bar is the pound. Sterling is only about 12% of the total. Dollars and euros together are over 60%. So a rates broker in London is watching the US central bank and the European Central Bank just as closely as the Bank of England. The total is about $297 trillion.
Careful with this number These are headline contract sizes for the whole market, on every trading venue. They are not money that changed hands, and they are not TP ICAP's revenue. Treat them as a thermometer for how busy each currency was.
So the answer to your question "TP ICAP's rates business covers the three big currencies: dollars, euros and sterling. London handles about half the world's OTC interest rate derivatives, so a desk here trades global rates, not only UK rates."

Chapter 3

The story

Here is the headline everyone knows, and then the part underneath it that almost nobody will mention.

What everybody knows

On 5 November 2026 the Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 3.75% to 4%. The facts, from the Bank's own minutes:

  • The vote was 8 to 1. The one dissenter did not want to hold. She wanted a bigger rise, to 4.25%.
  • The reason: the Middle East conflict, then in its ninth month, kept pushing energy prices up.
  • Inflation was 3.5% in September. The Bank now expects it to reach about 5% by the middle of 2027, then fall.
  • The key sentence: "Monetary policy is not on a pre-set path." In other words: we do not know what we will do next.

News reports said markets then bet on about three more quarter-point rises by the end of 2027.

What almost nobody will mention

Most people look at the price of money: rates went up. A broker looks at something else. How much is being traded? Because that is what a broker is paid on.

How to read this diagram Four bars, four quarters, oldest at the top. The blue bars are the busy period. The orange bars are what came next. Sterling swap trading more than halved. The third quarter was 51% below the first-quarter record, and 14% lower than the same quarter a year earlier. The Bank of England's rate rise came five weeks after the last bar ends.

Meanwhile, in dollars and euros

The same report shows the opposite picture in the other big currencies during that third quarter:

  • Dollar swaps: a record $99.9 trillion, up 15% on a year earlier.
  • Euro swaps: up 47% on a year earlier.
  • All currencies together: up 23% on a year earlier.
The story in three sentences Sterling interest rate trading hit a record at the start of 2026, then more than halved while the Bank of England sat still. On 5 November the Bank changed direction for the first time since 2023, and said openly that it does not know its next step. For a firm that is paid on activity rather than direction, that change is the news, not the quarter of a percent.

Why volume matters more than price to a broker

Remember the toll bridge from Part 2. TP ICAP does not own what crosses the bridge. It collects a toll each time something crosses.

  • Rates at 4% with nobody trading: bad for a broker.
  • Rates at 4% with everybody changing their positions: good for a broker.

So the useful question is never "are rates high or low?" It is "are people being forced to trade?"

Why did sterling go quiet? My reading

The data does not explain itself. This is my interpretation.

  1. The first quarter was a shock. When energy prices jumped, every bank, pension fund and hedge fund had to rethink where UK rates were heading. All at once. Record trading.
  2. Then the Bank held still. With Bank Rate parked at 3.75% and most people agreeing it would stay there, there was less to disagree about. Less disagreement means fewer trades.
  3. Agreement is the enemy of a broker. A trade needs two people who see things differently.
Chapter 4

The effects: who feels this, and how

A good answer follows the news all the way down the chain: from the event, to the clients, to the firm, to the role you want.

How to read this diagram Follow the numbers from 1 to 5. Each step causes the next. The link most people miss is step 2. A rate rise that everyone agreed on would cause little trading. It is the disagreement about what happens next that creates the business.

Effect on the clients

ClientWhat a new rate-rising cycle does to them
Banks and mortgage lendersThey lend at fixed rates and pay savers changing rates. When the direction of rates flips, they must re-hedge that gap using swaps.
Pension funds and insurersTheir promises stretch decades ahead. Higher long-term rates and higher inflation both change what those promises are worth. They use rate and inflation swaps to stay matched.
Hedge fundsThey bet on the path of rates. Uncertainty over the next move is their opportunity.
Bank trading desksThey take the other side of all of the above, then need to pass the risk on. That is the interdealer market. That is TP ICAP's core.

The big banks' own results show how uneven 2026 has been for them. News reports of third-quarter results said Citigroup's fixed income trading revenue rose 4% to $4.4 billion, while JPMorgan's fell 4% to $5.4 billion. Same market, different outcomes. Clients who are under pressure to perform lean harder on brokers who can find them a better price.

Effect on TP ICAP

  • Rates is the firm's biggest earner. £635 million in 2025, or 27% of the whole group. Anything that wakes up rates trading matters more to TP ICAP than any other single thing.
  • It held up through the quiet patch. Rates revenue grew 10% in the first half of 2026, and the firm reported "continued good growth in Rates" for the first nine months. That was while sterling trading was halving.
  • The reason is the mix. Dollar and euro trading stayed strong. A firm covering all three currencies is cushioned when one goes quiet.
  • The data arm benefits too. When rates move, more firms need reliable swap prices to value their positions. That is what Parameta sells.

Effect on the role

  • A turning point is the best time to learn. Clients need their brokers most when the picture changes. A junior on a rates desk in 2027 will see the market at its most alive.
  • The timing is striking. The Bank expects inflation to peak around the middle of 2027. The internship runs from late June. You would arrive just as the argument over the next move is at its loudest.
  • Human judgement counts more. In a calm market, a standard swap trades on a screen. In a turning market, clients want to move size without showing their hand. That needs a person.

Effect on the industry

  • Every broker benefits, so the fight for share gets sharper. BGC and Tradition see the same opportunity.
  • Electronic platforms are growing faster than the market. Tradeweb, an electronic rival, reported its swaps trading up about 42% on a year earlier in the third quarter, against roughly 23% for the market as a whole.
  • So the work is splitting in two. Simple swaps go to screens. Complex and sensitive trades stay with people. TP ICAP's answer is to offer both, through Fusion and i-Swap alongside its voice brokers.
Chapter 5

Your take

This is the part that wins the question. Anyone can repeat news. Very few candidates say "and here is what I think happens next, and why." Below is a take with three layers. Read it, argue with it, and keep the parts you believe.

How to read this diagram Read from the bottom up. The bottom layer is a short-term prediction. The middle layer is what it tells you about the firm. The top layer is the big picture for the industry. A strong answer touches all three in under two minutes.

Layer 1: what I think happens next

My take "I expect sterling swap activity to recover from that third-quarter low. Not because rates went up, but because the Bank of England has gone from being predictable to being uncertain. It told the market it is not on a pre-set path, one member already wants to go faster, and it is forecasting 5% inflation. That is a recipe for disagreement, and disagreement is what creates trades."

The honest limit: I would not expect a return to the first-quarter record. That was a one-off shock. This is a slower, steadier kind of busy.

Layer 2: what it tells you about TP ICAP

My take "What struck me is that TP ICAP's Rates revenue still grew 10% in the first half, even though sterling volumes had halved. That tells me the franchise isn't a UK business. It's a three-currency business, and dollar swaps hit a record in the third quarter. So a sterling recovery would be an extra on top of a base that was already holding up."

Layer 3: what it means for the industry and the job

My take "Longer term, I think this is the kind of moment that shows why voice broking survives. Electronic platforms are growing faster than the market on standard swaps. But when the direction of rates changes, clients need to move large positions without revealing them, and that takes a person they trust. So I see the broker's job becoming less about volume and more about the difficult trades."

How the view changes the way we look at things

You asked how this news changes the way we view the market. Three shifts:

BeforeAfter
"Rates are coming down slowly. The only question is when.""Rates can go up again. The question is how far."

The second row is worth a moment. For most of 2026, bond yields simply followed oil. Reports in late November say that link broke: oil dropped, yet yields did not. The reason is that price rises have spread beyond energy. That moves the centre of attention from the oil desk to the rates desk.

What would prove this take wrong?

Saying this out loud makes you sound thoughtful, not unsure.

  • A quick peace deal. If the conflict ends and energy prices fall fast, the Bank may stop after one rise. The market would go quiet again.
  • The rise was already expected. Two-year UK yields moved only a few hundredths of a percent on the day. If everyone already agrees on the path, there is less to trade.
  • Trading moves to screens. Activity could recover without voice brokers getting their share.

Chapter 7

Two backup stories

In case you want a different angle, or they ask for a second story. Both are specific to this industry. Both are harder than the main one, so only use them if they make sense to you.

Backup 1: the new rule for US government bonds

What is happening

The market for US government bonds, called Treasuries, is the biggest bond market in the world. From 31 December 2026, a new rule from the US regulator requires many trades in it to go through a clearing house. A second deadline, for short-term loans secured on those bonds, follows on 30 June 2027.

In plain English A clearing house is a safety net. It stands in the middle of a trade. If one side goes bust, the clearing house pays the other. It makes the market safer, but everyone has to post cash as security, which costs money.

Why it is a broker's story

The rule specifically covers trades done through interdealer brokers. Law firms have been advising clients to check, platform by platform, whether each venue they use counts as an interdealer broker.

A possible take

My take "I think the clearing rule is a quiet turning point for interdealer brokers in Treasuries. In the short term it adds cost for some clients, which could dampen activity. But longer term, once everyone clears through the same place, it matters less who you are trading against. That could let more types of firm trade with each other through a broker, which widens the network a broker can offer."

Honest note: this is a US market story, and the detail is technical. TP ICAP does not break out how much of its revenue comes from Treasuries. Use it to show you read beyond the headlines, not as your main answer.

Backup 2: oil and bonds have stopped moving together

What is happening

For most of 2026, government bond yields followed the oil price closely. When oil rose, traders expected higher inflation and higher rates, so yields rose too. Reports citing the Financial Times say that link was about 65% in September and fell below 20% by late November. Oil dropped to around $92. Yields stayed high.

Why it is a broker's story

It connects two TP ICAP divisions. Energy & Commodities had a hard year because oil futures trading dried up. Global Broking had a strong one. If markets now care more about central banks than about oil, attention shifts further towards the Rates desk.

A possible take

My take "For most of this year you could predict bonds by checking the oil price. That link has broken. Oil has fallen but yields haven't, which tells me the market's worry has moved from the price of energy to inflation spreading through the economy. For TP ICAP that suggests rates stay busy even if the oil market calms down, which is a good position for its biggest desk."

Honest note: the correlation figure comes from a news report quoting the Financial Times. I have not seen the original. Say "reports suggest".
