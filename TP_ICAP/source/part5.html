How to split your time

How to read this diagram The bar is all your preparation time. Most of it goes on the three questions that are nearly certain. A good slice goes on the next three. Only a sliver on the long shots. Do not spend an evening on AI and then stumble on "tell us about yourself".

The 60-second shape

At a normal speaking pace, 60 seconds is about 150 words. That is not much. So the shape tightens:

PartTimeWhat goes in it
Openabout 10 secondsA short thanks, then name your two points. Put your strongest specific here.
Point oneabout 20 secondsSomething you did. One number.
Point twoabout 20 secondsSomething specific about the job or the firm. One number.
Closeabout 10 secondsOne line linking back to the role.

Two points, not three. Three points in a minute means rushing all of them. If you turn out to have two minutes, add a third point about where the industry is going.

Chapter 3

Tier 1: the three you must nail

Each one has: what they are really testing, a 60-second answer with a gap for your own story, and the trap to avoid. The longer versions are in Part 2, Chapter 5.

1. "Tell us about yourself" or "Why are you a good candidate?"

What they are testing: can you be clear and likeable in a minute? Would a client enjoy talking to you?

60-second answer "Thanks for watching. I'm [name], a final-year [subject] student at [university], and I'd sum myself up in two things: I like persuading people, and I like markets.

On the first, [YOUR STORY: what you did, with one number. For example a sales role, a campaign where you contacted lots of people, a society or fund you ran]. What I learned is that I enjoy the part most people avoid: picking up the phone again after a no.

On the second, I follow markets closely. Recently I've been watching how the Bank of England's rate rise is changing trading in interest rate swaps.

Put together, that is why broking fits me better than any other seat in finance, and why I'm applying to TP ICAP."
The trapReciting your CV in date order. They have your CV. Give them two themes and one story instead.

2. "Why broking?"

What they are testing: do you know what a broker does, and do you want this job rather than any job in finance?

60-second answer "Thank you for watching. Two things draw me to broking: I've seen that I'm at my best when I'm persuading people, and I like that a broker is paid for trust and judgement rather than for taking risk.

First, [YOUR STORY, two sentences, one number]. That showed me I want a role built on relationships and results.

Second, the job itself. A broker finds the other side of a trade and closes the gap between a bid and an offer, without ever revealing who the client is. About 62% of TP ICAP's revenue comes from name passing, where the broker never holds the position. The skill is knowing who wants what before anyone else does.

I'd rather build a book of clients who call me first than sit behind a model, and that is why I want to start on a broking desk."
The trapSaying you want to "trade" or "invest". A broker does neither. Also avoid "I love fast-paced environments" with nothing behind it.

3. "Why TP ICAP?"

What they are testing: have you done real research, or would this answer fit any firm?

60-second answer "Thanks for taking the time. Two reasons: TP ICAP's strength in the market I find most interesting, and the fact that it is investing in junior brokers right now.

First, rates. The interest rate desks made £635 million in 2025, more than a quarter of the whole group, and Rates still grew 10% in the first half of this year. With central banks changing direction, I think that is the most interesting place to learn.

Second, the firm is building. Broker numbers rose 6% in a year, the trainee programmes were expanded, and it completed the Vantage Capital Markets acquisition in July. That tells me there are seats to earn, not just a name to put on a CV.

Add the rotation across four desks, and this is where I'd most like to start."
The trap"Largest interdealer broker in the world" and nothing else. Every candidate will say it. Use it only as a stepping stone to something specific.
If two of these are merged into one question "Why broking and why TP ICAP?" is common. Give one point for each: your story for broking, then one firm-specific fact for TP ICAP. Do not try to squeeze four points into a minute.
Chapter 4

Tier 2 and Tier 3: ready answers

"What is an interdealer broker?"

What they are testing: the basics. A clear, confident definition is worth more than a clever one.

45-second answer "An interdealer broker is a middleman between the big institutions in wholesale markets, mainly the trading desks of banks. When a bank needs to pass on risk, the broker finds another institution to take the other side, keeps both names confidential, and earns commission from each. The broker takes no position itself.

They exist because many markets, like interest rate swaps, have no central exchange, so someone has to find the price and the counterparty. TP ICAP is the largest by revenue, and it does this by voice, electronically, and through a hybrid of the two on its Fusion platform."

"What is the difference between a broker and a trader?"

20-second answer "A trader buys and sells with their firm's money and carries the risk. A broker sits between traders, finds the other side and earns commission, without taking risk. The trader is the broker's client."

"Tell us about a time you worked under pressure" or "showed resilience"

What they are testing: will you keep going after the tenth "no" of the morning?

60-second frame. Fill it with your own story Situation, one sentence: "Last year I had to [task] with [deadline or obstacle]."
The pressure, one sentence, with a number: "After [number] attempts I had [poor result]."
What you did, two or three sentences: the specific things you changed. This is most of the answer.
Result, one sentence, with a number.
Link, one sentence: "A broker hears no all day, and that experience is why I know I can keep dialling."

"Tell us about a time you made a mistake"

What they are testing: honesty. On a desk, an error must be flagged in seconds.

  • Pick a real mistake with a real consequence. Not "I work too hard".
  • Say what you did in the first few minutes after spotting it.
  • End with: "On a broking desk a wrong price costs real money, so I'd rather flag an error straight away than hope nobody notices."

"What skills would make you a successful broker?"

45-second answer "Three, and I'd rank them. First, trust: a trader gives the order to the broker who keeps a secret. Second, resilience, because most calls don't end in a trade. Third, speed and accuracy with numbers, since one wrong digit is expensive. I'd point to [YOUR EVIDENCE] for the first two, and I practise mental maths daily for the third."

"What recent news has interested you?"

Use the story in Part 4. The 30-second version is ready there. If you have a full minute, add the line about dollar and euro volumes staying strong.

"How do you see technology or AI affecting broking?"

45-second answer "I think it splits the work in two. Simple, standard trades move to screens, and AI speeds that up. But large or sensitive trades still need someone the client trusts. So I see AI making a broker more productive rather than replacing them. TP ICAP's First Mate is a good example: it flags possible matches for sales traders so they can make the call sooner. [If true: I've used AI tools myself to ...]"

"Why not investment banking?"

"I want my results to come from client relationships and what I bring in each day, not from building models on long projects. Broking puts me on the phone with the market from the start."

"What are your strengths and weaknesses?"

This one can only lose you points. Be brief and human. One real strength with evidence. One real weakness with what you are doing about it. No finance needed.

Chapter 6

What news affects Global Broking's clients?

This was the last question in your notes, and it is a good one for the "news" answer. Here is the rule, then the live list.

The rule News matters to a broker's clients when it forces them to change their positions. A bank's trading desk does not call a broker because something is interesting. It calls because it now holds risk it needs to move. So for any headline, ask: "who has to trade because of this?"

The clients, in one line each

  • Bank trading desks: the core client. They take trades from customers, then pass the risk on.
  • Hedge funds: they bet on where prices go, often with borrowed money.
  • Pension funds and insurers: they must match promises that stretch decades ahead.

The one thing only you can supply

Your story Four of the six answers above lean on one real story about you: a time you persuaded people, got knocked back and kept going. Choose it now. It needs a number in it. Without it, every answer above is just research that any candidate could recite.

On the day

  • Do the practice question first. Learn where the timer and the buttons are.
  • Read each question's rules before you start: think time, time limit, takes.
  • If the limit is one minute, use two points. If it is two minutes, use three.
  • If you only get one take, slow down for the first sentence. A calm start matters more than a perfect ending.
  • If you run out of time, stop cleanly. An unfinished sentence is worse than a short answer.
  • Finish a few seconds early if you can.
