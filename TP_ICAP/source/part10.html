The role: do you want it?

Part 10. What a broker does, the skills you really need, the best parts and the hardest parts, in plain English

Every other pack helps you get the job. This one helps you decide whether you want it. So it is honest. It covers the good and the bad, and it ends with a simple test.

The role in one sentence A broker talks to traders all day, finds two who want opposite things, and helps them agree a price. The firm earns a small fee from each. The broker never bets on the price.

As an intern

For six weeks you would mostly watch and learn. You would sit with brokers on each of the four desks, go to workshops, and meet a lot of people. At the end the firm decides whether to offer you a desk.

Chapter 2

The ten skills you really need

Not the skills on a CV. The ones a desk actually uses. Each has a small scene so you can picture it.

How to read this diagram Three layers. The dark top layer is what the job cannot be done without. The middle layer separates a good broker from an average one. The white bottom layer is what people think they need, and do not. This is a people job with numbers in it, not a numbers job with people in it.
1
Listening Why: clients rarely say exactly what they want. You have to hear it. Picture this: a trader says, "Nothing for me today... where's the ten-year?" He just told you he is interested. A good broker hears that. A poor one hears "nothing".
2
Keeping secrets Why: you know what every client is trying to do. If you leak it, nobody calls you again. Picture this: Bank A is selling. Bank B asks, "Who's the seller?" The answer is always, "I can't say." Even when telling them would close the trade.
3
Bouncing back Why: most calls end in "no". Picture this: you spend an hour on a trade. At the last second one side walks away. You have ten seconds to feel bad. Then the phone rings.
4
Building trust over time Why: a trader gives the big orders to the broker they trust most. Picture this: for six months a trader gives you small trades. You handle each one well. One day he rings you first with the biggest trade of his year. That call took six months to earn.
5
Speed and care with numbers Why: prices are called fast, and one wrong digit costs real money. Picture this: "Bid 4.20, offered 4.26." You need the middle, 4.23, before the trader finishes his sentence. And you must never say 4.32.
6
Memory Why: yesterday's "no" is today's match. Picture this: on Tuesday a client wanted to sell but found no buyer. On Thursday a different client wants to buy the same thing. If you remember Tuesday, you have a trade.
7
Short, clear speech Why: nobody has time for a long sentence. Picture this: not "I think there might be someone who could be interested." Just: "I have a buyer at 21."
8
Staying calm in a rush Why: the busiest minutes are the most valuable, and the easiest to get wrong. Picture this: a central bank surprises everyone. Four phones ring at once. The broker who stays calm earns a week's fees in ten minutes.
9
Curiosity about markets Why: clients ring the broker who has something useful to say. Picture this: two brokers call the same trader at 7:15. One says "morning". The other says, "Did you see what the Bank of England said?" Guess who gets called back.
10
Owning a mistake fast Why: a mistake grows every minute it is hidden. Picture this: you told a client the wrong price. You tell your desk head within the minute. It costs a little. Hidden until 5pm, it costs a lot.

Chapter 3

The best parts

What people who do the job say they like, and what follows from how the job works.

1
You know how you are doing, every day. Your result is a number. No waiting a year for a review to find out.
2
You are in the middle of the markets. A broker hears what dozens of banks and funds are doing. Few seats in finance see as much.
3
You do not carry the risk. If prices crash, the clients win or lose. You do not go home having lost money on a bet.
4
The people. In reviews of TP ICAP, brokers most often praise their colleagues and their team. A desk is a close group.
5
Pay follows results. Broker pay is tied to the revenue a desk brings in. Reviews often name pay as a plus, and one called it "a good first graduate job".
6
The day usually ends. Early start, but often finished around five. Not the late nights of investment banking.
7
What you build is yours. Your clients trust you. That list of people is the most valuable thing a broker owns.
One number On Glassdoor, brokers rate TP ICAP about 4.3 out of 5, across roughly 73 reviews. That is a small, self-chosen group, so treat it as a rough signal.
Chapter 4

The hardest parts

You asked for the truth. Here it is. None of these should surprise you on day one.

1
The early starts. At your desk around 7am, every day.
2
Long, intense days. "Long hours and no lunch" is one of the most common complaints in reviews. You are "on" from the first call to the last.
3
Hearing "no" all day. Most calls do not become trades. Trades fall apart at the last second.
4
Your income moves. A quiet market means less pay. Some reviews complain of low bonuses.
5
Clients can leave. A trader changes jobs, or picks another broker, and a slice of your income goes with them.
6
You never get to decide. The client chooses. You help. Some people find that frustrating.
7
Evenings are part of the job. Relationships are built over dinner and drinks, not only on the phone.
8
It depends on your desk. Reviews say the experience "varies depending on the brokers you work with". Some juniors complain of little training. A few individual reviews describe a harsh culture, including bullying, on some desks.
9
The job is changing. Simple trades are moving to screens. The future belongs to brokers who handle what a screen cannot.
About number 8 These are anonymous, individual accounts, some of them years old. I cannot check them. But they point to something true of every broking firm: the desk you land on matters more than the firm's name. The six-week internship is your chance to see the desks for yourself before you commit.

Chapter 5

Do you want it?

Be honest. Nobody is marking this.

Tick the ones that are true

  • I would rather talk to someone than write to them.
  • I get energy from a busy, noisy room.
  • When someone says no, I want to try again.
  • I like being measured against a number.
  • I am fine with pay that goes up and down.
  • I can keep a secret, even when sharing it would help me.
  • I can be fair to two people who want opposite things.
  • I am a morning person, or can become one.
  • I enjoy quick sums more than long analysis.
  • I would rather have twenty clients for ten years than a new project every month.
What your ticks tell you Eight or more: this job fits you. Go for it with confidence.
Five to seven: it could fit. Use the internship to test the ones you were unsure about.
Four or fewer: think hard. You might enjoy sales or client work somewhere slower and steadier more.

The internship is the real test

You do not have to decide today. Six weeks on the desks will tell you more than any pack. If you get there, watch for these:

  • Do I enjoy the noise, or does it wear me out by Wednesday?
  • How do the senior brokers treat the juniors?
  • Does anyone actually teach?
  • Which desk has people I would want to sit with for years?
  • Do I still want to pick up the phone after a bad day?
My honest read, from what you have told me You sold to the public from 14. You liked the middle seat at Pictet. You debate for fun, on fifteen minutes' notice. Those are the habits of someone who would enjoy this job. But you know yourself better than I do. Trust your ticks above my guess.
Chapter 6

How to talk about it in the interview

Knowing the hard parts is a strength. It shows you have thought about doing the job, not just getting it.

"What does a broker do?"

20 seconds "A broker talks to traders at banks and funds all day, finds two who want opposite things, and helps them agree a price. The firm earns a small fee from each side. The broker never takes the risk."

"What skills does a broker need?"

40 seconds "I'd pick three. Listening, because clients rarely say exactly what they want. Trust, because a trader gives the big orders to the broker who keeps a secret. And bouncing back, because most calls end in no. I built the first and third selling school uniform to parents from the age of 14, and the second working with pension fund clients at Pictet."

"What do you think will be hardest?"

30 seconds "I think it will be hearing no so often, and having my results seen every day. I'd rather that than not know how I'm doing. And I've had practice: in debating you lose a round and are back up an hour later, and in the shop not every customer was pleased to see you."

"What appeals to you most about the role?"

30 seconds "Two things. The middle seat: helping two sides agree is the part of work I've enjoyed most. And the scoreboard: I like knowing each day how I did. Broking is the one role I've found with both."

"Why would you be good at it?"

Use the three-proof answer in Part 9: the shop, Pictet and debating.

What not to say Do not mention pay as your reason. Do not repeat the complaints from reviews. And do not say the hardest part is "working too hard". Pick a real hard part of the job, then show you have met something like it before.
