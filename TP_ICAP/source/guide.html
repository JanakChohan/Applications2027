Section 1

The 60-second version

TP ICAP is a matchmaker for the world's biggest financial institutions. When a bank wants to buy or sell something huge and complicated, TP ICAP finds another institution to take the other side. It charges a small fee on each trade. It never bets its own money.

In plain English Think of an estate agent. The agent does not own houses. The agent knows who wants to sell and who wants to buy, puts them together, and takes a fee when the deal completes. TP ICAP does that, but its "houses" are things like interest rate contracts, currencies, bonds, oil and electricity. Its customers are banks, investment funds and energy companies.

  1. What it is: the world's largest interdealer broker by revenue. "Interdealer" means it sits between dealers, which are mostly big banks.
  2. What it sells: a service, not a product. It sells finding the other side of a trade, and it sells price data.
  3. How it earns: commission on every trade it arranges, plus subscription fees for data.
  4. Size: revenue of £2,353 million in 2025. Adjusted operating profit of £348 million.
  5. Four divisions: Global Broking, Energy & Commodities, Liquidnet, and Parameta Solutions.

Section 2

Six ideas you need first

You do not need a finance degree. You need these six ideas. Everything else in the guide builds on them.

1. A market is just buyers meeting sellers

A market is any place where people who want to buy meet people who want to sell. A fruit market works this way. So does the market for interest rate contracts.

2. Some things trade on an exchange. Many do not.

An exchange is a central marketplace with a public screen of prices. The London Stock Exchange is one. Anyone can see the price of a share.

But a huge part of finance has no central marketplace. Deals are agreed privately between two parties, by phone, chat or private electronic system. This is called over the counter, or OTC.

In plain English Exchange trading is like buying from a supermarket. The price is on the shelf. OTC trading is like buying a second-hand car. There is no shelf price. You have to find a seller and agree a price. OTC is TP ICAP's home turf.

OTC markets are enormous. The Bank for International Settlements counted about $845 trillion of OTC derivative contracts outstanding at the end of 2025.

3. Dealers are the shopkeepers of finance

A dealer is usually a big bank, such as JPMorgan, Barclays or Goldman Sachs. Dealers stand ready to buy from, and sell to, their customers. This is called making a market.

4. Sell side and buy side

  • Sell side: the dealers. Banks that sell trading services.
  • Buy side: the investors. Pension funds, asset managers, hedge funds and insurers. They manage other people's money.

5. Liquidity means how easy it is to trade

A market is liquid when you can buy or sell quickly, in big size, without moving the price. A market is illiquid when it is hard to find anyone to trade with. TP ICAP's whole job is to create liquidity by finding the other side.

6. A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else

A derivative is an agreement between two parties. Its value depends on something else, such as an interest rate, a currency or the oil price. Firms use derivatives to protect themselves. This protection is called hedging.

Example: an interest rate swap A company has a loan where the interest rate moves up and down. It wants certainty. So it agrees a swap with a bank: the company pays a fixed rate, and the bank pays the moving rate. Now the company knows its costs. The bank has taken on the risk, and will often want to pass that risk to someone else. That is where TP ICAP comes in.

Section 4

Why clients need a middleman

Banks are sophisticated. They could phone each other directly. So why do they pay TP ICAP? Because the broker solves real problems.

The client's problemWhat it looks like in real lifeHow TP ICAP fixes it
"I can't show my hand" If Bank A tells Bank B it needs to sell a huge position, Bank B can move the price against it. Anonymity. The broker shows the order to the market without saying whose it is.
"I don't know who wants the other side" There is no central screen. Phoning 30 banks takes time, and the price moves while you search. Reach. One call to the broker reaches the whole market. The broker already knows who is looking.
"I don't know the fair price" Many OTC products trade rarely. No public price exists. Price discovery. The broker sees flow from everyone, so it knows where the market really is.
"My trade is too big or too complicated" A very large or custom trade cannot simply be clicked on a screen. Skill. The broker works the order quietly, in pieces, and negotiates between both sides.
"I need to follow the rules" Since the 2008 crisis, regulators want many trades done on supervised venues and reported. Regulated venues. TP ICAP runs approved trading venues and handles the reporting.
"I need to know what's going on" Traders need a feel for the market: who is buying, what is moving, and why. Market colour. Brokers share general information all day. This is a big part of the relationship.
In plain English: the value in one sentence The client pays a small fee so it can trade faster, in bigger size, at a better price, without revealing who it is. On a trade worth hundreds of millions, getting a slightly better price is worth far more than the broker's fee. That is why the service is worth paying for.

The network effect: why big brokers stay big

A broker is only useful if it can find the other side. The more clients a broker has, the more likely it is to find a match. So clients go to the broker that already has the most clients. That brings even more clients.

This is called a network effect. People in the market say "liquidity attracts liquidity". It is the main reason a handful of big firms dominate, and why being the largest matters so much to TP ICAP.

Say this in your interview "The way I understand it, a broker's value comes from its network. The more participants it speaks to, the better the prices and the faster it finds the other side. So scale reinforces itself, and that is TP ICAP's core advantage as the largest interdealer broker."

Voice, hybrid and electronic

Brokers arrange trades in three ways. You will hear these words a lot.

  • Voice: a human broker on the phone or on chat. Best for big, complex or rare trades.
  • Electronic: clients click prices on a screen. Best for simple, frequent trades.
  • Hybrid: a mix. The screen shows the prices, and the broker steps in to negotiate. TP ICAP's hybrid platform is called Fusion.

Why these clients? What does TP ICAP like about them?

This part is my own reasoning from how the business works. It is not a quote from the firm.

  • They trade in huge size. A tiny percentage of a very large number is still a good fee.
  • They trade again and again. A bank trading desk needs a broker every day, not once a year. Revenue repeats.
  • They are both customer and supply. Every client that joins is also a possible "other side" for every other client. More clients make the service better for all of them.
  • They are reliable payers. Large regulated institutions rarely fail to pay an invoice.
  • They are sticky. A trader trusts a broker they have spoken to for years. Relationships move slowly.
  • They need OTC markets. Their needs are too large or too tailored for a simple public exchange.

Why the firm wanted new types of client

Relying only on banks is risky. After the 2008 financial crisis, banks were made to hold more capital and take less risk. Many cut their trading desks. Fewer and smaller bank desks means fewer customers for an interdealer broker.

So TP ICAP set out to diversify, which simply means spreading out. It moved into energy clients, bought Liquidnet to reach investors, and built Parameta to sell data. This is the first pillar of its strategy.

Say this in your interview "What I found interesting is how the client base has widened. The traditional client was the bank trading desk. But through Liquidnet the firm now reaches over 1,100 buy-side firms, and through Energy & Commodities it serves producers and trading houses. So it is less dependent on any one type of client than a classic interdealer broker."

Where the money goes

A broker's biggest cost is its brokers. Most of what a broker earns for the firm is shared with that broker and their desk.

How to read this diagram The bar is one pound of revenue, split into three slices. The blue slice shows that roughly 63p goes straight back out on the people and tools that earned it. The three rounded numbers add to 101p. That is only rounding.

Why this cost structure matters

  • Pay moves with revenue. The firm says front office costs are "variable with revenue". In a bad year, pay falls too. That protects profit.
  • It is a performance culture. Brokers are rewarded for what they bring in. This is why the advert stresses resilience, sales ability and an entrepreneurial mindset.
  • Support costs are the lever. The firm cannot easily cut what it pays brokers. So it attacks the grey slice: moving systems to the cloud, simplifying its legal structure, and opening lower-cost hubs in Belfast and Manila. The target is at least £50 million of yearly savings by the end of 2026, a year ahead of plan.

What a broker actually does all day

This is a general picture of the job across the industry. Your desk may differ.

  • Early start. Brokers are in before markets open, reading overnight news and working out what clients will want.
  • Constant contact. Phone lines and chat screens are open to clients all day. A broker may speak to the same traders dozens of times.
  • Fast, exact communication. Prices and sizes are called out quickly. A single wrong digit is costly. This is why the advert asks for "excellent attention to detail".
  • Relationships. Trust is everything. A trader gives the order to the broker they trust to keep a secret and get it done. Meeting clients in person is part of the job.
  • Rejection. Most calls do not end in a trade. Brokers hear "no" all day and keep going. This is why the advert asks for resilience.

The firm's medium-term goal

Revenue growth in the "mid-to-high single digits" each year, meaning roughly 5% to 9%, with profit growing faster than revenue. Profit growing faster than revenue is called operating leverage. It happens when you add revenue without adding the same amount of cost.

The big idea behind the strategy

In plain English Old-style voice broking is a good business but a bumpy one. It booms when markets are wild and sags when they are calm. Investors pay less for bumpy profits. So TP ICAP is trying to become part broker, part technology platform, part data company. Platform and data income is steadier and more profitable. That is what "market infrastructure" is code for.

Fusion: the technology at the centre

  • What it is: TP ICAP's cloud-based electronic platform. Clients see prices on screen and can trade electronically or with a broker's help.
  • Why it matters to brokers: it handles the simple trades, which frees brokers to focus on the complex, valuable ones.
  • Why it matters to data: every trade on Fusion is captured cleanly. That feeds Parameta.
  • Why it matters defensively: if clients are going to trade on screens anyway, TP ICAP wants it to be TP ICAP's screen.

Where AI fits

The job advert says an interest in AI "would be beneficial". That is not a throwaway line. The firm says it is:

  • Using AI to spot likely matches between buyers and sellers faster.
  • Automating routine work such as handling orders.
  • Using AI to turn raw broking data into new products for Parameta.
  • Building software faster with AI coding tools.
Say this in your interview "I don't see AI replacing brokers, because the hard part of the job is trust and judgement. But I can see it making a broker more productive, by flagging who might want the other side of a trade before the broker has picked up the phone. The firm's First Mate assistant at Liquidnet is a good example of that."

Section 15

Commercial awareness

Commercial awareness means understanding what makes a business earn more or less money. For TP ICAP there is one golden rule.

The golden rule TP ICAP earns from activity, not direction. It does not care much whether prices go up or down. It cares whether clients are trading. Uncertainty makes clients change their positions. That means more trades and more commission. Calm, predictable markets are bad for business.

The word for jumpy markets is volatility. Brokers like volatility.

The live stories, as of late November 2026

1. Interest rates are rising again

  • The Bank of England raised its rate to 4% on 5 November 2026, its first rise since 2023.
  • The US central bank is widely expected to raise rates in December.
  • The UK government's 10-year borrowing cost is around 5.3%.

So what: a change in the direction of rates forces everyone to re-think their positions. That is good for Rates desks, which made £358 million in the first half of 2026.

2. The Middle East conflict and the oil market

  • From early 2026, conflict disrupted oil shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow sea route out of the Gulf. The International Energy Agency called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market.
  • Oil prices spiked above $110 a barrel in the spring. Brent crude, the main oil benchmark, is now around $92.

So what, part one: the shock gave TP ICAP a record first quarter in 2026, at £689 million.

So what, part two: this is the clever point. When physical oil stopped flowing, trading in oil contracts fell sharply. Oil is over half of the Energy & Commodities division, so its profit more than halved. Volatility helps a broker only while clients can still trade. Extreme disruption can shut a market down.

3. Voice broking is slowly moving to screens

Every year, more simple trades are done electronically. That can cut out the human broker. TP ICAP's answer is Fusion: keep the client on TP ICAP's own screen, and move brokers towards complex trades where a human adds value.

4. Artificial intelligence

Both TP ICAP and BGC are promoting AI tools. The open question for the industry: does AI make each broker more productive, or does it mean fewer brokers are needed? TP ICAP's line is productivity. Revenue per broker in Global Broking rose 7% in the first half of 2026.

5. The fight for talent

A broker's clients often follow them if they move firms. So rivals poach whole teams. TP ICAP said its oil business suffered "significant competitor disruption" at the end of 2024, and it has since spent heavily on hiring and keeping energy brokers. That is a big reason Energy & Commodities profit is down.

6. Electricity demand and the energy transition

The International Energy Agency expects electricity use to grow at least 2.5 times faster than total energy demand through 2030. Data centres for AI are part of that. More power trading means more for brokers to arrange. TP ICAP said strong growth in Power helped its energy division recover in the third quarter of 2026.

7. Digital assets

Large institutions are starting to trade cryptocurrencies and "tokenised" assets. The UK's full rulebook for crypto is due in 2027. TP ICAP is positioning early with Fusion Digital Assets, so it is ready when banks arrive in size.

8. Demand for data

Global spending on financial market data reached about $49 billion in 2025. Regulators keep asking firms to prove their prices are fair. That is a tailwind for Parameta.

9. London versus New York

TP ICAP considered listing Parameta in New York rather than London, because US investors tend to pay more for data companies. It is part of a wider debate about London losing listings to the US.

Say this in your interview "One thing I've been following is the oil market this year. I found it interesting that the Middle East conflict helped TP ICAP at first, with a record first quarter, but then hurt the energy division once physical oil flows were disrupted and futures volumes dropped. It showed me that brokers benefit from volatility, but only while clients are still able to trade."
Careful Market levels move daily. Before you record, spend two minutes checking today's Bank of England rate and the Brent oil price. If you quote a number, say "around".

Legal and regulatory background

Any firm of this size and age has legacy legal matters. The Annual Report discloses several, mostly inherited from ICAP before 2016:

Three "what if" questions to think through

Having a view on these puts you ahead of most candidates.

  1. What if markets go quiet in 2027? Broking revenue would fall. Broker pay would fall with it, which cushions profit. Parameta's subscriptions would hold up.
  2. What if more trading goes fully electronic? Commission per trade falls, but volumes can rise and costs are lower. The winners will be whoever owns the platform. That is why Fusion matters.
  3. What if BGC overtakes TP ICAP on revenue? The headline title would be lost. But what clients care about is which broker has the best network in their particular product. TP ICAP's strength in rates is the thing to protect.

Section 18

Glossary

Plain-English meanings for every term in this guide.

Adjusted profit
Profit with one-off costs removed, to show how the normal business is doing.
Asset class
A category of thing that is traded, such as rates, currencies, shares, bonds or oil.
Block trade
One very large trade, done privately so it does not move the price.
Bond
A loan you can trade. Governments and companies issue bonds to borrow money.
Broker
A middleman who arranges trades for a fee and takes no risk.
Buy side
Investors: pension funds, asset managers, hedge funds, insurers.
Buyback
When a company buys its own shares to reward shareholders.
Commission
The fee a broker charges for arranging a trade. Also called brokerage.
Constant currency
Growth with exchange rate swings removed, so you see real progress.
Credit
The market for company debt, and contracts linked to it.
Dealer
A firm, usually a bank, that stands ready to buy from and sell to customers.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, like a rate or a price.
Desk
A team of brokers covering one product. For example, the sterling swaps desk.
EBIT
Earnings before interest and tax. Another name for operating profit.
Exchange
A central public marketplace with visible prices.
Fusion
TP ICAP's electronic trading platform.
FX
Foreign exchange. Swapping one currency for another.
Hedging
Doing a trade to protect yourself against a price moving the wrong way.
Hybrid broking
A mix of screen trading and human brokers.
Interdealer broker (IDB)
A broker whose main clients are dealers trading with each other.
Liquidity
How easy it is to trade quickly, in size, without moving the price.
Margin
Profit as a share of revenue. A 15% margin means 15p profit per £1.
Matched principal
The broker briefly stands in the middle, buying and selling at the same instant.
Money markets
Very short-term borrowing and lending between institutions.
Name passing
The broker introduces two sides, who then deal with each other directly.
Notional
The headline size of a derivative contract. Used to calculate payments.
OTC
Over the counter. Traded privately between two parties, not on an exchange.
Price discovery
Working out what something is worth when there is no public price.
Proprietary trading
Betting a firm's own money on markets. TP ICAP does not do this.
Rates
Products linked to interest rates. TP ICAP's biggest area.
Sell side
Banks and dealers that provide trading services.
Spread
The gap between the price to buy and the price to sell.
Swap
An agreement to exchange one set of payments for another, such as fixed for floating interest.
Trader
Someone at a bank or fund who buys and sells and carries the risk. A broker's client.
Voice broking
Arranging trades by phone or chat.
Volatility
How much prices are jumping around. More volatility usually means more trading.
Volume
The amount of trading taking place.
Wholesale markets
Markets where only large institutions trade, not the public.
