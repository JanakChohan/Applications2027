# When your mind goes blank and the answer comes out generic

You told me two things happen. You read the question and your mind goes blank. Then what comes out is general, like "I set mini deadlines".

These are one problem, not two. This guide explains it and shows you how to fix it.

- **You are trying to think of an answer.** That is the mistake. A specific answer cannot be thought up. It can only be remembered.
- **Your mind goes blank because the search is too big.** "Any project from my whole life" is too much to look through in 30 seconds.
- **The fix on the day is one question.** Not "what do I say?" but "which one?"
- **The fix at home is to dig out the detail in advance.** The reasons behind what you did will not come back to you under a clock unless you have said them out loud before.

One limit to know. I have not heard your recordings. I am working from what you told me you said.

## Part 1: What happened in your deadline answer

### The question

> "Describe a project that required your attention for an extended period. How did you engage and ensure deadlines were met?"

### What you told me you said

> **Weak answer.** "To ensure I meet deadlines, I set mini deadlines to help, and I keep myself accountable for the overall deadline."

You already know this is weak. Here is exactly why.

### Why it is generic

**There is no project in it.** The question said "describe a project". Your answer is about deadlines in general.

**Anyone could say it.** A person who has never met a deadline in their life could say that sentence.

**Nothing in it happened.** It has no day, no person, no number and no problem.

### Two tests for any sentence

**The stranger test.** Could a stranger say this sentence without having done what you did? If yes, it is general.

**The camera test.** Could a camera have filmed it? "I set mini deadlines" cannot be filmed. "On the Monday I emailed all eighteen people one by one" can.

> **Remember.** A general sentence is a claim. A specific sentence is proof. The marker can only give marks for proof.

### What markers say they want

A recruitment manager at HMRC runs a team that marks thousands of interviews a month. He says the top marks go to your personal actions, what your thought process was, and why you made the decision.

So you were right. They do want the thinking behind it. "I set mini deadlines" has no thinking in it.

## Part 2: Why your mind goes blank

### You are searching too much

The question says "a project". Your memory holds hundreds of things that could count.

Looking through all of them in 30 seconds is too big a job. So nothing comes. That is the blank.

It is not a fault in you. It is what happens to anyone given a huge search and a short clock.

### Then you switch from remembering to inventing

When no memory comes, you start thinking about the topic. "Deadlines. What do people do about deadlines? They set smaller ones."

That is inventing. Inventing from a topic always gives the textbook answer, because the textbook answer is all a topic contains.

The real detail lives in one place only. It is inside the memory of something that happened.

### Why the "why" is hardest of all

At the time, you did not put your reasons into words. You just did the thing.

So the reasons are not sitting in your memory as sentences. Under a clock you cannot build them.

You have to put them into words once, at home, out loud. After that they come back.

> **Remember.** You cannot think up detail on the spot. Nobody can. People who sound specific are remembering something they have already talked through.

## Part 3: The fix on the day

Two questions, in this order.

### First question: "Which one?"

Do not ask yourself "what do I say?"

Ask "which one?" This means: which of my six real things fits this?

You will have six ready, each with a short nickname. So the search is six items, not hundreds. Part 5 shows you how to build them.

### Second question: "What happened?"

Now start telling it. Begin with the question's words and the name of the thing.

> **Say it like this.** "The project was…"

Saying the name of the real thing out loud pulls the rest of the memory up behind it.

### Then: one "for instance" after every general sentence

General sentences will still slip out. That is fine, as long as each one is followed straight away by what really happened.

> **Say it like this.** "I set smaller deadlines of my own. For instance, the hostel wanted its deposit by the first of March, so I told everyone to pay me by the fifteenth of February."

The first sentence is the claim. The second is the proof.

### A full example

Sam is made up. Sam is a final-year student and the treasurer of the university hiking society. Nothing here is about you.

The question is the same one you had.

> **Strong answer.** "The project was our society's Easter trip to the Lake District. Forty people, and it took about eight weeks to organise. I was the treasurer, so the money was my job.
>
> To stay engaged over those weeks, I kept one list of who had paid and updated it every evening. Seeing the number go up each day kept me on it.
>
> To make sure the deadlines were met, I worked back from the hostel. They wanted the deposit by the first of March. I knew people pay late, so I set my own deadline two weeks earlier, the fifteenth of February.
>
> On the fifteenth only twenty-two of the forty had paid. Reminders in the group chat were being ignored. So I stopped using the group chat and messaged the other eighteen one by one. I also let people pay in two halves, because three of them told me the full amount was the problem.
>
> By the twenty-seventh, thirty-eight had paid. I filled the last two places from the waiting list. The deposit went in two days early."

### What makes it specific

- **It names one project** in the first five words.
- **It has numbers and dates.** Forty people. The first of March. Twenty-two paid.
- **It has a problem.** Only twenty-two had paid. Real projects have problems. Generic answers never do.
- **It has reasons.** "I knew people pay late, so…" and "because three of them told me…".
- **It has a choice.** One by one, and not the group chat.

Sam also said "I set my own deadline". That is the same idea as your "mini deadlines". The difference is that Sam's has a date, a reason and a result.

## Part 4: How to add the thinking

The thinking is what you asked about. It comes from three small phrases.

### "Because"

After anything you did, add why.

> **Say it like this.** "I set my own deadline two weeks early, because people pay late."

### "And not"

Say what you chose against. This shows you made a decision.

> **Say it like this.** "I messaged people one by one, and not in the group chat, because group reminders were being ignored."

### "What went wrong was"

Say the moment it nearly failed, and what you did then.

> **Say it like this.** "What went wrong was that only twenty-two had paid by my deadline. So I…"

Most people hide the part that went wrong. Do the opposite. It is the most believable part of any answer, and it is where your thinking shows.

The same HMRC manager says this too. You do not have to make everything sound perfect. You can describe difficulties, as long as you explain how you overcame them.

> **Try it now.** Take this sentence: "I worked well with the other teams." Say it again three times. First add "because". Then add "and not". Then add "what went wrong was". Use something that really happened to you.

## Part 5: Preparing at home

This is where the real work is. It takes about twenty minutes for each real thing. You do it once.

### Step 1: Pick six real things and give each a nickname

These are possibilities from your own life. Only you know which have the best detail inside them.

- **Fifty markets.** Moving 50 market openings forward by chasing documents.
- **The portal.** Building the portal that replaced the 400-line spreadsheet.
- **The inbox.** Running the client inbox for US institutional clients.
- **The target model.** Modelling sales targets at the software company.
- **The sixty-day plan.** The plan for bringing new clients on board.
- **The pitch.** Your Urban Outfitters pitch in the student investment fund.
- **A debate.** One round that went badly, and what you changed.
- **Head Boy.** One thing that happened, not the title.

Pick six. Try to include one that went wrong and one from outside work.

### Step 2: Dig each one out, out loud

Answer these questions out loud, on your own, and record it on your phone. Do not write full sentences.

1. What was it? When? How many weeks?
2. Who else was involved? Use first names.
3. What was the hard part? When could it have failed?
4. What did you do? List five things. Each one starts with "I".
5. For each of the five: why that? What else could you have done?
6. What went wrong? What did you do then?
7. How did it end? What can you point to?
8. What would you do differently?

Question 5 is the one that matters most. It is where the "why" gets put into words for the first time.

### Step 3: Put it on a card in key words

One card for each real thing. Key words only. Never full sentences.

- The nickname.
- Two numbers.
- Five things you did.
- One "because" for each.
- What went wrong.
- How it ended.

### Step 4: Find the hooks

One real thing can answer many questions. But only if you have already found the right moment inside it.

For each card, find the moment that fits each of these:

- deadlines
- pressure
- working with others
- persuading someone
- a mistake or a setback
- doing something nobody asked for
- learning something new fast

Write the hook words on the card.

This matters a lot. In experiments, people use a story they already know for a new problem less than a third of the time. Once someone points out that it fits, most of them use it. Finding the hooks at home is how you point it out to yourself in advance.

### Step 5: Say each one three times in different words

Not to learn the words. To prove you can tell it without them.

If it comes out the same each time, you are reciting. Change the first sentence and go again.

## Part 6: Your deadline answer, rebuilt

"Fifty markets" could fit the deadline question, if it ran for several weeks.

I do not know what happened inside it. So here are the questions that would turn your answer from general to specific. Answer them out loud.

- How many weeks did it run?
- What was the deadline, and who set it?
- You said you set mini deadlines. What was one of them? What day? For which bank or which document?
- Why did you set it there?
- Which bank or document was the slowest?
- What did you do about that one? Did you phone, email, or tell your manager?
- Why that way and not another?
- How did you keep track? A list? A count?
- How many were ready by the end?

Now look at your original sentence again.

> **Weak answer.** "I set mini deadlines to help, and I keep myself accountable for the overall deadline."

And the shape it could take once you have the answers. Square brackets are things only you know.

> **Strong answer.** "The project was getting 50 market openings ready to trade, over [number] weeks last summer. To make sure the deadlines were met, I set my own date for each one. For instance, [one real mini deadline, with the day and what it was for]. I set it there because [your reason]. What went wrong was [the slow one]. So I [what you did], and not [what you chose against], because [why]. By the end, [how many] were ready."

It is the same idea you had. Now it has a day, a reason, a problem and a result.

## Part 7: Three short drills

Ten minutes a day is enough.

### The zoom drill

Say a general sentence. Then zoom in three times.

1. "I'm organised."
2. Which time? "On the Easter trip."
3. What exactly? "I kept one list of who had paid."
4. Why? "Because I needed to see every evening how far off we were."

Do it with five different general sentences.

### The "which one?" drill

Read a question. Say only the nickname of the real thing you would use. Nothing else.

Do ten in a row. This trains the first two seconds, which is where the blank happens.

Use the [HireVue Practice Room](https://claude.ai/artifact/FbfrGNoYHcAxtKQ4P8QWt8). After each answer, look at the box called "General statements that prove nothing on their own".

It lists the general sentences it heard. Your aim is for that box to be empty.

## The card

**When the question appears:**

1. Read to the last word. Count the asks.
2. Ask "which one?" Pick a nickname.
3. Start with "The project was…" or "The time was…".

**While you speak:**

- After a general sentence, say "for instance".
- After something you did, say "because".
- Say what went wrong and what you did.

**Never:**

- Ask yourself "what should I say?"
- Answer about the topic. Answer about the thing that happened.
