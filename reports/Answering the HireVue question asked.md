# Answer the question they actually asked

Your own diagnosis is probably right, and the research can say why it happens to prepared candidates in particular. A question on screen that looks like one you have rehearsed calls up the rehearsed answer before you have finished reading, and the answer you then give is fluent, true and aimed at a neighbouring question. In the one study that counted, candidates in real interviews gave the specific story that "tell me about a time" asks for only 23% of the time. Your hours of firm research do not rescue this, because research produces facts about the firm while every question collects evidence about you. The repair is a skill you already have under another name: in British Parliamentary debating you take an unseen motion, work out what kind of motion it is and what you must prove, and build a case in 15 minutes. Decoding an interview question is the same act compressed into 30 seconds. Here is each of your questions answered in brief, with how far to trust the answer.

- **What is each question trying to assess?** Each one collects a different kind of evidence about you. "Tell me about yourself" collects selected achievements and how clearly you communicate; "why this firm" collects a preference that only this firm satisfies; "why this role" collects your understanding of the job and a match to things you have done; a scenario collects the decision you would make and the order of your steps. Trust: moderate. It rests on published marking schemes from the UK Civil Service and US public bodies and on named bank recruiters quoted in 2012 and 2013. No finance firm publishes a marked model answer, so every firm-specific reading in this guide is inference.
- **What headspace do you need?** Aim to give the marker what this question asks for, using the first workable material the question itself brings to mind, and stop trying to deliver the best answer you have ever written. High standards are fine; fear of falling short of a perfect script is what the research links to worse results. Trust: moderate for the general finding, weak for interviews, because the studies come from education, sport and firefighting.
- **How do you decode a question in 30 seconds?** Read for five things in a fixed order: the type (from the opening phrase), the skill word, the number of parts, any twist, and any printed instruction. Then choose material and compose only your first sentence. Almost all of the work has to be done before the interview so that the window is recognition and choice. Trust: the method is built from exam, advocacy and debating practice and from how the research measures this skill; it has not been tested in interviews.
- **Is scripting the perfect answer the problem?** Very likely part of it. A scripted answer is the strongest thing in your memory, so it wins the race against a careful reading. Trust: strong that the mechanism exists (chess, exam and reading experiments); untested on interview candidates.
- **Why do hours of firm research not convert?** They feed two of roughly eleven question types, and even there the facts are supporting proof, not the answer. Trust: this is my analysis of what the published marking schemes collect; it is logic more than data.
- **How much do smiling, charisma, jokes and sounding natural matter?** Little, and less than answering the right question. More smiling lowered hiring in the one experiment on a serious job, a joke helps only when the listener visibly laughs, and impressions of competence carried into ratings where warmth did not. Trust: small studies, mostly not recorded interviews.
- **A correction.** The first guide told you to write four keywords inside a 30-second window. The arithmetic in this guide shows that is unrealistic for the long questions firms now set. The replacement is in the section on the window.

## Why a well-prepared candidate ends up answering a different question

A **neighbouring question** is one that shares its topic or its opening words with the question on your screen but asks for something different. "Tell me about a time you led a team" and "tell me about a time you contributed to a team without being the leader" are neighbours. So are "why this firm" and "why this role". A Goldman Sachs graduate recruitment manager named the failure in 2013 as a reason for rejection: "candidates answer the question they hope to be asked, rather than the one that is posed to them" ([eFinancialCareers](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2013/11/graduate-recruiters-for-the-u-s-asia-and-europe-at-macquarie-ubs-and-goldman-sachs-give-their-top-tips-on-getting-into-banking-now)).

### It has been counted: a real story 23% of the time

A **past-behaviour question** asks for one real event from your past ("tell me about a time when..."). Researchers recorded 62 real job interviews in Switzerland and coded every response to questions of this kind. **Candidates produced an actual story only 23% of the time.** The commonest response, in 76% of cases, was what the authors call a pseudo-story: "a description of a generic situation or recurrent set of similar situations, without unity of time or action". Statements of values or opinions appeared in 29% of responses and plain self-descriptions ("I am someone who...") in 29%; one response can contain more than one of these. Stories and pseudo-stories raised hiring recommendations and self-descriptions lowered them ([Bangerter et al. 2014](https://libra.unine.ch/bitstreams/c09ffd04-9bea-4a69-969b-d3c4ac890a0c/download)). A later sample by some of the same researchers is reported as finding stories in 50% of responses; I saw that figure only in a search excerpt ([Brosy et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7379714)). These were live interviews for other jobs. Nobody has published the equivalent count for UK one-way finance interviews.

People who mark written answers for a living describe the same thing. Examiners for the ICAEW accountancy apprenticeship, an exam they call "a written competency based interview", report that "a common reason for candidates scoring low marks for a question relates to them describing generic rather than specific situations", and their checklist includes "Make sure they answer the right question" and "Check that they have answered all of the questions" ([ICAEW examiners' report](https://icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/learning-and-development/apprenticeships/level-7-examiners-report-august-2022.ashx)). A recruiter writing about graduates' video interviews describes scripted candidates who "acknowledge the new question, but continue to ignore answering the question because they feel their pre-response will be better... but it's not the answer I was waiting for" ([Undercover Recruiter](https://theundercoverrecruiter.com/video-interview-tips-for-graduates/)). And one of your target firms prints the warning on screen: M&G's 2027 internship assessment, as captured by a candidate, includes the sentence "Please remember to answer both questions in your response" ([FinanceFluency, M&G](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/m-and-g?p=summer)). A firm adds that line when it has watched second questions go unanswered.

### Four ways to miss a question

"Not answering the question" is four different errors. Knowing which one you make decides what you practise.

| Error | What it looks like | Example from reported questions |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong form | A habit or opinion where one event was asked for; or a story where a method or a decision was asked for | A single anecdote for "What strategies do you use to maintain your composure and effectiveness under pressure?" |
| Missing part | The headline is answered and a later ask is not | Answering what motivates you and skipping "and what drives you?" |
| Ignored twist | The answer breaks a limiting word in the question | A leadership story for "a time you contributed to a team without being the leader" |
| Substituted topic | The prepared story for a neighbouring skill | A teamwork story for a question about a team member who was not contributing |

### Five mechanisms, and where each has been shown

Five mechanisms from psychology each predict your failure. Only fragments have been tested on interview candidates, so each paragraph ends by saying where its evidence comes from.

**A familiar-looking question triggers the expected task.** Researchers at Cambridge's exam board studied why pupils lose marks on questions they know how to answer. Their conclusion: "a student may end up answering the question that they expected to be asked rather than the question that the examiner was trying to ask." Readers "will read what they expect to read", and "even the first few words of a sentence or question can trigger further expectations of what will be read next". The effect grows with preparation: "The more familiar the style or scenario of the question, the more powerful the schema that is evoked by it and the stronger the expectations of what is required." A schema here means a ready-made mental template for a task. They add that the effect is strongest in an exam, "where students are often anxious to 'recognise' the tasks and get on with answering" ([Sweiry et al. 2002](https://www.cambridgeassessment.org.uk/Images/109695-tales-of-the-expected-the-influence-of-students-expectations-on-exam-validity.pdf)). In a follow-up with 339 pupils aged 15 and 16, rewording three of four science questions to break the expectation raised scores; on the fourth the rewording barely helped, which the authors read as a sign that "in some cases, students' expectations are too strong to be suppressed successfully during an exam" ([Crisp et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/00131880801920445)). This is direct evidence of the failure, in exams and not in interviews.

**Mental set.** Psychologists call it the Einstellung effect: a solution you already know blocks a better one. Chess experts who had spotted a familiar winning sequence said they were still searching for something better, "but their eye movements showed that they continued to look at features of the problem related to the solution they had already thought of" ([Bilalić et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2008.05.005)). The familiar idea cut their problem-solving to the level of players three standard deviations weaker, which is a very large drop ([Sheridan & Reingold 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/)). The same paper found the exception that matters to you: when the familiar move was an obvious blunder, the effect "was severely reduced". People drop a prepared idea when it plainly does not fit and cling to it when it nearly fits. This is chess. Nobody has run it on interview answers. It does match your report of realising only afterwards that the answer missed.

**Partial-match reading.** People do not read questions word by word to the end. Asked "How many animals of each kind did Moses take on the Ark?", most people answer "two" without noticing that the Ark was Noah's ([Erickson & Mattson 1981](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371%2881%2990165-1)). A later paper reports that people who demonstrably knew the right fact still gave the wrong answer about 52% of the time, and about 26.5% of the time when their attention was directed to the critical word; I took those figures from a secondary description ([Memory & Cognition 2021](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-020-01128-z)). The error vanishes when the swapped word is unrelated, such as Nixon for Noah ([Kamas et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03201094)). So the risk is highest when the question is a near neighbour of a prepared one. This is laboratory work on trivia questions, used here as an analogy.

**Goal neglect.** "In goal neglect, a person ignores some task requirement though being able to describe it." The cause is the total complexity of the instructions being carried, not how busy the moment is: as the set grows, "vulnerable components may be lost" ([Duncan et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.137.1.131)). If you go into a recording telling yourself to smile, use the structure, mention the firm's strategy, hit the keywords and watch the clock, the one instruction that matters most, answer this question, competes with all the others. Laboratory tasks, analogy.

**Anxiety and attention.** A leading theory holds that anxiety weakens goal-directed attention and specifically harms inhibition (stopping an automatic response) and shifting (switching to a different one) ([Eysenck et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/1528-3542.7.2.336)). A **meta-analysis** is a study that pools the results of many studies. One, covering 58 studies and 8,292 people, found that anxious people have weaker attentional control by a moderate margin ([Shi et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2019.101754)); another, covering 22,061 people, found that anxiety goes with lower working memory, the mental workspace you use to hold a question's parts while you choose a story ([Moran 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000051)). Stopping the prepared answer and switching to a different story are exactly the two operations the window demands. These are laboratory studies of anxious people in general, not of interview candidates.

Only two findings come from interviews themselves. The Swiss count above is one. The other: in a set of real interviews, longer delays before answering predicted both a higher chance of an inappropriate first response and lower ratings ([Brosy et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2016.1150769)). Everything else is analogy, though it is consistent analogy from five separate directions.

Three things reduced the error in experiments: drawing attention to the critical word, noticing explicitly that the new item differs from the old one ([Wahlheim & Jacoby 2013](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0246-9)), and keeping the question in view. All three are an explicit comparison between the question as asked and the answer about to be given. The decoding method later in this guide is built on that comparison.

### Does a reviewer notice a fluent answer to the neighbouring question?

Sometimes not, which is why the habit survives, and usually yes under the conditions of a marked interview. In four experiments with 1,139 participants, "listeners can fail to detect dodges when speakers answer similar, but objectively incorrect, questions", and listeners rated a fluent answer to a similar question more positively than a hesitant answer to the right one. But when the question text stayed on screen during the answer, detection "more than doubled, from 39 percent without the text to 88 percent with the text", and listeners who noticed rated the speaker lower ([Rogers & Norton 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023439)) ([American Psychological Association release](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/05/110505111945.htm)). The clips were debate-style answers, not job interviews.

A HireVue reviewer rates your answers one question at a time, as the first guide describes, and is marking against a named skill. That is closer to the question-in-view condition than to casual listening. Whether the question text sits beside your video on the reviewer's screen is not documented. Two conclusions follow. First, do not expect a near-miss to pass a reviewer who is checking relevance. Second, even when nobody notices the swap, the evidence the question was collecting is missing from your answer, so the mark is low for a reason the reviewer may never put into words. Debating may have trained the habit. A debate audience often listens for fluency and force, the conditions in which near-misses were rewarded, and one coach of American limited-preparation speaking complains that competitors bring "examples that were neatly packaged weeks earlier" and fit them to any prompt ([Richardson 2010](https://cornerstone.lib.mnsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1021&context=ndcieproceedings)). That is one coach's opinion with no data.

### Why hours of firm research do not turn into marks

Firm research produces **facts about the firm**: what it does, its strategy, its recent results, its values. An interview question collects **evidence about the candidate**: what you did, what you would do, what you want and whether you understand the job. Those are different materials. The table shows, for each question type in this guide, what the marker is collecting and what your research can supply.

| Question | Evidence it collects | What firm research supplies |
|---|---|---|
| Tell me about yourself | Your selected achievements | Nothing, beyond which achievements to select |
| Why this firm | Your preference, and proof it is informed | One or two distinguishing facts |
| Why this role | Your understanding of the job and your match to it | What the team does day to day |
| Why this industry | Your history of commitment | Nothing |
| What motivates you; strengths | What energises you, with examples | Nothing |
| Past behaviour | One real event and your actions | Only which values to expect |
| Scenario and integrity | Your decision and ordered steps | The firm's wording on speaking up |
| Commercial awareness | A view, linked to the firm | The firm's side of the link |

Research feeds two question types directly (why this firm, and the firm half of a commercial question) and one partly (why this role). Even there the facts are supporting proof. A generic public-sector marking scheme puts research in its top band as one marker among several: "All points addressed. All points relevant. Good examples. Candidate displayed that (s)he did research for the position and the place of employment" ([Oregon Employment Department](https://www.oregon.gov/employ/agency/documents/interview-scoring-2023-09.pdf)). Research is a tick inside a top answer. It is not the answer.

The research also does harm in three ways. It produces a polished why-firm script, which becomes the strongest response in your memory and so the one most likely to be delivered to any neighbouring question. It adds to the list of things you are trying to remember to say, which is the condition for goal neglect. And it tilts you towards saying what you believe the firm wants to hear. In a study of entrepreneurs pitching to investors, "catering harmed investors' evaluations, while being authentic improved them", and catering raised the speaker's anxiety ([Gino et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.01.003)). Treat that study with caution: another paper by its first author in the same journal has been retracted, and I checked this one only at summary level.

None of this means stop researching. Researchers who study how candidates work out what a question is marking list "information about the company" among the clues candidates use ([Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)). Research earns marks when it is pointed at two things: which skills this firm's questions will collect, and which one or two facts prove that your reasons belong to this firm. The practice section gives a fixed list and a time cap.

## A self-test to confirm this is your problem before you rebuild

Run these before changing your preparation. The first guide's diagnosis asked whether your problem was content, delivery or something outside the video. These tests ask a narrower question: do your answers match the questions? They are my constructions from the research procedures cited; none is a validated test.

**Test 1: guess the question.** Draw five questions from the bank later in this guide: one with a twist, one with three or more parts, one "how do you", one scenario and one motivation question. Record an answer to each with 30 seconds to prepare and one take. Give a listener the recordings, or typed transcripts, without the questions. Ask them to write down the exact question they think each answer was responding to, including any parts. Then compare. Score each answer on four points: did they get the type (a past event, a method, a decision, a reason), the skill, the number of parts, and the twist? The logic comes from the experiment above: a listener who cannot see the question is the easiest audience to fool, so if even they reconstruct a different question, a marker with the question in front of them will certainly see the gap.

**Test 2: the part-by-part transcript check.** For each recording, write the question's parts as a numbered list. Mark in the transcript the sentences that answer each part. A part with no sentence was not answered. Then check your first sentence: does it contain the question's own key words and its limiting word? Finally count the seconds before the first sentence that addresses the question. ICAEW markers work this way, assessing each submission "against each question's five sub-requirements" ([ICAEW examiners' report](https://icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/learning-and-development/apprenticeships/level-7-examiners-report-august-2022.ashx)).

**Test 3: what was it marking?** Straight after each recording, before you look at anything, write one skill word you think the question was marking and two behaviours that would show it. Compare with the firm's published values and the question's own wording. This copies the research procedure for measuring the ability to identify criteria, described in the practice section. People score higher on the specific questions whose target they identified ([Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)), so check whether your weakest answers are the ones where you named the wrong skill.

**Test 4: timed against untimed.** Repeat Test 1 on five fresh questions with two minutes to prepare. If the mismatches disappear, your reading is sound and the window is the problem.

| Result | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The listener writes a neighbouring question for two or more of five | Substituted topic: prepared answers are capturing you | Drop word-for-word scripts; decoding and re-angling drills |
| Right headline, missing parts | Missing part | Count parts on the first read; one spoken signpost per part |
| Right skill, twist absent | Ignored twist | Twist-word drill; put the twist in your first sentence |
| Story given where a method or decision was asked, or the reverse | Wrong form | Learn the decoder table of opening phrases |
| Mismatches cluster on neighbours of your scripted answers | Mental set | Hold stories as components, not scripts |
| Wrong skill named in Test 3 on your weakest answers | You are misreading the target | The criteria-identification drill |
| Fine with two minutes, poor with 30 seconds | The window, not the reading | The window routines and practice under mild pressure |
| Everything matches and every part is covered | This is not your main problem | Return to the first guide's diagnosis: thin content, or causes outside the video |

## What each question is really asking

A structured interview question is written backwards. The designer starts with a **competency**, a named skill or behaviour the job needs, and drafts a question that can only be answered with evidence of it. The US Office of Personnel Management (OPM), which publishes the fullest public manual on this, tells question writers: "Each question should measure at least one of the specified competencies" ([OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)). Before any candidate is seen, the designer also writes a **scoring key**: a description of what a low, middle and high answer looks like. A university HR department explains why keys exist: "Too often, panel members have varying opinions of what constitutes a 'good' answer to the interview question" ([University of South Carolina HR](https://www.biomedical.sc.edu/about/offices_and_divisions/human_resources/docs/rating_guide.pdf)).

So each question has a target, and the wording points at it. **No finance firm publishes a scoring key or a marked model answer.** The keys quoted below come from the OPM, a US university, a US state agency and the UK Civil Service. Where I say what separates a top answer from a middle one at a bank or asset manager, that is my inference from those keys and from recruiters' statements, and I label it. Most of the named recruiter quotes date from 2012 and 2013.

Each subsection gives the same seven things: what is assessed, the nearest published key, top against middle, what counts as evidence, the neighbouring question, the wording cues, and the shape of the answer.

### Tell me about yourself, and the CV walk-through

**Assessed.** Your experience and achievements, and how you handle an open question. Warwick's careers service describes the interviewer's purpose as "a sense of who you are, the person behind the interview mask" and "a good way to see how you respond to such an open, unstructured question" ([Warwick careers](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/careers/blog/how_to_answer_tell_me_about_yourself_in_an_interview)).

**Nearest published key.** No key written for this question was found anywhere. The UK Civil Service definition of an experience question is the closest: assessors look for "previous examples of things you have achieved", "your knowledge in a particular field which is relevant to the job role" and "How you performed in a particular field - not how long you served" ([gov.uk, Experience](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-experience)). A generic key used for open questions gives the top mark to "All points addressed. All points relevant. Good examples" and the middle mark to "Some points covered. Relevant information given. Some examples given" ([Oregon Employment Department](https://www.oregon.gov/employ/agency/documents/interview-scoring-2023-09.pdf)).

**Top against middle (inference).** The middle answer is an accurate tour of your CV in date order. The top answer is a selection: two or three achievements chosen because this role needs them, each with one fact, and every part of the question covered.

**Evidence.** Achievements, not job titles or durations.

**Neighbouring question.** "Read me your CV", and for you, "why this firm". There is a second trap. The wording candidates report is usually two questions joined by "and": "Tell us about yourself and why you feel you are a good candidate" (Goldman Sachs), "Can you tell me about yourself and why you are interested in this program and/or business?" (Bank of America), "Please introduce yourself and tell us what areas or roles interest you most at UBS, and why?" (UBS). The neighbour is the one-part version you rehearsed.

**Cues.** "Tell us about yourself", "introduce yourself", "walk me through". Then look for "and".

**Shape.** Warwick's advice is to move "from the personal to the professional and the general to the specific" and to give a "trailer", not a "director's cut". One sentence on who you are and what you are aiming at; two or three selected achievements; then the second ask under its own spoken signpost ("As for why I am a good candidate for this role...").

### Why this firm

**Assessed.** **Motivational fit** is the term designers use for how well a job, an organisation and a location match what drives a candidate; the consultancy DDI splits it into job fit, organisation fit and location fit ([DDI](https://www.ddiworld.com/blog/motivational-fit)). "Why this firm" collects organisation fit. Recruiters say what they listen for. Macquarie's regional recruitment lead: "being able to articulate why they want to work at Macquarie versus another firm is key." UBS's head of graduate training in Asia-Pacific: "Get a sense of who we are, how we operate as an organisation and what our current and future priorities are", and "We frequently see applicants who cut and paste cover letters without customisation" ([eFinancialCareers](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2013/11/graduate-recruiters-for-the-u-s-asia-and-europe-at-macquarie-ubs-and-goldman-sachs-give-their-top-tips-on-getting-into-banking-now)).

**Nearest published key.** None exists for this question. The generic key's top band includes "Candidate displayed that (s)he did research for the position and the place of employment" ([Oregon Employment Department](https://www.oregon.gov/employ/agency/documents/interview-scoring-2023-09.pdf)). The recruiter Hays describes the failing answer as one that "could be given to any employer for any job" ([Hays](https://www.hays.com.au/career-advice/interview-tips/why-do-you-want-work-here)).

**Top against middle (inference).** The middle answer is true, admiring and portable. The top answer contains a preference of yours, a fact showing that this firm satisfies it and a named alternative does not, and something from your history that proves the preference is real.

**Evidence.** The subject of the answer is you. The firm fact is the proof that you have looked.

**Neighbouring question.** "Tell us what you know about our firm." This is the question your hours of research answer, and it is not the one asked. The other neighbour is "why this industry".

**Cues.** The firm's name after "why"; any comparison phrase: "versus", "among its competitors" (Morgan Stanley), "set us apart from other global investment banks" (UBS), "what it is about NatWest Group that made you want to apply" (NatWest).

**Shape.** "Two reasons", then each reason in three beats: what you want, the fact that shows it is here, your proof. Where the wording contains a comparison, name the comparison out loud.

### Why this role or division

**Assessed.** Job fit, and knowledge of the business area. UBS's recruiter: "you need to demonstrate your knowledge and focus on the business area for which you have applied" ([eFinancialCareers](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2013/11/graduate-recruiters-for-the-u-s-asia-and-europe-at-macquarie-ubs-and-goldman-sachs-give-their-top-tips-on-getting-into-banking-now)). Schroders says its assessments look at "what drives you, how you work with others, and how your strengths align to the role and our values"; I saw that sentence only as a search excerpt of Schroders' page ([Schroders graduate FAQs](https://www.schroders.com/en-gb/uk/individual/about-us/careers/graduates/faqs/)).

**Nearest published key.** None. The clearest statement of the two halves is a question reported from Schroders: "What is your understanding of the role, and why are you excited about it?" ([FinanceFluency, Schroders](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/schroders?p=summer)).

**Top against middle (inference).** The middle answer describes the division in brochure terms. The top answer says what a junior person in that seat produces in a normal week, then pairs two of those tasks with things you have done and liked.

**Evidence.** An accurate account of the work, a match from your own record, and a reason the interest will last.

**Neighbouring question.** "Why this firm." For you this is the costliest swap in the guide. You are applying to client solutions and change management roles in Goldman Sachs asset management and to private wealth management. A polished account of Goldman's standing says nothing about why you want to manage change projects or serve wealthy clients.

**Cues.** "This role", "this position", "this programme", "business area", "what skills would help you succeed", "specifically, for the long term".

**Shape.** "My understanding of the role is [who the team serves and what it produces, in one sentence]. Two parts of it match what I have done." Your summer in client servicing and onboarding is direct material for a client-facing role, and the questionnaire portal and the 60-day onboarding plan are possible material for change management, if the facts of each support it.

### Why this industry

**Assessed.** Commitment to the sector. Goldman's then global head of graduate recruiting: "we do not expect candidates to be experts, but they do need to be keen." Credit Suisse's campus head: "We're looking at long-term potential, and we seek enthusiastic people", and candidates fail "by showing a lack of interest in the industry or position" ([eFinancialCareers](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2012/10/qa-with-graduate-recruiters-from-emea-apac-and-the-us)). A long-running banking guide puts the marker's doubt this way: "Did you suddenly 'get interested' in finance, or have you made a long-term commitment to the field?" ([Mergers & Inquisitions](https://mergersandinquisitions.com/walk-me-through-your-resume/)).

**Nearest published key.** None.

**Top against middle (inference).** The middle answer asserts interest. The top answer shows a sequence of choices, each of which makes sense only if the interest is real.

**Neighbouring question.** "Why this firm", or "describe the industry".

**Cues.** "Why asset management", "why wealth management", "what drives your interest in a career in", and the same test from the other side: "If you do not land a role in investment banking, what alternative would you pursue?", reported from Morgan Stanley ([Leland, reposted by a university careers office](https://frankecareer.nau.edu/blog/2025/02/07/morgan-stanley-hirevue-questions-and-how-to-answer-them/)).

**Shape.** The spark, then what you did next, and next, then what you want from here. You have a sequence to draw on (bookkeeping work experience, the student fund, the placement year, the summer at the asset manager, the spring week): [put them in true order and say what each one changed]. Because you are applying to asset management, wealth management and banking, each needs its own honest version.

### What motivates you

**Assessed.** This is a **strengths-based question**: it asks what you naturally do well and enjoy, where a competency question asks what you have done. The Civil Service defines strengths as "the things we do regularly, do well and that motivate us" ([gov.uk, candidate overview](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-candidate-overview)). The assessment firm Cappfinity, as quoted by Warwick, says "a strength is something we perform well at, is energising and we do often" ([Warwick careers](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/careers/blog/how_to_answer_strengths_based_questions_in_interviews)). A third-party guide says M&G runs its video interview through Cappfinity ([Practice Aptitude Tests](https://www.practiceaptitudetests.com/top-employer-profiles/mg/)), and Legal & General describes "strength based behavioural questions" in its online assessment ([Legal & General](https://careers.legalandgeneral.com/our-recruitment-process-for-future-talent)).

**Nearest published key.** Warwick's account of what strengths assessors read, as summarised by the tool I used to fetch the page: generic answers with few specific actions are weaker; several specific actions are better; several specific actions described in detail with clear energy and enthusiasm are best. The Civil Service tells candidates there are "no right or wrong answers" and "Do not rehearse your answers - because we are looking for your initial response" ([gov.uk, Strengths](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-strengths)).

**Top against middle.** Detail of what you do, and visible enjoyment. This is the one question type whose designers set out to defeat preparation, so a scripted answer is the thing the format is built to see through.

**Evidence.** Something you do often, do well and enjoy, shown by a specific recent example.

**Neighbouring question.** "What have you achieved?" or "why this firm".

**Cues.** "What motivates you", "what drives you", "what do you enjoy", "how do you". BlackRock's reported "What motivates you, and what drives you?" is read by the site that collected it as two questions: "an answer that gives one has answered half the question" ([FinanceFluency, BlackRock](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/blackrock?p=spring)). That wording is a candidate's recollection and the two-part reading is the site's.

**Shape.** Name the motivator plainly, give the most recent time you felt it, say how it shows up in what you choose to do, and stop.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Assessed.** How accurately you see yourself, and what you did about it. Goldman's global head of graduate recruiting: "The best candidates have developed their sense of self-awareness" and "There is a fine line between self-confidence and arrogance" ([eFinancialCareers](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2012/10/qa-with-graduate-recruiters-from-emea-apac-and-the-us)). A Goldman recruiter lists as a failure "coming across as either over or underconfident" ([eFinancialCareers](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2013/11/graduate-recruiters-for-the-u-s-asia-and-europe-at-macquarie-ubs-and-goldman-sachs-give-their-top-tips-on-getting-into-banking-now)).

**Nearest published key.** None. The nearest firm wording is in UBS's published behaviours: "I actively seek and provide feedback" and "I learn from every success and failure" ([UBS, Our culture](https://www.ubs.com/global/en/our-firm/our-culture.html)).

**Top against middle (inference).** The middle answer offers a safe weakness and a promise. The top answer names a real pattern, one occasion when it cost something, the change you made, and a sign that the change is holding. What is marked is the accuracy of the self-assessment, not which weakness you pick.

**Neighbouring question.** For a weakness: "tell me a strength" (the disguised strength), or "tell me about a failure" (a failure is one event; a weakness is a pattern). For a strength: a list of adjectives, the self-description that lowered hiring recommendations in the Swiss study.

**Cues.** "Strength", "weakness", "area to develop", "a gap in your skills or knowledge" (JPMorgan), "what skills would help you succeed" (Goldman Sachs wealth management), "name one or two skills" (EY), where the count is itself a limit.

**Shape.** Strength: name it, one event that shows it, how often you use it. Weakness: name it, one occasion, what you changed, evidence since.

### Something not on your CV

**Assessed.** The person behind the CV. Fidelity International says its first-round video questions "may revolve around your interests and hobbies", that it wants a sense of your "natural abilities" and that the interview is "not about catching you out" ([Fidelity International](https://careers.fidelityinternational.com/working-here-overview/our-hiring-process/)). St. James's Place says its recruiters review videos by hand and want candidates to "showcase their own authentic voices and experiences" ([St. James's Place](https://www.sjp.co.uk/careers/students-and-graduates/application-process)).

**Nearest published key.** None. The strengths definitions above are the closest.

**Top against middle (inference).** The middle answer names an interest. The top answer gives one concrete detail that could only come from real involvement and says what it shows.

**Neighbouring question.** "Tell me about your best achievement." The limiting words exclude your strongest prepared stories, and that is their purpose.

**Cues.** "Not on your CV" (Deutsche Bank), "outside of school or work" (UBS), "outside of education and work" (St. James's Place, from a Glassdoor excerpt of unclear date).

**Shape.** The thing, one concrete detail, what it shows or taught, and a link to the firm only if the question asks for one. Read the limit literally. Your CV lists boarding pass collecting and spice blending, so for "not on your CV" those are excluded as headlines, though [a skill you have that the CV does not mention] is not. For "outside of education and work" they are allowed. Debating runs through your university, so if you use it, say in your first sentence how you are reading "outside".

### Past-behaviour questions ("tell me about a time")

**Assessed.** One named competency, through one real event. The premise in the OPM manual is that "the best predictor of future behavior on the job is past behavior under similar circumstances" ([OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)).

**Nearest published key.** OPM publishes a full key for the question "Describe a situation in which you had to deal with individuals who were difficult, hostile, or distressed. Who was involved? What specific actions did you take and what was the result?" The top level is "Applies the competency in exceptionally difficult situations. Serves as a key resource and advises others"; the middle is "Applies the competency in difficult situations. Requires occasional guidance"; the bottom is "Applies the competency in the simplest situations. Requires close and extensive guidance" ([OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)).

**Top against middle.** The levels are defined by how hard the situation was and how much help you needed. They are not defined by eloquence. A tidy story about a low-stakes event is capped near the middle however well you tell it, so choosing the example is a marking decision. A 2024 study reports that only about 41% to 48% of candidates describe one of their best performances when asked a standard question of this kind; I saw that figure in a summary only ([Personnel Assessment and Decisions 2024](https://doaj.org/article/db6fe9dfb112448497863e3a2acd6f0d)).

**Evidence.** A single incident: the context, what you did, and the result.

**Neighbouring question.** Three of them. "How do you usually handle this?", which produces the pseudo-story. The question about the skill next door. And the headline of the question without its listed parts.

**Cues.** "Tell me about a time", "describe a situation", "give an example", "share an experience", "when have you". Superlatives such as "best illustrates" and "most proud" are put there deliberately: OPM tells writers to "use superlative adjectives in the questions (e.g., most, last, worst, least) to help the candidate focus on specific incidents".

**Shape.** A first sentence that contains the skill word and any twist, then the story in the STAR order the first guide sets out (situation, task, action, result), with each listed sub-question answered under its own spoken signpost in the order asked.

### Hypothetical scenarios ("what would you do if")

**Assessed.** Judgement, read from what you say you would do. The premise is that "a person's intentions are closely tied to his/her actual behavior" ([OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)). The psychologist who developed this format says each question must contain a dilemma, "having to choose between two or more exclusive courses of action", and that questions where most people give the same answer are thrown out during testing ([Latham & Itzchakov 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.674815/full)). So if a scenario seems to have one obvious answer, the obvious answer is probably the middle mark.

**Nearest published key.** OPM's list of follow-up questions for a scenario shows everything a marker wants covered: "What do you consider the most critical issue in this situation?", "What is the first thing you would do?", "What factors would affect your course of action?", "How do you think your action would be received?", "What would you do if your action was not received well?" ([OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)).

**Top against middle (inference).** Take two urgent deadlines that clash. A low answer picks one silently or promises both. A middle answer says "I would prioritise by importance". A high answer finds out the true deadlines and who is waiting, tells both people early, lets the seniors settle the priority if you cannot, proposes a workable plan, then delivers and confirms. In a live interview the interviewer asks those follow-ups. In a recording nobody does, so an answer that stops at "I would tell my manager" leaves the higher marks unsaid.

**Evidence.** The choice, the order of steps, who you tell, and your reasoning.

**Neighbouring question.** "Tell me about a time when this happened." A past story is optional support here. It is not the answer.

**Cues.** "What would you do", "how would you handle", "imagine", "you are working on", "what steps would you take". Watch for a second ask: "who would you speak to", "with your team and/or the client".

**Shape.** The critical issue in one sentence, your first action, the next steps in order with who is told, what would change your course, and what you would do if the first step did not work.

### Integrity and ethics dilemmas

**Assessed.** Whether you would act honestly and at once when it costs you something. In UK financial services this has a legal backbone. The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), the UK regulator, sets Individual Conduct Rules that staff at regulated firms must follow, junior staff included:

> "1. You must act with integrity." "2. You must act with due skill, care and diligence." "3. You must be open and cooperative with the FCA, the PRA and other regulators." "4. You must pay due regard to the interests of customers and treat them fairly." "5. You must observe proper standards of market conduct." "6. You must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers." ([FCA, Conduct Rules](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/senior-managers-and-certification-regime/conduct-rules))

The rules separate two things. Making a mistake is a matter for Rule 2, care. Hiding it is a matter for Rule 1, integrity. FCA guidance, which I could read only in excerpts, lists misleading by act or omission as the first kind of integrity breach and says a person does not lack integrity merely because they acted without due care ([FCA Handbook, COCON 4.1](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/cocon4/cocon4s1)). A scenario about a mistake is therefore testing what you do next.

Firms' own codes turn reporting into a duty. JPMorgan's Code of Conduct: "You must do the right thing - not only when it's easy or convenient", and "you can also be held responsible for not reporting actions you knew or should have known were in violation". It contains a worked dilemma about an odd-looking client transaction, with the answer: "You should share the concern with your manager and, if necessary, contact your Compliance Officer for assistance" ([JPMorgan Chase Code of Conduct](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/content/dam/jpmc/jpmorgan-chase-and-co/documents/code-of-conduct.pdf)). Compliance is the department that checks the firm follows the rules. Goldman's Business Principles on confidential client information and on integrity, and UBS's "I step up and act when something isn't right", are in the table of firms' values in the next section.

**Nearest published key.** The one three-level key found for an integrity scenario is OPM's. An investigator is offered a car at well below market value by a suspect. "Unsatisfactory: Accept the offer." "Satisfactory: Say no to the offer and continue the investigation; document the incident in your report." "Superior: Probe the dealership managers to determine how they are able to offer a car at such a reduced price; attempt to get contact information of others involved; say no to the offer; and document the details of the incident" ([OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)). It is from US federal hiring, not finance.

**Top against middle.** Doing the compliant thing is the middle mark. The top mark adds steps that protect the organisation's wider aim. Applied to "you sent confidential information to the wrong client" (inference): a low answer tries to fix it quietly or delays; a middle answer tells the manager and follows instructions; a high answer names the priority (the client whose information is exposed), tries to stop further spread, tells the manager at once, brings in Compliance or the data protection team, lets the firm decide how the recipient and the affected client are contacted, records what happened, and says how the cause would be fixed.

**Neighbouring question.** "Tell me about a time you were honest." Also the heroic version in which you sort it out yourself, which markers read as concealment.

**Cues.** "Confidential", "against firm policy", "a senior colleague tells you to ignore it", "a teammate suggests not mentioning it", "who would you speak to", "how would you first respond".

**Shape.** The priority, then contain, tell, escalate, record and prevent, in that order, with the trade-off named: your embarrassment against the client's interest.

### Commercial awareness

**Assessed.** Whether you follow markets and business and can connect them to the firm. A careers publisher's definition: graduates with it "are conscious of how economic and political trends can impact" the employer, and it is "not so much a skill as a way of thinking" ([TARGETjobs](http://targetjobs.co.uk/careers-advice/skills-for-getting-a-job/what-commercial-awareness)).

**Nearest published key.** None. Every careers-service definition found contains a clause about how events affect the employer.

**Top against middle (inference).** The middle answer is an accurate summary of a news story. The top answer states the effect on this firm, this division or its clients, and gives a view.

**Neighbouring question.** "Summarise something you have read."

**Cues.** The marks sit after the "and": "and why", "and what interested you about it", "that affects the business division you are applying to" (JPMorgan), "how might it impact the markets and the role you've applied for" (Bank of America), "related to BlackRock".

**Shape.** What happened with one number, why it matters, what it means for this firm or its clients, and your view. The first guide covers this shape in more detail.

## How to decode a question: the method you already use on a motion

In British Parliamentary debating, "the debate should commence 15 minutes after the motion is announced" ([British Parliamentary handbook](https://www.uvm.edu/~debate/watch/wudcdvd/text/bphandbook.pdf)). A debating federation's guide to that preparation reads: "Look for important words in the motion", then "Figure out what kind of motion this is" and "plan how you will meet the burdens for your side" ([Saskatchewan Elocution and Debate Association](https://saskdebate.ca/resources/resources-for-students/prepping-for-impromptu-debates)). You do not walk into a round and deliver last week's case because the motion contains a familiar word. You work out what this motion obliges you to prove. An interview question is a motion with a 30-second preparation time.

| In a debate | In a one-way interview |
|---|---|
| Type of motion | Type of question |
| Key words and actors in the motion | The skill word and the twist |
| Your burden: what you must prove | What a complete answer must contain |
| Your team line | Your first sentence |
| A point of information you have accepted | Each part of the question |

On the last row, the handbook's rule is one you know: "Once you have accepted a point of information you can't just ignore it and carry on. You must deal with it or risk the adjudicator's wrath." Every part of an interview question is an accepted point of information.

Decode every question in the same five steps and the same order. **One, type:** read the opening phrase to see whether the question wants a past event, a decision, a method, a reason, an opinion, an explanation, a recommendation or an open introduction. **Two, skill:** find the word that names what is being marked. **Three, parts:** count the separate things you are asked for. **Four, twist:** find any word that limits which answer is allowed. **Five, instructions:** obey any printed line about what your response must include. The output is one silent sentence, your burden: "a complete answer here is a [type] about [skill], in [number] parts, that [twist]".

<!-- figure:decode -->

This method is assembled from four fields that teach the same moves under different names: exam technique, the marking of professional exams, courtroom advocacy and debating. None of it has been tested in job interviews, and the classroom versions have only one teacher's uncontrolled report behind them ([WHP Trust](https://www.whptrust.org/component/k2/item/83-my-pupils-really-started-to-bug-me-action-research-on-a-year-11-exam-technique)).

### Step one: the opening phrase tells you the type

The table is my synthesis from the reported questions, by analogy with the "command words" of school exams, the instruction words such as "explain" and "evaluate" ([AQA](https://www.aqa.org.uk/resources/economics/gcse/economics/teach/command-words)). Learn it before any interview, because there is no time to work it out in the window.

| Opening phrase | Type | What it demands | The wrong form |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Tell me about a time", "Describe a situation", "Give an example", "Share an experience", "When have you" | Past event | One specific, dated event told as a story | A habit or a philosophy |
| "What would you do", "How would you handle", "Imagine", "You are working on" | Decision | A decision and ordered steps in the future tense, including who you tell | A past story as the whole answer |
| "How do you", "What strategies do you use", "What role do you typically take", "What is your approach" | Method | Two or three methods in the present tense, each with a line of evidence | One long anecdote, unless "Give an example" follows |
| "Why X", "What motivates you", "What interests you" | Reason | Reasons specific to X; "why X and why Y" needs both | Facts about X |
| "What... and why", "what interested you about it", "what do you think" | Opinion | The view is the answer; the description is set-up | A summary |
| "Walk me through", "Outline", "Explain", "Define" | Knowledge | An ordered explanation | A story |
| "Pitch" | Recommendation | Recommendation, reason, evidence, risk | A company description |
| "Tell us about yourself", "Introduce yourself" | Open | Selected achievements, plus any second ask | The CV in date order |

The commonest slip is to treat a method question as a story. Of Morgan Stanley's "What strategies do you use to maintain your composure and effectiveness under pressure?", the site that collected it says: "This is not asking for a story... a single STAR anecdote answers a different question" ([FinanceFluency, Morgan Stanley](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/morgan-stanley?p=summer)).

### Step two: the skill word

Look in four places. A value sentence before the question names it outright ("At NatWest we make good decisions and solve problems..."). The headline noun or verb names it ("persuade", "mistake", "feedback"). The listed sub-questions name what will be marked: "how you handled any objections" means influence is being marked, not the outcome. And the tail names a second skill: "what did you learn", "how did you know" and "what would you do differently now" mark self-awareness; "who would you speak to" marks escalation.

### Step three: count the parts, because real questions are longer than the lists you practised on

The question lists that circulate on forums compress each question to its headline. Wording that candidates captured from their screens in the 2027 cycle is twice as long and far more often multi-part.

| Set of reported questions | Number | Median words | Two or more asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aggregated lists, nine banks, September 2026 | 67 | 9 | 16% |
| Aggregated list, five US banks, November 2023 | 50 | 9 | 2% |
| Vendor-collected 2027-cycle wording, 14 firms | 136 | 18 | 40% |

In the vendor-collected set a quarter of questions exceed 28 words and one in ten exceeds 51; the longest, from M&G, is 103 words. By firm, the median and longest are: M&G 74 and 103 words; Bank of America 43 and 53; Morgan Stanley 20 and 39; UBS 17.5 and 44; Goldman Sachs 17 and 58; JPMorgan 17 and 36; Schroders 16 and 25; BlackRock 14 and 25. These figures were computed by the researcher from the three sets, and the share with two or more asks is a keyword count, not a hand count ([eFinancialCareers 2026](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/hirevue-questions-banking)) ([eFinancialCareers 2023](https://www.efinancialcareers.co.uk/news/finance/hirevue-interview-questions-goldman-sachs-jpmorgan)) ([FinanceFluency](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews)). The practical point: if you prepared from short lists, you rehearsed for a shorter, simpler question than the one that appears. The Goldman question a forum would carry as "a time you made a decision without all the information" is, in the reported full wording, 58 words with six asks.

Parts are not decoration. Where a key exists, coverage is written into it. The generic key gives 5 for "All points addressed", 3 for "Some points covered" and 1 for "A few good points but main issues missing" ([Oregon Employment Department](https://www.oregon.gov/employ/agency/documents/interview-scoring-2023-09.pdf)). A published key for a three-part question on stress gives 1 out of 5, labelled "Marginal", to the candidate who answers only the "how would you" part: someone who "has not been exposed to situations involving mental or physical stress but is able to articulate reasonably strategies" ([University of South Carolina HR](https://www.biomedical.sc.edu/about/offices_and_divisions/human_resources/docs/rating_guide.pdf)). Goldman's own recruiters said in 2018: "Be specific and be sure to answer all parts of the question" ([eFinancialCareers](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2018/07/video-interview-goldman-sachs)). No source gives a rule for how many marks one missed part costs.

Six structures cover almost every multi-part question in the reported set: a story plus sub-questions listed after "In your response, please"; a story plus the fixed formula "the situation, your actions, and the outcome"; a story plus a reflective tail; a double motivation ("why X and why Y"); a "what, and why" where the second half carries the marks; and, new at Goldman this cycle, a past event joined to a hypothetical ("...and how AI could have helped you do it"). To count quickly, count the question marks and the "and"s.

### Step four: the twist

A twist is a word or clause that changes which answer qualifies. These all appear in the reported questions: "outside of school or work"; "not on your CV"; "without being the leader"; "without prior experience or guidance"; "even when it was difficult"; "at a significant personal or academic cost"; "while others saw it as the way things are"; "preferred less"; "turned down"; "that you struggled with"; "while collaborating with others"; "that affects the business division you are applying to"; "apart from F1"; "first respond"; "one or two"; "two trends". The exam researchers warn that readers "may overlook negative words (e.g. 'not', 'only', 'a few')" ([Sweiry et al. 2002](https://www.cambridgeassessment.org.uk/Images/109695-tales-of-the-expected-the-influence-of-students-expectations-on-exam-validity.pdf)). The twist usually sits at the end of the sentence, which is the part a triggered script stops you reading.

A question with any other tail (lessons, how you knew, what you would do differently, why it mattered to you) is asking for something STAR does not deliver unless you add it.

### Turning the decode into speech: the first sentence and the signposts

Two devices make the decode audible. Both are unanimous practitioner advice; neither has been tested in interviews.

The first is a first sentence that answers directly and reuses the question's own words: the direct answer, the skill word, the twist, and a count of parts when there are three or more. Advocates are taught the same: "answer directly... and then say, 'Let me explain'" ([Marquette Law faculty blog](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/23563)). Against the reported questions it sounds like this:

> "The clearest time I moved a team's result without being the leader was ..."
>
> "The decision was whether to [X] when I was missing [Y]. I'll cover what was missing, how I weighed what I had, and how I know it was the right call."

The echo forces you to have read the twist, commits you to the right form before a script can take over, and gives the marker an immediate match. Keep it to a clause; do not recite the question back while your answer clock runs.

The second is one spoken signpost per part, in the order asked ("On what was missing...", "How I knew it was the right call..."). ICAEW's lead examiner recommends exactly this in writing: structure answers "around the different parts (or sub-requirements) of each question, using sub-headings", which "should make it easier for them to ensure each of the sub-requirements is covered" and "also aides the marking" ([ICAEW examiners' report](https://icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/learning-and-development/apprenticeships/level-7-examiners-report-august-2022.ashx)). It costs no preparation time because the question supplies the order, and it turns "answer every part" from something to remember into something you can hear: a part with no signpost was not answered. End with a reflection only where the question asks for one; where it does, the reflection is a marked part and needs its own signpost.

## The window: what fits in 30, 60 and 180 seconds

### The arithmetic of reading and writing

A pooled analysis of 190 studies puts the average adult's silent reading speed for non-fiction in English at **238 words a minute**, with most adults between 175 and 300 ([Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)). From that, the time to read a question once and twice:

| Question length | Example | One read | Two reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 words | "Why investment banking and why MS among its competitors?" | 2.5 s | 5 s |
| 20 words | Goldman's scenario questions | 5 s | 10 s |
| 40 words | Goldman's "desire to excel" question | 10 s | 20 s |
| 60 words | Goldman's decision question; NatWest | 15 s | 30 s |
| 75 words | NatWest's longest; M&G's data question | 19 s | 38 s |
| 100 words | M&G's compliance question | 25 s | 50 s |

A slower reader at 175 words a minute needs about 21 seconds for one pass of 60 words. These are speeds for reading at leisure; nobody has measured how long it takes to take in an interview question under pressure.

Handwriting is far slower. University students copying text averaged 22.85 words a minute, and earlier work cited in the same paper gives 26 words a minute writing neatly and 36 writing as fast as possible ([South African Journal of Occupational Therapy](https://journals.assaf.org.za/index.php/sajot/article/download/19695/22614/121466)). Four keywords therefore cost about 7 to 10 seconds, and more when you are composing them, not copying.

**This is the correction to the first guide.** It gave a 30-second routine in which you read the question twice in five seconds and spent fifteen seconds writing four keywords. Reading a 20-word question twice takes ten seconds on its own, and for a 58-word Goldman question two readings take the whole window. Writing four keywords takes a third of the window before any thinking. The routine was built for the short questions on forum lists. Drop the writing at 30 seconds. Use your hands for counting parts.

You said you can barely read the question in 30 seconds. For a 20-word question the reading itself takes five. If the window feels too short to read in, either the question is one of the long ones, which is now common, or part of your attention is on something else: a prepared answer that has already surfaced, or worry. Both have a fix below.

### The 30-second routine

For a question of about 20 words, with no writing: **first read, 5 seconds**; **second read for the opening phrase, the twist and the count of parts, 5 seconds**; **name the type and the parts, 3 seconds**; **choose the story or method, 5 seconds**; **compose the first sentence and say it silently, 8 seconds**; **breathe out and bring your eyes to the lens, 3 seconds**. That totals 29 seconds.

<!-- figure:window -->

For a question of 40 to 60 words a full second read does not fit. Make one deliberate read of 10 to 15 seconds and count the parts on your fingers as they go past. Re-read only the clause that holds the sub-questions, usually the second half, for 4 to 6 seconds. Choose in 4 seconds. Compose the first sentence in 5. The count has to happen during the first read because there is no time for it afterwards.

Why the first sentence gets a quarter of the window: in the dodge experiments, speakers who answered the right question but "fumbled their words" were rated poorly. And advocates warn against "starting to give an answer before thinking about what to say... It's always better to take a beat to be sure you know what you want to say before you start speaking" ([Duke Law](https://law.duke.edu/life/mootcourt/tips)). In a study of language learners, the speakers who planned in less detail were the most accurate, which the authors put down to limited attention ([Foster & Skehan 1996](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0272263100015047)). That supports a skeleton plan (type, parts, material, first sentence) over composing sentences, with the caution that the speakers were not using their first language.

### The 60-second routine

Two full reads fit for anything up to about 60 words. Read once (up to 15 seconds). Read again for the opening phrase, twist and parts (up to 15 seconds). Write the parts as two to four initials, for example "M / E / T / K" for missing, evaluate, thought process, know (5 seconds). Choose the story or method (7 seconds). Compose the first sentence and say it silently (10 seconds). Glance back at the question's last clause to check the twist (5 seconds). Breathe and look at the lens (3 seconds). The seconds are my allocation from the reading and writing speeds above.

### The 180-second routine

Schroders is reported to give two minutes and BlackRock up to three; M&G's simulation questions are long. Read twice (up to 50 seconds for a 100-word question). Write the parts as a vertical list (15 seconds). Put one content keyword beside each part (40 seconds). Draft your first sentence and your last (40 seconds). Then read the question a third time against your list (20 seconds), and use what remains to settle. Spend spare time re-reading the question. Do not spend it adding content.

One special case. Some firms deliver the question as a video of an employee or an avatar speaking it, "which catches people out if they expected to read and re-read the prompt" ([FinanceFluency, WTW](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/wtw?p=summer)). You hear it once at speaking speed. Jot an initial for each part as you listen; this is the one case where writing pays even in a short window.

### What has to be done before the interview

The window can only hold recognition and choice. Everything else moves to preparation:

- The decoder table of opening phrases, known by heart.
- Your stories held as components (covered in the section on re-angling), each action tagged with the skill it shows.
- The firm's published value lines, and the skill each could be marking.
- The firm's reported questions, each already decoded: type, skill, parts, twist.
- Two facts that distinguish the firm, and one sentence on what the role produces.
- The integrity sequence: priority, contain, tell, escalate, record, prevent.
- The format: seconds to prepare, seconds to answer, number of attempts, and whether the question is read or heard.

Thinking through what a question is marking takes time, and the only way to make it nearly free on the day is to have done it for every plausible question beforehand.

### If-then plans: automate the one step that matters

An **if-then plan** (researchers call it an implementation intention) is a rule you set in advance in the form "if this happens, then I will do that". Across 94 studies and more than 8,000 people, adding such a plan to a goal improved goal achievement by a medium-to-large margin, and a 2024 update covering 642 tests found benefits for thinking, feeling and behaviour, larger "when plans had a contingent (if-then) format, participants were highly motivated to pursue the goal, and plans were rehearsed" ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0065-2601%2806%2938002-1)) ([Sheeran et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)). No study has applied them to interview answers.

The wording matters, and one kind backfired. In a study of 51 students doing maths problems with distractions, anxious students who had formed the plan "if I hear a distraction, then I will ignore it" completed far more problems than anxious students whose plan was to concentrate harder (about 78 against 54). The concentrate-harder plan left anxious students doing worse than calm ones ([Parks-Stamm et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2009.09.001)). It is one small study. Its warning fits you closely: a plan such as "then I will focus harder" or "then I will give a perfect answer" is the kind that failed. A concrete procedure or an instruction to ignore something is the kind that worked.

Use two plans at most, because a long checklist recreates goal neglect:

> "When the question appears, then I read to its last word and say in my own words what it asks for."
>
> "If a prepared answer comes to mind while I am reading, then I ignore it and finish reading."

Both are my constructions and untested. Run them on every practice question; rehearsed plans are the ones that work.

### Introductions and motivation

**1. "We'd like to get to know you. Tell us about yourself and why you feel you are a good candidate."** Goldman Sachs; list.

- Type: open introduction, plus a reason.
- Assesses: selected achievements; your own judgement of your fit.
- Parts: (1) who you are; (2) why you are a good candidate.
- Trap: your CV in date order, or your why-Goldman script. Neither answers part 2.
- Could fit: one achievement from the asset manager and one from the software company, chosen for what this role needs.
- Opening: "I'm [who you are and what you are aiming at]. I think I'm a good candidate for [role] for two reasons: [one] and [two]."

**2. "Why Private Wealth Management, and why Goldman Sachs?"** Goldman Sachs private wealth management; list.

- Type: two reasons.
- Assesses: job fit and organisation fit, separately.
- Parts: (1) why private wealth management; (2) why Goldman.
- Trap: spending the answer on Goldman. The division comes first and is the half firm research does not cover.
- Could fit: owning the servicing inbox for US institutional mandates, if what you valued was the client relationship; the spring week, for people you really met.
- Opening: "Private wealth management because [what you want from advising individuals]; and Goldman because [one thing its private wealth business has that a named competitor's does not]."

**3. "Why did you apply to Wealth Management, what business areas are you most interested in, and what skills would help you succeed?"** Goldman Sachs wealth management; list.

- Type: a reason, a preference and a self-assessment.
- Assesses: sector commitment; knowledge of the business; self-awareness.
- Parts: (1) why you applied; (2) which business areas; (3) which skills.
- Trap: ninety seconds on part 1. Part 3 is a strengths question hidden at the end.
- Could fit: for part 3, skills you can evidence, such as [keeping a client inbox accurate and prompt].
- Opening: "Three parts. I applied because [reason]; the areas that interest me most are [areas]; and the skills I'd bring are [two skills]."

**4. "Why [this department], specifically, for the long term?"** Goldman Sachs; list.

- Type: a reason, with two twists: "specifically" and "for the long term".
- Assesses: job fit, and whether the interest will last.
- Parts: one.
- Trap: why Goldman, or why you want an internship now.
- Could fit: for change management, the portal and the 60-day onboarding plan, if they show you keep choosing this kind of work.
- Opening: "[Department] specifically, because the work I keep choosing is [kind of work]; and for the long term because [what you want to be doing in it several years in]."

**5. "What is your understanding of the role, and why are you excited about it?"** Schroders; recalled.

- Type: knowledge, then a reason.
- Assesses: job fit.
- Parts: (1) your understanding; (2) your excitement.
- Trap: "why Schroders". The firm is not in the question.
- Could fit: what you saw the equivalent team do at the asset manager.
- Opening: "My understanding is that the role [who it serves and what it produces]. What excites me is [task], because [when you did something like it]."

**6. "tell me about why you want investment management"** Fidelity International; excerpt, February 2025.

- Type: a reason about the industry.
- Assesses: sector commitment.
- Parts: one.
- Trap: why Fidelity.
- Could fit: the sequence from the student fund to the summer at the asset manager.
- Opening: "I want investment management because [what the work is that you like], and I have tested that in [the experiences, in order]."

**7. "What motivates you, and what drives you?"** BlackRock; recalled.

- Type: strengths.
- Assesses: what energises you.
- Parts: the collecting site reads it as two. One way to split them: what you enjoy day to day, and what you are working towards.
- Trap: why BlackRock, or a list of achievements.
- Could fit: something you built without being asked, such as the client stories hub, if that is how it came about.
- Opening: "Day to day, what motivates me is [activity]; over the longer run, what drives me is [aim]."

- Opening: "My approach has two parts: [method], [method]. For example, [event]."

In your response, please describe the situation, the actions you took to influence your stakeholders, how you handled any objections, and the final outcome."** Bank of America; reported. 43 words.

- Assesses: influence.

**23. "You are working on a task and someone is not contributing. How would you mitigate this?"** Goldman Sachs; reported.

- Assesses: judgement in a team.
- Parts: one.
- Trap: telling the story from question 15. This asks what you would do.

- Opening: "I would start by finding out why, privately, before assuming anything. Then [next step], and if nothing changed I would [who you would tell]."

- Parts: (1) your approach; (2) who you would speak to.
- Trap: the quiet fix (delete it and say nothing), or answering part 1 without naming anyone.
- Could fit: no story needed; one line on a real mistake you owned can follow.

- Assesses: integrity; speaking up.
- Parts: one.
- Trap: obeying, or going over their head at once. "I would tell my manager" on its own is the middle answer.

- Assesses: integrity; honest communication.
- Parts: (1) with your team; (2) with the client.
- Trap: addressing only one audience, or a speech about honesty with no steps.
- Could fit: no story needed.
- Opening: "I would raise it, first with my teammate and then with the client, and here is how."

**28. "Walk us through an achievement you are proud of, academic or personal. If you were starting it again today with full access to AI, what would you do differently?"** Goldman Sachs; list.

## Re-angling one story for different questions, and what to do when none fits

UK careers guidance agrees that one experience can serve several questions if the emphasis moves. Prospects: "Even if the situation is the same, shift your focus. For a leadership question emphasise decision-making and motivating others. While for a problem-solving question highlight analysis and creative solutions. But avoid overusing one example across the whole interview as it can seem like you have limited experience" ([Prospects](https://www.prospects.ac.uk/careers-advice/interview-tips/competency-based-interviews)). Advocates are taught the principle that makes this possible under time pressure: "Internalize the substance of those responses, rather than memorizing the words, so that you can shape your arguments to fit the actual questions the judges ask" ([Duke Law](https://law.duke.edu/life/mootcourt/tips)).

There is a reason a scripted story does not re-angle itself. In a classic experiment, people read a story whose solution could be reused on a new problem. Without a hint only about 30% applied it; with a hint about 75% did ([Gick & Holyoak 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285%2883%2990002-6)). A story stored under one label ("my process-improvement answer") is found only by the question that carries that label. That experiment used puzzles, not interviews.

### Hold each story as components, not as a script

Write one card per experience with these lines, and nothing in full sentences:

- the situation, in one line;
- three or four separate actions, each tagged with the skill it shows (spotted, analysed, persuaded, escalated, organised, learned);
- the result;
- one lesson;
- one thing you would do differently now;
- one cost or risk you accepted.

The last three lines exist because the reported questions ask for them in so many words ("what did you learn", "what would you do differently now", "at a significant personal or academic cost"). On the day, the question chooses which components you speak and in what order. This procedure is my synthesis of the guidance above; it has not been tested.

### One experience, three questions

Take the portal you built at the asset manager to route due diligence questionnaires to the owning team, replacing a 400-line Excel tracker. Its card might hold: action A, [how you noticed the problem and proposed a fix]; action B, [how you worked out which team owned which kind of question, and built the portal]; action C, [how you got the teams to use it, and what they objected to]; action D, [what you had to learn to build it]; result, [what replaced what, and any measure]; lesson, [one]; differently, [one]; cost, [what you gave up to do it]. I do not know which of these happened. Each angle below works only if the facts support it.

| Question | First sentence | Gets most of the time | Cut to a clause |
|---|---|---|---|
| BlackRock: "something you saw as an opportunity to improve while others saw it as the way things are. What effect did it have?" | "Everyone treated the 400-line tracker as simply how it was done; I saw [opportunity]." | Action A, then the result as "the effect" | How you built it |
| Bank of America: "a time that you had to persuade people to accept your point of view or recommendation... how you handled any objections" | "I had to persuade [the owning teams] to answer questionnaires through a portal in place of the tracker they knew." | Action C: what you did to influence, each objection and your reply | Actions A and B |
| Morgan Stanley: "a complex problem you solved without prior experience or guidance. What was your approach, and what would you do differently now?" | "I had never built [a tool like this] and nobody showed me how; the problem was [routing each question to its owner]." | Actions B and D, then the "differently" line under its own signpost | Action C |

The situation line is the same in all three. The first sentence, the action that gets the time and the close all change. That is what "tailoring" means in practice: selecting and ordering components to match the parts of this question.

### The fit test

Before reusing a story for a question with a twist, say the twist and the story's headline as one sentence: "...without being the leader, when I built the portal". If that sentence is false or strained, the story does not fit, however good it is. If you led the portal work, it cannot answer "without being the leader".

### When no prepared story fits

Guidance on this is almost absent; no careers service or study found says what to do. The order below is my inference from the evidence in this guide.

1. **A smaller true event that meets the twist beats a larger one that does not.** The twist is what the question is testing. Scale is not.
2. **Check the type again.** "How do you" and "what would you do" questions need no story. Give the method or the decision and add one line of evidence.
3. **If a past-event question truly has no match, give the nearest specific instance as a story, say plainly where it falls short of the question, and say what you would do in the full case.** A specific partial instance is closer to what was asked than a general self-description, which is the response type that lowered hiring recommendations in the Swiss study.

If your mind goes blank, one piece of commercial advice is sensible: "say what you are thinking in one calm sentence rather than filling the silence with filler. Reviewers would rather hear a short, complete answer than a long, uncertain one" ([Finbound](https://www.finbound.org/blog/kpmg-video-interview-guide)). Two rules of my own: never invent an event, and when two stories fit, take the one with the harder situation, because the published keys reward difficulty.

## The headspace: aim for the right answer, not the perfect one

### Why aiming for the perfect answer hurts

Psychologists split perfectionism in two. **Perfectionistic strivings** are high personal standards. **Perfectionistic concerns** are worry about mistakes and about the gap between your standard and what you produced. A **correlation**, written *r*, shows how closely two things move together: 0 is no link, 0.2 a weak one, 0.5 a substantial one, and a minus sign means one falls as the other rises. Across 37 studies and 8,901 students, strivings went with better academic results (*r* = .24) and concerns with slightly worse ones (*r* = -.08, or -.15 once strivings are taken out), and the measure that captures the gap between standard and performance had the largest negative link, *r* = -.16 ([Madigan 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09484-2)). Over time, concern about mistakes predicts rising anxiety ([Smith et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2017.1384466)). No study has looked at perfectionism and interview scores.

The evidence does not say lower your standards. It says the damage comes from fearing the shortfall. A scripted "perfect" answer manufactures a shortfall on every question, because every sentence you say that is not in the script registers as a miss, and every question that does not match a script registers as a threat.

Two more findings point the same way. When people in a conversation were told to focus on themselves and on managing how they came across, this "increased feelings and appearance of anxiety and undermined performance for all participants", as judged by themselves, their partner and independent raters ([Leigh et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0247703)). Those were adolescents in conversation, not interviews. And among real job candidates whom the employer rated in its top 10%, those with a stronger drive to present themselves accurately were more likely to receive an offer ([Moore et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000223)). That benefit applied to strong candidates, which on paper you are.

One more fact to hold on to. Among real applicants, interview anxiety had "near-zero correlations with job performance" ([Schneider et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12263)). Anxiety lowers interview marks without making anyone a worse employee. It is a tax on the measurement and says nothing about you.

### What to aim for instead: the first workable answer that comes from the question

Thirty seconds is not long enough to find the best answer. It is long enough to find a good one, if you stop comparing. Researchers who interviewed 26 experienced fire commanders about 156 decisions found that in fewer than 12% was there any sign of comparing two or more options; in over 80% the commander recognised the situation as typical and took the typical action "without any need to consider more than one option" ([Klein et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1518/155534310x12844000801203)). Handball experiments are reported to have found that players' first idea was generally better than their later ones ([Johnson & Raab 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0749-5978%2803%2900027-x)) ([Raab & Johnson 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898x.13.3.158)). These are firefighters and athletes. Nobody has tested the choice of an interview answer.

There is one condition, and it is the hinge of this guide. The first option is good when it is generated **from the situation**. A first option generated from your script is the mental-set failure described at the start. So the order is fixed: decode the question, then take the first story or method that the decoded question brings to mind, then check it once against the twist. Do not scan your whole story list for the best one.

Choosing carefully still has its place, which is in preparation. Final-year students who searched exhaustively for the best job got starting salaries 20% higher than those who settled for good enough, though they felt worse about it ([Iyengar et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01677.x)). Be exacting when you build your story cards, when you have days. Take the first workable option in the window, when you have seconds.

A sentence to carry in: **"My job is to give this marker what this question asks for."** It is a concrete procedure, which is the kind of self-instruction that helped anxious students in the if-then study. "Give a perfect answer" is the kind that hurt.

### Two kinds of pressure, in sequence

Pressure harms performance by two routes. Worry about the outcome uses up working memory and hurts tasks that need attention; being watched makes you monitor yourself and hurts skills that normally run on their own ([DeCaro et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023466)). In maths experiments, pressure harmed only people with high working memory, and only on the hardest problems: it "harms individuals most qualified to succeed by consuming the working memory capacity that they rely on" ([Beilock & Carr 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2005.00789.x)).

A one-way interview applies both in turn; that mapping is my inference from experiments on category learning, maths and golf putting. The window is an attention task under outcome pressure, so protect it with a procedure that lives outside your head: the decoder, the two if-then plans, parts counted on fingers. The recording is a fluent skill performed while watched, so point your attention away from your own delivery: self-view hidden, eyes on the lens, your mind on the marker who needs the evidence.

### Remedies, ranked by evidence

An **effect size**, written *d* or *g*, says how big a difference is in standard units: about 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. No randomised trial has tested any of these remedies with interview performance as the outcome.

| Remedy | Evidence | Where from | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Practising under mild pressure | *g* = 0.67 across 14 studies, 394 people ([Low et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/spy0000202)) | Sport and policing | Use. Best supported; still an analogy |
| A fixed routine before performing | *g* = 0.64 to 0.70 ([Rupprecht et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984x.2021.1944271)) | Sport | Use. Your decode routine is the routine |
| If-then plans | *d* = 0.65 in 94 studies ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0065-2601%2806%2938002-1)) | Many fields | Use two, worded as procedures |
| Reading arousal as readiness | *d* = 0.23 overall; 0.34 for public performance ([Bosshard & Gomez 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-58408-w)) | Laboratory trials | Use. Free, small effect |
| Talking to yourself by name or as "you" | Rated better in a first-impression task and a speech; seven studies, 585 people in all ([Kross et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035173)) | Laboratory speaking tasks | Use. Free |
| Slow breathing | *g* = -0.35 on felt stress; the performance study used 15 minutes ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)) | Clinical trials | Before the session, not in the window |
| Improvisation classes | Small studies: more comfort with uncertainty; nothing on answer quality ([Felsman et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tsc.2020.100632)) | Laboratory | Optional |
| Writing out your worries beforehand | Failed replication; a 2025 pooled analysis "fails to support" it ([O'Meara & Lovett 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2552857)) | Exams | **Drop** |
| A lucky ritual before starting | The main paper was retracted in 2024 ([retraction notice](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2024.104377)) | Laboratory | **Drop** |
| Power posing | Evidence indistinguishable from no effect ([Simmons & Simonsohn 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616658563)) | Laboratory | **Drop** |

On the first row, the point to remember is that the pressure cue has to be present in practice and that mild pressure is enough; the authors advise creating "pressurized training environments rather than relying on greater amounts of training". On the fourth row, the technique is to tell yourself that a fast heart and quick breathing are your body getting ready to perform.

## A two-week practice programme built for unseen questions

### Why rehearsing fixed answers to fixed questions does not transfer

Rehearsing the same answer to the same question is what learning researchers call blocked practice. It builds fluency on those items and feels effective: in one experiment people rated it as more effective "even after their own test performance had demonstrated the opposite" ([Kornell & Bjork 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x)). The alternative is **interleaving**: mixing different kinds of item so that each one makes you decide what kind it is. A pooled analysis of 59 studies found interleaving better overall (*g* = 0.42), strongest when the categories resemble each other, and worse for memorising word lists, which is a fair description of a script ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)). In 54 school maths classes, pupils whose homework was mixed so that "no 2 consecutive problems require the same strategy" scored 61% on a surprise test a month later, against 38% for blocked practice ([Rohrer et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)).

Three further results shape the drills. People who answered three different questions about a concept transferred it to new questions better than people who answered one question three times ([Butler et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000142)). Business students who compared two negotiation cases side by side "were nearly three times more likely" to use the shared principle in a live negotiation than students who studied the cases separately ([Loewenstein et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03212967)). And in a study of student speeches, the "number of rehearsals for an audience" predicted quality ([Menzel & Carrell 1994](https://doi.org/10.1080/03634529409378958)). No experiment compares scripted with interleaved interview preparation, so this programme is a translation from classrooms and laboratories, and each drill below is marked as derived from a research procedure or as my own.

### The drills

**Decoding drill** (derived from the interleaving studies; untested). Put 20 questions from the bank on cards and shuffle them. For each, start a 20-second timer and say aloud the type, the skill, the number of parts and the twist. Do not answer. A debating guide gives the same advice for motions: "Practice prepping motions on your own or with a partner with reduced time... The more you practice prepping for motions, the more patterns you'll notice" ([Saskatchewan Elocution and Debate Association](https://saskdebate.ca/resources/resources-for-students/prepping-for-impromptu-debates)). Twenty seconds in practice makes thirty feel long.

**Criteria-identification drill** (derived from a measurement procedure; untested as training). Researchers measure a candidate's **ability to identify criteria**, defined as "a person's ability to correctly perceive performance criteria when participating in an evaluative situation", by listing each interview question and asking candidates "to write the dimension that they believed was assessed with each interview question, and to provide behavioral examples for this dimension"; raters then score the fit from 0 to 3 ([Ingold et al. 2015](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/IngoldKleinmannKoenigMelchersvanIddekinge_inpress.pdf)). People with more of this ability score higher in structured interviews (*r* = .27 and .35 in two studies), and each person scores higher on the questions whose target they spotted ([Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)). The same authors call whether it can be trained an open question and suggest that training "could include such components as identifying cues in job advertisements, and studying instructions", followed by "feedback concerning the targeted dimensions". Nobody has published a test of that. The first guide cited one study in which this ability did not predict who improved. With that warning, the drill: take 20 questions; give yourself 10 seconds each to write one skill word and two behaviours that would show it ("integrity: report at once, escalate to Compliance"); check against a key made from the value sentence before the question, the listed sub-questions and the firm's published values; score yourself 0 to 3; and log each miss by the cue you overlooked.

**Compare-two-cases drill** (derived from the comparison studies; untested for interviews). Take two of your experiences and write what they have in common and which skill each action shows. This produces the tags on your story cards.

**Re-angling drill** (my construction). Take one story and four different questions in a row, for example bank questions 12, 14, 17 and 19. With 30 seconds for each, change only the first sentence, the action that gets the time and the close. If two of the four first sentences are strained, your stories have a hole in that area, which is better learned now than in a recording.

**First-sentence drill** (my construction). Fifteen shuffled questions, 30 seconds each, and speak only the first sentence. Check that each contains the skill word and the twist.

**Mild-pressure set** (derived from the pressure-training studies; untested for interviews). Five mixed questions with the firm's real timings, camera recording, a visible countdown, one take, and an arrangement made in advance that someone will watch the result.

**Guess-the-question check** (my construction, from the dodge-detection experiments). The listener from the self-test reconstructs each question from your answer alone.

Stop doing two things: recording the same answer again and again, and writing new word-for-word scripts.

## Smiling, charisma, humour, warmth and sounding natural

These matter less than any earlier section of this guide. A charming answer to the neighbouring question is still an answer to the neighbouring question. The evidence is thin, comes mostly from laboratory studies and written scenarios, and does not support "be more charming" as a way to pass.

| Behaviour | What the evidence says | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Smiling | In the one experiment, for a job expected to be serious, "Smiling was negatively related to hiring", and hiring was highest "when applicants smiled less in the middle of the interview relative to the start and end" ([Ruben et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224545.2014.972312)). Genuine smiles beat fake ones ([Krumhuber et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10919-008-0056-8)) | A real smile as you start and as you finish. Stop holding a smile through the substance |
| Jokes | In a written interview scenario a joke raised status from 4.15 to 4.95 out of 7 only when the manager laughed; with no laugh it was 4.22. An inappropriate joke cut rated competence from 5.08 to 3.27 ([Bitterly et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000079)) | Do not plan jokes. A recording has nobody to laugh |
| Warmth and likeability | In real interviews, perceived competence carried the effect of self-presentation on ratings and warmth did not ([Amaral et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12260)) | Let warmth be a by-product. Put the effort into evidence |
| Charisma | Training raised rated charisma by a moderate amount in managers and MBA students; nine of its twelve techniques are ways of organising content ([Antonakis et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.5465/amle.2010.0012)) | Use the content devices you know from debating: a contrast, a list of three, a stated conviction |
| Voice | Speakers who talk somewhat louder and vary their volume seem more confident "without undermining their perceived sincerity" ([Van Zant & Berger 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000193)) | Speak a little louder than feels natural and vary it |
| Thanking the viewer | No study of any kind | Skip it. Start with the answer |

Four details behind the table. **Smiling:** the experiment found a chain. The more applicants wanted to make a good impression, the more they smiled, and the smiling lowered hiring. It was one study, with students, for a newspaper reporter post, and I could not retrieve its sample size. **Humour:** every benefit needed the joke to be appropriate and to land visibly. You cannot see whether it landed, so your best case is the unlaughed-at joke, which added nothing, and your downside is more than a point on a seven-point scale. In experiments with hiring managers, an applicant's pun helped women and hurt men when gender was prominent ([Gloor et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000937)). The one exception is narrow: in written scenarios, admitting a weakness with a light joke at your own expense was rated more hireable than admitting it plainly, 5.06 against 4.16 out of 7, but readers of those scenarios were told it was a joke ([Bitterly & Schweitzer 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2019.01.005)). **Warmth:** in real internship interviews, good first impressions during the opening chat went with higher ratings and more offers, but only impressions of competence carried over to other interviewers; "assessments of candidate liking and similarity were not significantly related to other judgments when ratings were provided by different interviewers" ([Barrick et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019918)). In a one-way interview every marker is a different interviewer and there is no opening chat. **Sounding natural:** the support is indirect. Accurate self-presentation helped strong candidates get offers, and what interviewers believe a candidate is doing affects their marks even though they are poor at spotting it ([Roulin et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12079)). No study compares the same content delivered from memory and delivered freshly. The practical route to sounding natural is the one this guide already requires: speak from components to the question in front of you.

### Why a strong live debater can underperform to a lens

Three documented mechanisms apply; no study compares the same speakers live and recorded.

First, speakers rely on listeners. When listeners in an experiment were distracted, narrators "told their stories significantly less well, particularly at what should have been the dramatic ending" ([Bavelas et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.941)), and speakers given more listener feedback produced clearer accounts ([Kraut et al. 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.43.4.718)). A debate room tells you when a point has landed and when to stop. A lens tells you nothing, and the loss shows most at the end of a story, which is where the result and the reflection sit.

Second, the camera turns attention on yourself. In the one interview experiment, a self-view window "did not directly affect interview performance or applicant reactions, but did increase cognitive load" ([Horn & Behrend 2017](https://doi.org/10.25035/pad.2017.005)). That was a live video interview.

Third, being recorded for later judgement is the watched kind of pressure, the kind that disrupts fluent skills.

What to do follows directly. Decide your last sentence before you start, because nobody will signal the ending. Hide the self-view once your framing is checked. Practise to a recording camera so that the silence is itself rehearsed. And run one diagnostic: record the same answer once to an empty lens and once with a person sitting behind the camera. If the second is clearly better, the missing listener is your issue and camera practice is the fix.

## Cards to print

### Card 1: the decoder

1. **Type.** Opening phrase: past event, decision, method, reason, opinion, explanation, recommendation or open.
2. **Skill.** The word that names what is marked. Check any value sentence first.
3. **Parts.** Count question marks and "and"s. Use your fingers.
4. **Twist.** The limiting word, usually at the end.
5. **Instructions.** "In your response, please...", "answer both questions".

Burden, said silently: "A complete answer is a [type] about [skill], in [number] parts, that [twist]."

First sentence: direct answer + skill word + twist + the count if three or more parts.

One spoken signpost per part, in the order asked.

Two plans: "When the question appears, I read to its last word and say what it asks." "If a prepared answer comes to mind while reading, I ignore it and finish reading."

### Card 2: the 30-second window

About 20 words:

1. First read: 5 seconds
2. Second read for opening phrase, twist and parts: 5 seconds
3. Name the type and the parts: 3 seconds
4. Choose the story or method: 5 seconds
5. Compose the first sentence and say it silently: 8 seconds
6. Breathe out, eyes to the lens: 3 seconds

Forty to sixty words: one slow read, counting parts on fingers (10 to 15 seconds); re-read the sub-question clause (4 to 6); choose (4); first sentence (5). No writing in either case.

### Card 3: the 60-second window

1. First read: up to 15 seconds
2. Second read for opening phrase, twist and parts: up to 15 seconds
3. Write the parts as initials: 5 seconds
4. Choose the story or method: 7 seconds
5. Compose the first sentence and say it silently: 10 seconds
6. Glance back at the last clause for the twist: 5 seconds
7. Breathe out, eyes to the lens: 3 seconds

### Card 4: the 180-second window

1. Read twice: up to 50 seconds
2. Write the parts as a vertical list: 15 seconds
3. One content keyword beside each part: 40 seconds
4. Draft the first sentence and the last: 40 seconds
5. Third read of the question against the list: 20 seconds
6. Settle; eyes to the lens

Spare time goes on re-reading the question, not on adding content.

## What this guide corrects, and where the evidence stops

### Corrections to the first guide and to earlier advice

| Earlier advice | Correction | Why |
|---|---|---|

### Where the evidence stops

- **Question wording rests on one vendor.** Nearly every full-length question comes from one commercial site's account of what candidates told it, plus aggregated lists. No firm has confirmed any wording. For asset and wealth managers the record is thinner and less exact: recollections for BlackRock and Schroders, search excerpts for Fidelity International and St. James's Place, and nothing for Legal & General.
- **Recruiter quotes are old.** The named bank recruiters spoke in 2012 and 2013, some from US or Asian offices. No on-the-record statement from 2023 to 2026 on what these questions are marked on was found.
- **No firm publishes a scoring key or a marked model answer.** Every key quoted is from a US public body or the UK Civil Service. Every statement about what a top answer looks like at a bank or asset manager is inference, and so is every link from a question to a firm value.
- **Some firm texts were not verified at source.** Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Deutsche Bank and Legal & General text came from search excerpts or third-party copies.
- **Most of the psychology has not been tested in interviews.** Mental set comes from chess, partial-match reading from trivia questions, expectation effects from school exams, goal neglect and the pressure findings from laboratories, taking the first option from firefighters and handball. The only interview data are the Swiss count of stories and the finding on response delays.
- **Several figures are second-hand.** The 39% and 88% detection rates come from a press release; the 52% and 26.5% rates, the 41% to 48% figure and the 50% follow-up come from summaries.
- **The method is untested.** The five-step decode, the window routines, the if-then wordings, the story cards and every drill are constructions from adjacent evidence. The reading speeds behind the window arithmetic are for unhurried reading.
- **No remedy for pressure has been trialled on interview performance**, and the studies on smiling and humour are small and mostly not about recorded interviews.
- **Nobody has measured the problem in this setting.** There is no figure for how often candidates in one-way finance interviews miss the question, and no rule for how many marks a missed part costs.

What this adds up to is a change in what preparation is for. You have been preparing answers. The evidence suggests preparing to read: knowing the question types cold, holding your experience in parts that can be reassembled, and practising on questions you have not seen until a listener can reconstruct each question from your answer alone. That is a measurable target, and it is the one you already hit every time you stand up on an unseen motion. As the first guide says, nothing here can promise a pass, because most strong candidates at these firms are still cut. It can make sure that when a marker scores your answer, they are scoring an answer to their question.
