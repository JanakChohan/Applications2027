## Part 1: What is really happening when you fail

### You already found the main problem

You wrote: "I answer with the prepared answer I memorised, not the question on the screen."

That is a known reason for rejection. A Goldman Sachs graduate recruiter said it in 2013. Candidates "answer the question they hope to be asked, rather than the one that is posed to them."

So you are not imagining it. It is common. It can be fixed.

### How it happens, second by second

1. The question appears.
2. The first few words look familiar. "Why do you want to..."
3. Your brain says: I know this one.
4. You stop reading carefully. You skim the rest.
5. The memorised answer starts to play in your head.
6. You miss the last part of the question, or one small word that changes it.
7. You deliver a smooth answer to the question next door.

A "question next door" is one that shares a topic or opening words with the real one. "Why this firm?" and "Why this role?" are next door to each other. So are "a time you led a team" and "a time you helped a team without leading it".

This is not carelessness. It happens most to people who prepare hard. Researchers who study exams found the same thing in school pupils. The more familiar a question looks, the stronger the pull to answer the expected one.

### The examples in this guide: meet Sam

Every full example answer in this guide is spoken by Sam. **Sam is made up.** Sam's life is ordinary on purpose, so you can see the method and not the CV.

- Sam is a final-year Business student.
- Sam has worked weekends at a supermarket for two years.
- Sam is treasurer of the university hiking society.
- Sam spent last summer in the office of a small lettings agency.
- Sam fixes up second-hand bikes for fun.
- Sam is applying for the TP ICAP broking internship.

After many examples you will see a line that starts "Your version". It points to something in your own life that could do the same job. These are possibilities only. You know what really happened. I do not.

### What the problem looks like

The question on the screen is: "Why broking, and what makes you think you would be good at it?"

Sam prepared an answer to "Why TP ICAP?". The question looks close enough. Out it comes.

> **Weak answer.** "TP ICAP is the world's largest broker of its kind, with offices in 28 countries. I've researched the firm in depth. I admire its values of Accountability, Adaptability and Authenticity, and its strong results this year. I would love to learn from such talented people."

It is smooth, and the facts come from the firm's own pages. But it answers "Why TP ICAP?". Nobody asked that.

> **Strong answer.** "Broking, because I like being the person in the middle who gets two sides to agree. I found that out last summer at a lettings agency. I spent my days on the phone between landlords and tenants, and I loved it. Why would I be good at it? Two things. I'm accurate under pressure. My till balances to the penny after a Saturday rush. And I'm comfortable on the phone all day with people who want different things."

What changed:

- The first word is the question's own word: "Broking".
- Both things asked for are answered, in the order asked.
- The proof is from Sam's life, not from the firm's website.

### Why hours of research make it worse, not better

This is hard to hear after two years of effort. Here is the reasoning.

**Research gives you facts about the firm. Questions ask about you.** "Tell me about a time" needs an event from your life. "What would you do" needs your decision. "Why this role" needs your match to the work. Only "Why this firm?" needs firm facts, and it needs one or two.

**A polished script is the loudest thing in your memory.** You have said it thirty times. So it arrives first, before you finish reading. A rough plan would not push in like that.

**A long list of things to remember crowds out the one that matters.** Smile. Mention the strategy. Use the keywords. Watch the clock. With all that in your head, "answer this question" gets dropped.

So more hours made the script stronger. A stronger script made the mismatch more likely. You were working hard on the wrong thing. That is fixable, and it means less work, not more.

> **Remember.** Research is still useful. Cap it at one hour per firm. Part 7 lists the few things to collect.

### The habit of saying what you will cover

You told me you open by saying what you are going to cover. An earlier prep guide taught you that.

Here is the straight answer. For a story about one real event, the evidence does not support it. It uses ten to fifteen seconds. If the answer time is one minute, that is a quarter of your answer gone before you say anything that can earn a mark.

What the evidence does support is one short first sentence that answers the question.

> **Weak answer.** "Thank you for the question. I'd like to cover three things today. First my background, then the situation I faced, and finally what I learned from it."

> **Say it like this.** "The clearest time I improved something was at the supermarket, where I cut our Sunday bread waste by about three quarters."

What changed:

- The second version answers in the first breath.
- It uses the question's own word, "improved".
- The marker already knows what the proof will be.

There is one place a count helps. When a question asks for reasons, you can say "Two reasons." That is two words, not fifteen seconds.

### Putting your strongest material first

Keep doing this. It is a good habit.

But "strongest" has to mean strongest for this question. Your best story aimed at the wrong question is still a miss. So the order is: read the question first, then choose the material.

## Part 3: What the buzzwords mean as things to do

People keep telling you to "be yourself" and "be genuine". Those phrases are useless until someone says what to do with your mouth and your hands. Here is each one as actions.

"Genuine" and "authentic" just mean: the listener believes you did the thing you describe. One former Goldman Sachs interviewer said he often rejected people because he "did not believe their answers". So the real question is: what makes an answer believable?

A government recruitment manager, whose team marks several thousand interviews a month, gave the clearest answer I found. The best candidates explain three things:

- what they personally did,
- what they were thinking,
- why they decided to do it.

He added that people often try to make their examples sound bigger than they were. That lowers the mark. Ordinary and true beats grand and vague.

### "Be yourself" means: use your own words and your own events

1. **Use words you would say to a tutor you get on with.** If you would never say it out loud in a seminar, do not say it on camera.
2. **Use events that really happened, at their real size.** A small true event beats a big stretched one.
3. **Say "I'm", "don't", "it's".** That is how people talk. "I am" and "do not" sound like reading.

> **Weak answer.** "I am a highly motivated individual who is passionate about driving value in fast-paced environments."

> **Say it like this.** "I like busy days. Saturday is our worst shift at the supermarket and it's the one I ask for."

What changed:

- No sentence a thousand other people could say.
- One real fact that only Sam could give.

### "Be genuine" means: say what you thought and why you chose

1. **After each thing you did, add why you chose it.** "I rang her, not emailed, because I needed an answer that day."
2. **Say the hard bit out loud.** What went wrong, or what you were unsure about. Markers say you "do not have to make everything sound perfect".
3. **Picture the room while you speak.** See the person you were dealing with. Your voice follows your attention. Radio presenters are trained to do this.

> **Weak answer.** "I made a small error with a payment but I quickly resolved it and learned to be more careful."

> **Strong answer.** "As treasurer I paid the same £120 minibus invoice twice. I found it when I checked the bank statement at the end of the month. My stomach dropped, because it was the society's money, not mine. I told our president that day, before I'd fixed anything, because I didn't want her to hear it from someone else. Then I emailed the hire company and the refund came within a week. Now every payment goes in a shared log with its invoice number before I pay it."

What changed:

- Real amount, real moment, real feeling.
- Each action has its reason.
- The mistake is stated at its true size, not shrunk.

### "Sound natural" means: do not read, and do not recite

Listeners can usually tell when someone is reading aloud. In one small study they spotted it about four times in five, from the tune of the voice alone. Reading has one regular tune. Real talk goes up and down and changes speed.

1. **Never have full sentences in front of you.** If the words are there, you will read them.
2. **Change the wording every time you practise.** Keep the points. Let the sentences come out differently. Then there is no script to recite.
3. **Keep sentences short.** One idea each. A listener gets one chance to follow you.

Do not add "um" on purpose to sound relaxed. In one experiment, speech with more stumbles was rated as less sincere, not more.

> **Try it now.** Say out loud what you had for dinner last night and why you chose it. Twenty seconds. Notice how your voice moved. That is your natural voice. That is the voice to use.

### "Be specific" means: add how and why to every action

A university careers guide shows the move clearly. Take each thing you did. Add how you did it. Add why.

> **Weak answer.** "I told my manager we should reduce the bread earlier and she agreed."

> **Strong answer.** "I'd counted the waste for three Sundays, so I took her the numbers. About forty loaves a week were going in the bin. I suggested one o'clock, not half past three, because by half past three most shoppers have gone. She worried people would wait for the cheap bread. So I offered a four-week trial and said I'd count the full-price sales myself."

What changed:

- "Told" became how Sam told her: with three weeks of numbers.
- Each choice has a "because".
- The manager's worry is in the answer. That makes it believable.

For a "why this firm" answer, "specific" has a simple test. Swap in another firm's name. If the sentence is still true, it is too vague.

### "Answer the question" means: let the question choose your words

1. **Read to the last word before you think of any answer.**
2. **Start with the question's own key words.**
3. **Give one chunk of answer for each thing asked, in the order asked.**

Here is the test. Someone hears your answer but not the question. Can they guess the question? If they guess a different one, you answered a different one. The portal runs this test for you.

> **Remember.** Believable means: what I did, what I thought, why I chose it. In my own words. At its real size.

## Part 5: The method, eight small steps for every question

There are three moves: **read, choose, say**. Inside them are eight small steps. You repeat the same eight for every question. After a week of practice they take seconds.

1. Read to the last word.
2. Work out what kind of answer it wants.
3. Count the things it asks for.
4. Spot any limiting word.
5. Choose real material that fits.
6. Say a first sentence that uses the question's own words.
7. Give one chunk for each thing asked, in the order asked.
8. Stop.

Steps 1 to 4 are reading. Step 5 is choosing. Steps 6 to 8 are saying.

### Step 1: Read to the last word

Do nothing else until you reach the full stop or question mark at the very end.

Reason: the part that changes the question is usually at the end. A script that has started playing stops you getting there.

Carry two rules into every question:

- When the question appears, I read to its last word.
- If a prepared answer pops into my head while I read, I ignore it and keep reading.

> **Try it now.** Answer this fast: "How many animals of each kind did Moses take on the Ark?" Most people say two. Read it again slowly. It was Noah, not Moses. That is how the brain reads a familiar-looking question.

### Step 2: Work out what kind of answer it wants

The opening words tell you. There are five main kinds.

- **One real event.** "Tell me about a time...", "Describe a situation...", "Give an example...". They want one thing that happened, on one occasion.
- **What you would do.** "What would you do if...", "How would you handle...", "Imagine...". They want your decision and your steps, in order.
- **How you usually do something.** "How do you...", "What is your approach to...". They want two or three habits, each with a quick proof.
- **A reason.** "Why...", "What attracts you to...", "What motivates you...". They want your reasons, with proof from your life.
- **An opinion.** "What do you think about...", "...and why did it interest you?". They want your view. The description is only the set-up.

Two more turn up often:

- **An explanation.** "What is a broker?", "What do you know about...". They want it explained simply, in order.
- **An introduction.** "Tell me about yourself." They want two or three chosen things, not your life story.

The most common slip is giving the wrong kind. They ask "How do you stay calm under pressure?" and you tell one long story. They asked for your habits.

> **Try it now.** Name the kind for each. (a) "Describe a situation where you disagreed with a colleague." (b) "How do you keep up with markets?" (c) "What would you do if a client was rude to you?" (d) "What attracts you to broking?" Answers: one real event, how you usually do it, what you would do, a reason.

### Step 3: Count the things it asks for

Count the question marks and the word "and". Put up one finger for each thing asked.

Reason: online lists shorten every question. Real ones are about twice as long. About four in ten ask for more than one thing. Published marking forms give the top mark only when every part is answered.

Examples:

- "Why TP ICAP and why broking?" Two things.
- "Tell us about yourself and why you applied." Two things.
- "Tell me about a time you made a mistake. What did you do to fix it, and what did you learn?" Three things: the mistake, the fix, the lesson.

Some firms print an instruction. One is reported as "Please remember to answer both questions in your response." Treat a line like that as the most important one on the screen.

> **Try it now.** Count the things asked: "What do you know about what a broker does, and why would you be good at it?" Hold up your fingers. The answer is two: what a broker does, and why you would be good.

### Step 4: Spot any limiting word

A limiting word narrows which answer is allowed. Miss it and your whole answer can be ruled out.

Real examples from reported questions:

- "...**without** being the leader"
- "...**not** on your CV"
- "...**outside** of work or study"
- "...even when it was **difficult**"
- "Why broking and **not** trading?"
- "Name **one**..."
- "How would you **first** respond?"

Reason: readers skip small words like "not", "only" and "without". Those are the words the marker checks.

> **Try it now.** Find the limiting words: "Tell me about a time you helped a team succeed without being the leader." Then say which of your stories it rules out. Any story where you were in charge is out.

### Step 5: Choose real material that fits

Take the first true thing the question brings to mind. Check it once against the limiting word. Go.

Do not search for your best ever story. There is no time, and the first fitting one is good enough.

**The fit test.** Say the limiting word and your story as one sentence. "A time I helped without being the leader was when I ran the society's budget." If that sentence is false or strained, pick another.

**If nothing fits:**

1. Use a smaller true event that does fit. A small story that fits beats a big one that does not.
2. Check the kind again. "How do you" and "What would you do" need no story at all.
3. If you are still stuck, give the nearest real event and say plainly where it differs. Then say what you would do in the exact case.

Never invent an event.

> **Try it now.** Think of one real time something went wrong for you. Say the fit test out loud: "A time I made a mistake was when I..." If the sentence is true and easy to say, the event fits.

### Step 6: Say a first sentence that uses the question's own words

Take the main words from the question. Put your answer next to them. That is your first sentence.

- Question: "Tell me about a time you contributed without being the leader."
- First sentence: "The clearest time I contributed without being the leader was on a group project last spring."

Reason: it proves you read the limit. It locks you onto the right kind of answer before a script can start. And the marker hears a match straight away.

Keep it to one sentence. Do not read the whole question back.

> **Try it now.** Say only the first sentence for each. Do not go on. (a) "Why this role?" (b) "What would you do if you made a mistake nobody noticed?" (c) "Tell me about a time you persuaded someone." Each should start with the question's own words.

### Step 7: One chunk for each thing asked, in the order asked

If you counted three fingers, you owe three chunks.

Start each chunk with the question's own word for that part. Then the marker hears you move on.

> **Say it like this.** "What I did to fix it was..." and later "What I learned was..."

Reason: a part you never name out loud is a part you probably skipped. This turns "answer every part" into something you can hear yourself do.

**When a chunk is a story**, give it this shape:

1. Set the scene in one or two sentences.
2. Say what you did and why. This gets most of the time. Use "I", not "we".
3. Say what happened in the end. Give a number if you measured one.

Roughly two thirds of a story should be the middle step.

> **Try it now.** The question is: "Tell me about a mistake. What did you do to fix it, and what did you learn?" Say only the three openers, nothing else. "The mistake was..." "What I did to fix it was..." "What I learned was..."

### Step 8: Stop

Decide your last sentence before you start. Say it. Let your voice drop. Stop talking. Click "Done Answering".

Reason: nobody will nod to tell you it landed. The urge to keep talking into the silence is strong, and it costs marks.

A good last sentence is the result, or one line on what you took from it.

> **Try it now.** Say: "Waste fell from forty loaves to ten." Drop your voice on "ten". Then stay silent for two full seconds. Notice the urge to add more. Do not.

### The 30-second window: what really fits

Most banks are reported to give about 30 seconds to prepare. Here is what fits in it.

- An average adult reads a 20-word question in about 5 seconds.
- A 60-word question takes about 15 seconds. A slower reader needs 20 or more.
- Writing four keywords takes 7 to 10 seconds.

So there is no time to write. An earlier guide told you to write keywords in this window. That advice was wrong for the long questions firms now set. **Do not write. Use your fingers.**

Do this:

1. Read once, slowly, to the last word. Count the things asked on your fingers as you go.
2. Look again at the part that holds the asks. It is usually the second half. If the question is short, read all of it again.
3. Choose your material.
4. Say your first sentence silently.
5. Breathe out. Look at the lens.

You may see a "Start Recording" button during the countdown. Leave it alone. Use all the time you are given.

On current HireVue help pages the question stays on the left of the screen while you prepare. Nothing says it disappears when recording starts. Check this on the practice question. If it stays, glance back at it mid-answer.

If reading the question uses most of the window, that is a real barrier, not a lack of effort. Part 10 covers what you can ask for.

### How long to speak

- For a story, aim to finish with a little time left. About 70 to 80 seconds of a 90-second limit.
- For a reason or an opinion, shorter is fine. About 50 to 65 seconds of 90.
- For a one-minute limit, aim for about 50 seconds. That is roughly 120 to 140 words.

A complete answer that stops early is fine. Padding is not.

> **Remember.** Read, choose, say. The reading comes first, and it goes all the way to the last word.

## Part 6: How to answer each common kind of question

Each kind below has the same five things. What they are checking. The trap. The shape. A weak and a strong answer from Sam. A pointer to your own material.

Sam's strong answers are written the way people talk. They are sized for a 60 to 90 second limit. Do not learn them. Read them aloud once to hear the shape.

### Tell me about yourself

**What they are checking.** Can you pick what matters for this job and say it clearly?

**The trap.** Your CV in date order. Or missing a second thing asked, such as "and why you applied".

**The shape.**

1. One sentence: who you are and what you are aiming at.
2. Two or three things you have done, chosen for this job. One fact each.
3. One sentence that lands on this role.

> **Weak answer.** "My name is Sam. I did my GCSEs and A-levels, then started a Business degree. In first year I joined the hiking society. I also got a job at a supermarket. Then last summer I worked at a lettings agency. Now I'm in my final year and I'm looking for internships."

> **Strong answer.** "I'm Sam, a final-year Business student, and I'm aiming for a job on a broking desk. Three things about me fit that. First, I work weekends at a supermarket, and I like the pressure. Last year I noticed we were binning about forty loaves every Sunday. I got the manager to change how we mark them down, and waste fell by three quarters. Second, I'm treasurer of the hiking society. I look after other people's money and I check every pound. Third, last summer I worked in a lettings agency office. I spent my days on the phone between landlords and tenants. That's where I found out I like being the one in the middle. And that's what brought me to broking."

What changed:

- Three chosen things, not seven listed things.
- Each one carries a fact.
- The last line points at the job.

**Your version.** Three things that could do this job for you: running the client inbox at the asset manager, the portal you built there, and debating. Choose the three that fit the role in front of you. They will differ by firm.

### Why this firm

**What they are checking.** Your reason for choosing them, and proof that you looked.

**The trap.** Reciting facts about the firm. The subject of this answer is you.

**The shape.** For each reason, three beats:

1. What I want.
2. The fact that shows this firm has it.
3. The proof from my life that I really want it.

> **Weak answer.** "TP ICAP is a world-leading firm with a great reputation and a presence in 28 countries. I admire its culture and its values of Accountability, Adaptability and Authenticity. I'd love to work with such talented people and learn from the best in a fast-paced environment."

> **Strong answer.** "Two reasons. First, I want to learn broking where there's the most of it to learn from. TP ICAP describes its Global Broking arm as the world's largest broker between dealers, with around 2,700 brokers. And the internship moves you across several desks in six weeks. That suits me, because I learn by sitting next to people doing the job. At the lettings agency I learned more from one week of listening to calls than from the training folder. Second, the firm doesn't trade with its own money. It earns commission for bringing a buyer and a seller together. I like that. The broker only does well when the client's trade gets done. That's the kind of work I enjoyed most last summer."

What changed:

- Each reason starts with what Sam wants.
- The firm facts are few, and each is tied to Sam.
- Swap in another firm's name and it stops being true.

**Your version.** Start with what you want from a firm. Then find one fact. Then your proof. At the asset manager you sat in meetings with brokerage firms. If those showed you what a client values in a broker, that is rare proof for TP ICAP.

### Why this role

**What they are checking.** Do you know what the job is day to day? Does it match things you have done?

**The trap.** Talking about the firm. "Why this role" is next door to "Why this firm". For you this is the most costly swap.

**The shape.**

1. My understanding of the role, in one or two sentences.
2. Two parts of it that match things I already do.
3. One line on why I would enjoy it.

> **Weak answer.** "I want this role because TP ICAP is the largest firm of its kind and has had a very strong year. It would be a fantastic place to start my career and develop my skills in a global business."

> **Strong answer.** "My understanding is that an intern on a broking desk shadows the brokers and helps the desk run. From the firm's trainee adverts, that means three things. Getting trades into the system on time. Keeping the price boards up to date. And sorting out queries when two sides remember a trade differently. Meanwhile you learn to talk to clients. Two parts of that match what I already do. One is accuracy at speed. On a Saturday checkout my till has to balance to the penny after eight hours of rush. The other is sorting out mismatches. As treasurer I check the bank statement against our records every month, and I chase every difference until it's explained. I like that kind of work. I'd like to do it somewhere faster."

What changed:

- It describes the work, not the firm.
- Two tasks from the job are paired with two things Sam does.

**Your version.** For a role serving clients, running the inbox for US institutional clients could be your match. For a role about changing how work gets done, the portal or the 60-day plan could be. Say what the job produces first. Then pair.

### Why this industry

**What they are checking.** Is your interest real and lasting, or did it start last month?

**The trap.** "I've always been passionate about finance." That is a claim with no proof.

**The shape.**

1. Where it started.
2. What you did next, and next.
3. What you want now.

A run of real choices over time is the proof.

> **Weak answer.** "I've always been passionate about finance. The markets are fast-paced and exciting and no two days are the same. I think it's an industry where I could really grow and make an impact."

> **Strong answer.** "Because I've followed markets every week for a year, and I want to work where the prices are made. It started with a second-year module on financial markets. I realised I couldn't explain why the pound had moved. So every Friday since, I've written down where the pound, the ten-year gilt yield and oil ended the week, with one line on why. That's about fifty Fridays now. Then last summer, at a lettings agency, I found I liked being the go-between on a deal. Broking puts those two things together. So this isn't a sudden idea. It's the next step in something I already do every week."

What changed:

- "Always passionate" became a habit you could check.
- The steps are in time order.

**Your version.** You have a real run of choices. Bookkeeping work experience. The student investment fund. The placement year. The summer at the asset manager. The spring week. Put them in true order. Say what each one made you want next.

### A time you... (one real event)

**What they are checking.** One skill, shown through one thing that really happened.

**The trap.** Saying what you usually do. "I'm someone who always..." is not an event. In one study of real job interviews, fewer than one in four answers to this kind of question were one real event.

**The shape.**

1. First sentence: the question's word plus your headline.
2. The scene, in one or two sentences.
3. What I did and why. Two or three actions. Most of the time goes here.
4. What happened.
5. One line on what I took from it, if there is time.

The question here is: "Tell me about a time you improved the way something was done."

> **Weak answer.** "I'm someone who always looks for ways to improve things. At the supermarket where I work we had an issue with waste, so we decided to reduce items earlier, and it made things much more efficient. It taught me a lot about teamwork and initiative."

> **Strong answer.** "The clearest time I improved something was at the supermarket, where I cut our Sunday bread waste by about three quarters. We close at four on Sundays. Bread was being marked down at half past three, and most of it still went in the bin. Nobody had asked me to look at it. I did three things. First, I counted. For three Sundays I wrote down what we threw away. It was about forty loaves each time. I wanted numbers, because 'it feels like a lot' wouldn't persuade anyone. Second, I took the numbers to my manager and suggested marking down at one o'clock, when the shop is still busy. She worried shoppers would wait for cheap bread and stop paying full price. So third, I offered a four-week trial, and I counted the waste and the full-price sales myself. Waste fell from about forty loaves to about ten. Full-price sales stayed the same. One o'clock is now how every Sunday shift does it. What I took from it is that a small count beats a strong opinion."

What changed:

- One event on named Sundays, not "I always".
- "We decided" became "I counted", "I took", "I offered".
- There is a result with numbers.

**Your version.** The portal that replaced the 400-line spreadsheet could be this story. Say what you noticed, the two or three things you did, and what replaced what. Only use a number you really measured.

### What would you do if...

**What they are checking.** Your judgement. They read it from your steps, their order, and who you tell.

**The trap.** Telling a past story. Or saying "I'd prioritise" with no steps. In a live interview someone would ask "and then?". On a recording nobody does, so say the "and then" yourself.

**The shape.**

1. The main risk, in one sentence.
2. My first step.
3. My next steps in order, and who I tell.
4. What I would do if that did not work.

The question here is: "Two people each give you an urgent task, due at the same time. What would you do?"

> **Weak answer.** "I'd stay calm and prioritise by importance. I'm very organised and good at time management, so I would work hard to make sure both tasks were completed to a high standard."

> **Strong answer.** "The risk here is that one of them finds out too late that their work isn't coming. So I wouldn't quietly pick one. First, I'd find out the real deadline for each task and who is waiting on it. 'Urgent' can mean today or it can mean Friday. Second, I'd work out honestly how long each one takes. Third, if I can't do both, I'd tell both people straight away, while they can still change their plans. I'd suggest an order and ask if they agree. If they don't, I'd ask my manager to decide, because it isn't my call whose work matters more. Then I'd do the work in that order and tell each person when theirs is done."

What changed:

- Steps in order, not a claim about being organised.
- Both people are told early.
- It says what happens if the first plan fails.

**Your version.** No story is needed. If there is time, one line of proof helps. Moving fifty market openings forward at once could be that line.

### An honesty or mistake question

**What they are checking.** Will you be honest, and fast, when it costs you something?

In UK finance this has rules behind it. The Financial Conduct Authority is the regulator. Its first conduct rule for staff is "You must act with integrity." Making a mistake is one thing. Hiding it is far worse.

**The trap.** The quiet fix. "I'd correct it before anyone noticed." Markers read that as hiding.

**The shape.** Six words to learn: **priority, stop, tell, pass up, record, prevent.**

1. Say who matters most. Usually the client.
2. Stop it getting worse.
3. Tell your manager now.
4. Pass it up to Compliance if needed. Compliance is the team that checks the firm follows the rules.
5. Write down what happened.
6. Fix the cause.

The question here is: "You realise a report you sent to a client yesterday has a wrong number in it. Nobody has noticed. What do you do?"

> **Weak answer.** "I would quickly correct the number and send the client an updated version so that it didn't cause any problems. I'd make sure to double-check my work in future."

> **Strong answer.** "I'd report it straight away. The client matters more than my embarrassment, and they may be making decisions on a wrong number. First, I'd check exactly what's wrong and how big it is, so I can explain it clearly. That takes minutes, not hours. Then I'd tell my manager the same day, with the right figure ready. I wouldn't send a quiet correction myself. How we tell the client is the firm's decision, not mine, and Compliance may need to know. Once it's corrected, I'd write down what happened. Then I'd find out how the wrong number got through, and change my checks so it can't happen the same way again."

What changed:

- It names who matters most.
- The manager is told before anything is sent.
- It ends with fixing the cause.

**Your version.** No story is needed. You checked performance reports on a $900m account. If you ever found and raised a wrong figure there, one line about it is strong proof. Use it only if it happened.

### Something in the news or markets

**What they are checking.** Do you follow markets? Can you link a story to this firm? Do you have a view?

**The trap.** Summarising the news. The marks are after the "and why".

**The shape.**

1. What happened, with one fact.
2. Why it matters.
3. What it means for this firm or its clients.
4. My view.

The question here is: "Tell us about something in the markets you have followed recently, and why it interested you."

> **Weak answer.** "I've been following the oil market. Oil prices have been very volatile this year because of the conflict in the Middle East, and this has affected inflation and interest rates around the world. It's a really interesting situation and I'm keen to see how it develops."

> **Strong answer.** "The story I've followed most this year is oil. The conflict in the Middle East shook the oil market in the first half of 2026. What interested me was what it did to trading, not only to the price. TP ICAP said in its half-year results that oil derivatives volumes rose in March, when prices were jumping around. Then oil futures volumes fell sharply once physical oil flows were disrupted. I'd assumed a crisis always means more trading. It doesn't. That matters for a broker, because a broker is paid on activity, not on prices going up. In the firm's November update, oil was still quiet while power trading grew strongly. So my view is that a broker with many markets has the steadier income."

What changed:

- It says why it interested Sam, which was the question.
- It links the story to how this firm earns money.
- It ends on a view.

**Your version.** You led the retail sector in a student investment fund and pitched a stock. A story you truly followed there could work, if you tie it to the firm you are speaking to. Check your facts the day before.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**What they are checking.** Do you see yourself accurately? Did you do anything about it?

**The trap.** A strength in disguise. Careers services warn against "I'm a perfectionist" by name.

**The shape for a weakness.**

1. Name it plainly.
2. One time it cost something.
3. What I changed.
4. A sign the change is working.

> **Weak answer.** "I'd say I'm a perfectionist. I sometimes work too hard and care too much about getting every detail right."

> **Strong answer.** "I say yes too quickly. Someone asks for help and I agree before I've checked what I've already promised. Last year I agreed to cover three extra shifts in the week a group project was due. I wrote my section the night before. It was the weakest part, and a teammate had to fix my references. That was a real cost to someone else. So I changed one thing. I don't answer on the spot any more. I say, 'Can I check and tell you tonight?' and I look at my calendar first. This term I've turned down two shifts and handed everything in early."

What changed:

- A real pattern, admitted as real.
- A cost someone else paid.
- One change, and a sign that it is holding.

**The shape for a strength.** Name it. Give one event that shows it. Say how often you use it.

> **Say it like this.** "I'm careful with numbers under pressure. My till balances to the penny after a Saturday rush, and I do that every weekend."

**Your version.** Only you know your real pattern. Pick a weakness that is true, that cost something small, and that you have already changed.

### Something not on your CV

**What they are checking.** The person behind the CV. Can you talk about something real, with detail?

**The trap.** Reaching for your best CV story. The limiting words rule that out.

**The shape.**

1. The thing.
2. One detail only someone who does it would know.
3. What it shows about you.

> **Weak answer.** "Something not on my CV is that I'm a very hard-working and determined person, as shown by my supermarket job and my role as treasurer."

> **Strong answer.** "I fix up old bikes. I buy them cheap, repair them and sell them on. The last one was a 1980s Raleigh I got for £25. It needed new brake cables and a chain. I learned how from videos, and I got it wrong the first time. I sold it for £70. I do it because I like taking something apart to see why it doesn't work. And I like that there's a number at the end telling me if I did it well."

What changed:

- It is truly not on the CV.
- It has a detail you could not make up.
- It says what the hobby shows.

**Your version.** Read the limit word for word. Your CV lists collecting boarding passes and blending spices. For "not on your CV", those are on it. A skill behind them that the CV does not spell out could still count. For "outside work and study", both are allowed.

## Part 7: Mixed and reworded questions

You asked what to do when the wording is different from what you prepared. And what to do when "why this firm" and "why this role" arrive in one question.

The answer is to stop preparing answers. Prepare blocks.

### Why one long script breaks

A script is one piece. It fits one question. When the question changes, you have two bad choices. Deliver the script anyway and miss the question. Or cut it up live, under a timer, which nobody can do well.

A block is small. It answers one thing. You can put blocks in any order and stop after any one of them.

### Build three small blocks for each firm

Each block is one or two sentences of reason, plus one proof from your own life. Nothing more.

- **Block A: why this firm.**
- **Block B: why this industry.** For TP ICAP, that means why broking.
- **Block C: why this role.**

Here are Sam's three for TP ICAP.

> **Say it like this.** Block A, why TP ICAP: "TP ICAP, because it's where there's the most broking to learn from. It calls its Global Broking arm the world's largest broker between dealers, and the internship moves across several desks in six weeks. I learn fastest sitting next to people doing the job. That's how I learned at the lettings agency."

> **Say it like this.** Block B, why broking: "Broking, because I like being the one in the middle who gets two sides to agree. Last summer at a lettings agency I spent my days on the phone between landlords and tenants. My best moment was getting a stalled let through by finding out what each side really needed."

> **Say it like this.** Block C, why this role: "The role, as I understand it, is helping the desk run while you learn. Getting trades into the system on time, keeping prices up to date, sorting out queries. That fits me. My till balances to the penny after a Saturday rush, and as treasurer I chase every mismatch in the accounts."

Sam keeps two spare blocks as well.

- **What a broker does.** A plain explanation, for "What is a broker?"
- **My markets habit.** The Friday notebook, as proof of interest.

### Hold each block as key words, not sentences

Write each block on a card as five or six words. Sam's Block B card says: "middle. lettings. phone all day. stalled let. four days."

Say the block aloud from the card. Then say it again in different words. Do this until you can say it three different ways.

Reason: if the sentences change every time, there is no script to take over. What you keep is the point and the proof.

### The same blocks, five different questions

Watch what changes each time: the first sentence, the order, and how much of each block is used. The blocks themselves stay the same.

**Question 1: "Why TP ICAP and why broking?"**

Read it: a reason. Two things. The firm is asked first.

> **Strong answer.** "TP ICAP because it's where there's the most broking to learn from, and broking because I like being the one in the middle. On TP ICAP first. It calls its Global Broking arm the world's largest broker between dealers, and the internship moves you across several desks in six weeks. I learn fastest sitting next to people doing the job. That's how I learned at the lettings agency last summer. On broking. At that agency I spent my days on the phone between landlords and tenants. One let had stalled over the move-in date. I asked each side what they really needed. They were four days apart, and it went through that afternoon. I want to do that for a living, in a faster market."

Blocks used: A, then B. The first sentence answers both halves.

**Question 2: "What attracts you to this role at our firm?"**

Read it: a reason. One question mark, but two things inside: the role, and the firm. The role comes first.

> **Strong answer.** "Two things attract me: the work itself, and doing it here. The work first. As I understand it, an intern helps the desk run while learning from the brokers. That means getting trades into the system on time, keeping prices up to date and sorting out queries. That fits me. My till balances to the penny after a Saturday rush, and as treasurer I chase every mismatch in the accounts until it's explained. Then the firm. TP ICAP has around 2,700 brokers, and the internship moves across several desks. So I'd see more kinds of broking in six weeks here than I could anywhere smaller. And I learn best by watching people who are good at it."

Blocks used: C, then A. No Block B. Nobody asked "why broking".

**Question 3: "Tell me about yourself and why you think you are a good candidate."**

Read it: an introduction, then a reason about you. Two things. Firm facts will not help with either.

> **Strong answer.** "I'm Sam, a final-year Business student. I work weekends at a supermarket and I'm treasurer of the hiking society. Last summer I worked in a lettings agency office. That's where I found I like being the one in the middle of a deal. Why I think I'm a good candidate. Your adverts ask for attention to detail, for people who get on with clients, and for real interest in markets. I do all three already, in small ways. Detail. My till balances to the penny after eight hours of Saturday rush. Clients. At the agency I got a stalled let through by finding out what each side really needed. Markets. Every Friday for a year I've written down where the pound, gilt yields and oil finished, and why. I don't know the job yet. But the habits are there."

Blocks used: a short introduction, then only the proofs from B, C and the markets habit. The reasons are left out, because the question is about Sam. Sam says "Why I think I'm a good candidate" out loud, so the marker hears the second part begin.

**Question 4: "Why broking and not trading or sales?"**

Read it: a reason. The limiting words are "and not trading or sales". So Sam has to compare.

> **Strong answer.** "Broking, because I want to be the person in the middle, not the person holding the risk. As I understand it, a trader at a bank owns a position, and its profit or loss. A salesperson sells their own bank's prices to that bank's clients. A broker sits between many firms, finds the other side of a trade, and earns commission when it's done. TP ICAP doesn't trade with its own money at all. That middle seat is the one I've enjoyed. At a lettings agency last summer I was the go-between for landlords and tenants. I wasn't on either side. My job was to get them both to yes, and I liked that more than selling. So it's broking, on purpose."

Blocks used: B, with a comparison added. Without the comparison, the limiting words would be ignored.

**Question 5: "What do you know about what a broker does, and why would you be good at it?"**

Read it: an explanation, then a reason. Two things.

> **Strong answer.** "A broker sits between two firms that want to trade, for example two banks. One wants to buy and one wants to sell. The broker finds the other side, helps them agree a price, and earns commission when the trade is done. The broker doesn't bet the firm's own money. A lot of it happens by phone and chat, so it runs on trust and speed. Why would I be good at it? Two reasons. I'm used to being the go-between. I spent last summer passing offers between landlords and tenants and getting them to agree. And I'm accurate when it's busy. My till balances to the penny after a Saturday rush. A wrong number on a trade matters far more, and I'd treat it that way."

Blocks used: the "what a broker does" block, then the proofs from B and C.

> **Remember.** Five questions. The same few small blocks. Only the order, the first sentence and the amounts changed.

> **Try it now.** Take Sam's three cards. Answer "Why this role, and why TP ICAP?" out loud using C then A. Then answer "Why TP ICAP, and why this role?" using A then C. Notice that you can do it without learning anything new.

### One real event, told three ways

The same works for stories. Sam has one bread-waste event. It holds three different moments:

- Sam noticed the waste and counted it.
- Sam persuaded the manager, who had a worry.
- The trial went wrong in week one and Sam fixed it.

The question decides which moment gets the time.

**"Tell me about a time you improved the way something was done."** You read this one in Part 6. The time went on the counting and the result. The persuading got two sentences. The week-one problem was left out.

**"Tell me about a time you had to persuade someone senior. How did you deal with their objections?"** Two things: the event, and the objections.

> **Strong answer.** "The clearest time I persuaded someone senior was when I got my store manager to change how we mark down bread on Sundays. We were marking it down at half past three and binning about forty loaves a week. I wanted to move it to one o'clock. I knew my opinion alone wouldn't do it. So I counted the waste for three Sundays first and took her the numbers. How I dealt with her objection. She worried shoppers would wait for cheap bread and stop paying full price. I didn't argue. I said it was a fair worry and I didn't know the answer. So I offered a four-week trial, and said I'd count the full-price sales myself, so she'd see the risk in numbers. She agreed to that. Full-price sales held, waste fell to about ten loaves, and she kept the change."

**"Tell me about a time a plan did not work at first. What did you do?"** Two things: what went wrong, and what Sam did.

> **Strong answer.** "A plan of mine that didn't work at first was a trial to cut bread waste at the supermarket. My manager had agreed we'd mark bread down at one o'clock on Sundays, not half past three. In week one it didn't happen. Two colleagues did it at the old time, out of habit. Nobody had told them properly, and that was my gap. I'd sold the idea to the manager and forgotten the people who actually do the job. So I did two things. I put a note on the pricing gun with the new time. And I asked the shift lead to mention it in the morning briefing, because it would land better from her than from me. From week two it was done at one. By the end of the trial, waste was down from about forty loaves to about ten."

What changed across the three:

- The first sentence uses each question's own words: "improved", "persuaded someone senior", "didn't work at first".
- A different moment gets most of the time.
- The scene and the result barely change.

**Your version.** The portal you built could hold three moments like this. Spotting the problem. Getting teams to use it. Something that went wrong on the way. You know which of these really happened. Write each as one line on a card.

Keep each of your events on one card:

- The scene, in one line.
- Three or four separate things you did. One line each.
- The result.
- One thing you learned.
- One thing you would do differently.

No full sentences on the card.

### The rule for splitting your time

1. **Every part gets one sentence before any part gets a second.** Then you can never run out of time with a part unanswered.
2. **Two things asked: about half each.** Three things: about a third each.
3. **If one part is a story and the other is "what did you learn", the story can take two thirds.** The lesson still needs its own clear sentences.
4. **At the halfway point on the timer, you should be starting your last part.** If you are not, finish your sentence and move on.

On a one-minute answer with two parts, that is about 25 seconds each.

### What to collect for each firm, in one hour

Stop when the list is full. This replaces open-ended research.

- [ ] The firm's values, in its own words.
- [ ] What the role produces day to day, in one sentence, from the advert.
- [ ] Two facts that set the firm apart from a named rival.
- [ ] One recent news item about the firm, and your view on it.
- [ ] The format: seconds to prepare, seconds to answer, number of tries.

Then build blocks A, B and C from it.

## Part 8: How to talk and act on camera

Everything in this part matters less than answering the question. A charming answer to the wrong question still fails. Fix the reading first. Then use this part.

Each item is one thing to do, with the reason.

### Set up the picture

1. **Sit at a table on a chair that does not swivel.** A swinging chair shows nerves.
2. **Raise the laptop on books until the camera is level with your eyes.** A low laptop makes the camera look up your nose.
3. **Put your face and shoulders in the middle of the picture.**
4. **Put the light in front of you.** A lamp or a window behind you turns you into a shadow.
5. **Clear what is behind you.** Everything the camera sees is recorded.

### Where to look

1. **Look at the lens while you speak.** To the viewer, the lens is eye contact. The screen is not.
2. **Stick a small face or a coloured dot right beside the lens.** It gives your eyes somewhere to go.
3. **Click "Hide Video" if you see it.** This hides your own picture. Watching yourself takes attention away from the answer.
4. **Keep nothing to read at the side of the screen.** In one experiment, looking a little above or below the lens did no harm. Looking sideways did.

### How loud and how fast

1. **Speak a little louder than feels normal.** Talk as if the person is across a table. People think they sound lively on camera when viewers hear them as flat.
2. **Aim for about 150 words a minute.** That is ordinary talking speed. Radio newsreaders go a little faster.
3. **Slow down for names and numbers. Speed up for background.**

How to measure your speed:

1. Record yourself answering a question for one minute.
2. Use your phone's dictation, or the portal, to turn it into text.
3. Count the words.

Under about 130, speed up a little. Slow speech is the one habit viewers reliably read as nerves. Over about 180, slow down. The marker may be playing you at faster speed already.

### Sentences, pauses and endings

1. **One idea per sentence.** If you need a breath in the middle, it is two sentences.
2. **Pause for a beat when the idea changes.** Silence feels long to you. To a listener it is thinking time.
3. **Let your voice drop at the end of each sentence.** A rising end sounds like a question. A falling end sounds sure.

> **Try it now.** Say "My till balances to the penny" twice. First with your voice going up at the end. Then with it going down. Hear the difference. Use the second.

### Starting and ending

1. **Start within a second of the countdown ending.** You see 3, 2, 1. Then speak. Long delays before answering lowered marks in real interviews.
2. **Have your first sentence ready.** You said it silently during the preparation time.
3. **Decide your last sentence before you start.** Say it, drop your voice, and stop.
4. **Hold your eyes on the lens for one second. Then click "Done Answering".**

Do not open by thanking the viewer. It uses time and earns nothing. Do not end with "so, yeah, that's it."

### After a slip

1. **Do not comment on it.** No "sorry" and no "I'm so nervous". Remarks like that cost more than the slip did.
2. **Pause. Say the sentence again, correctly.**
3. **If you lose your thread, go back to the last thing you said.** Do not hunt for what comes next.

> **Say it like this.** "Let me put that more clearly." Or: "So, to step back. I'd counted the waste, and I'd taken the numbers to my manager."

Markers say answers do not have to be perfect. Carry on.

### Hands, face and jokes

1. **Let your hands move, low down near the table.** Radio trainers get presenters to use their hands, because it makes the voice livelier.
2. **Smile as you start and as you finish.** Do not hold a smile through the middle. In the one experiment on this, more smiling went with lower hiring scores for a serious job.
3. **Do not plan jokes.** A joke only helps when the listener laughs. A recording has nobody to laugh.

### A two-minute voice warm-up

This comes from an NHS speech therapy leaflet. Do it just before you record.

1. Drop your shoulders. Loosen your jaw.
2. Breathe in through your nose and out through your mouth. Three times.
3. Yawn, and sigh the breath out. Three times.
4. Blow air through loose lips, like a horse. "Brrr." Slide the sound up and down.
5. Hold "sss", then "zzz", then "vvv" for a few seconds each.
6. Sip some water.

Then say one practice answer in a silly, over-the-top voice. Then say it normally. Broadcast coaches use this to shake out a flat voice.

### Make the lens feel like a person

1. **Tell the story to a real person first.** A friend, a flatmate, anyone.
2. **Then tell it to the lens, with a face stuck beside it.**
3. **Before your first sentence, say a friend's name in your head.** "Hey, Jo." One radio presenter starts her scripts with "Hey mum" for this reason.
4. **Picture the place where it happened while you talk.** Your voice follows your attention.

7. You should see your own picture. A small bar should move when you speak. That bar is the microphone.
8. Choose **"Try a practice question"**.

### The practice question

Practice mode has a **blue ribbon** across the top of the screen. If you can see the blue ribbon, nothing counts.

- There are three general practice questions.
- You can do them as many times as you like.
- You can watch each one back. Nobody else ever sees them.
- Once you leave the playback, the recording is gone.

Use the practice to learn the buttons and to check sound and picture. In the real interview you cannot watch anything back.

If you ever land in the real interview by mistake, close the browser window. Then open the link from your email again.

### What happens on each question

1. **The question appears on the left.** A countdown at the top shows your preparation time. The screen says "Not Recording".
2. **There is a "Start Recording" button.** It lets you skip the rest of the preparation time. Do not click it.
3. **When the preparation time ends, you see 3, 2, 1.** Then recording begins.
4. **A second countdown shows your answer time.** Speak.
5. **When you have finished, click "Done Answering".** You do not have to use all the time. If the time runs out, it sends your answer by itself.

### Three things that catch people out

- **There is no going back.** Once an answer is sent, you cannot return to that question.
- **The number of tries is set by the firm.** A page tells you before you begin. If you get more than one try, only your last recording is kept.
- **Clicking the link does not start the interview.** Clicking "Begin" or "Start Now" does.

### Just before the real thing

Run the hour-before list on Card 3 in Part 13. The two that matter most: close every other tab and window, and close Teams, Zoom and FaceTime. Switching windows during the interview can be flagged.

Put your cards away too. Some firms ban notes. And you are breaking a reading habit, so give yourself nothing to read.

### If something breaks

- **"Camera and microphone access required."** Click the padlock at the left of the address bar. Set Camera and Microphone to "Allow". Reload the page.
- **"Camera in use by another program."** Close Teams, Zoom or FaceTime. Try again.
- **The screen freezes.** Refresh the page. If that fails, close the browser and open the link from your email again. You return to your last unanswered question.
- **The internet drops in the middle of an answer.** That answer may already have been sent. Finish the interview. Then email the firm and say what happened.
- **The upload gets stuck at the end.** Refresh. If needed, switch to another network or a phone hotspot.

HireVue's help desk is open all day, every day. Afterwards, the code at the bottom of your invitation email lets you check the interview shows as complete.

## Part 10: Extra time and other adjustments

This part is for you if reading the question in the time given is hard, for any reason. That includes dyslexia, whether or not it has ever been diagnosed. If it does not apply to you, skip to Part 11.

This is information. It is not legal or medical advice.

### What UK law says

Under the Equality Act 2010, an employer must make reasonable changes to its selection process for a disabled applicant. The duty starts once the employer knows about the difficulty.

Official guidance names dyslexia as something that can count. The test is about the effect on you, not about holding a certificate.

ACAS is the government-funded workplace advice service. It says a person does not need a diagnosis, and is not legally required to give proof. Some firms still ask for a document, so practice varies.

### Asking is normal

Firms have teams for this. Several say in writing that asking will not count against you.

- Deutsche Bank: "Requesting adjustments will never negatively impact your application."
- Legal & General: "Requesting support or adjustments won't affect your application."
- Deloitte says the same in similar words.

The official code for employers says this information should be kept separate from the hiring decision.

One honest gap. No firm publishes figures comparing people who asked with people who did not. What is certain is the other side of it. If you do not ask, the 30-second window stays as it is.

### What to ask for

Ask for the thing that removes the barrier. If the barrier is reading the question in time, that means **more time before recording starts**.

- **A longer preparation time for each question.** This fits the problem best. Ask for a set amount, such as two or three minutes.
- **The questions shortly before the interview**, where a firm offers that.
- **A live interview where someone speaks the question**, if a recorded one does not work for you.

Be careful with the standard offer. Firms usually suggest 25% extra time. On a 30-second window, 25% is about 8 seconds. Even 50% is only 15 seconds. That barely helps.

Preparation time is one setting the employer controls. BlackRock's own candidate page says three minutes to prepare for each question. So a longer window is an ordinary thing to ask for.

### How and when to ask

**Ask before you start.** Once a question has appeared, it is too late for that interview.

1. **Ask on the day the invitation arrives.** Firms need a few days.
2. **Email the firm's adjustments contact**, or the recruiter named on your invitation.
3. **Say what you need, not what you have.** You do not have to name a condition. Describe the difficulty and the change that fixes it.
4. **Ask for a reply before your deadline.** Ask them to extend the deadline if they need longer.
5. **Ask again at every stage.** Some firms do not carry a request forward.

There is also a link inside HireVue. On the first page of the interview, look for "Click here to request accommodations". Then click "Request Additional Time". Two warnings:

- The employer can switch this link off. It may not be there.
- HireVue does not say how much time it adds. It does not say whether it lengthens the preparation time or only the answer time.

So do not rely on the link alone. Email the firm as well.

### A model email

> Subject: Adjustment request for my video interview
>
> Dear Recruitment Team,
>
> I have been invited to complete a recorded video interview for [programme].
>
> I read on-screen text slowly under time pressure. With a short preparation window, I can spend most of it reading the question.
>
> Could I have a longer preparation time for each question, for example three minutes? If that is not possible, could I see the questions shortly before I begin?
>
> My deadline is [date]. Could you confirm before then, or extend it? I am happy to talk this through by phone.
>
> Thank you.
>
> [Name and application reference]

The square brackets are the only blanks. Fill them in. Send only what is true for you. Never claim a diagnosis you do not have.

### What firms publish

This is from each firm's own careers pages, checked in November 2026.

- **Deloitte.** For video interviews: "Extended response and preparation time where required". Also: "You don't need a formal diagnosis or proof."
- **Legal & General.** "Interview questions may be shared in advance where appropriate."
- **Deutsche Bank.** An "Adjustments Concierge" email address. You do not need to share health details.
- **EY.** Extra time, usually up to 25%. No medical evidence required.
- **Barclays and KPMG.** Both name dyslexia and list extra time.
- **HSBC.** Extra time for interviews. You can zoom in on its assessments.
- **UBS.** Its form is reported to offer 25% or 50% extra time for the recorded video interview.
- **BlackRock and Schroders.** Each gives an email address for requests.
- **Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley.** A general line and a contact address, with no examples. Spell out what you want.
- **Lazard.** Asks for "supporting documentation".
- **TP ICAP.** Nothing was found on its careers pages. The general address is AskTalentAcquisition@tpicap.com. Writing there, or to the recruiter on your invitation, is my best guess at the route.

### If you have no diagnosis

1. **You can still ask.** Describe the difficulty truthfully. The model email does this without naming any condition.
2. **Contact your university's disability service.** Many offer a free screening. Some pay for a full assessment. Do this while you are still a student.
3. **Know the difference.** A checklist or screening can only say that dyslexia is likely. Only a full assessment can say whether someone has it. A private one costs roughly £300 to £900.
4. **Keep any paperwork you already hold.** If you ever had extra time in exams, that letter is the kind of document some firms mean.

### Free things to try on your own

None of these needs anyone's permission. No study has tested them in a timed interview. Try each one in practice and keep what works.

- **Zoom the browser.** Hold Ctrl and press the plus key. On a Mac, hold Command and press plus. Set it during the practice question.
- **Read the question aloud, under your breath.** The screen says "Not Recording" during preparation.
- **Count the parts on your fingers as you read.** Then you do not have to hold them in your head.
- **Glance back at the question during your answer**, if it stays on screen.
- **Hide your own picture.**

Two cautions. Coloured screens are harmless to try, but research does not show that they help. And do not run software that reads the screen aloud unless the firm has agreed. Firms ban outside tools during the interview.

## Part 11: TP ICAP, worked through

### What TP ICAP is, in plain words

TP ICAP is a broker for big financial firms. Its clients are banks, asset managers, hedge funds, companies and trading houses.

Here is the business in four steps.

1. A bank wants to sell something large. A block of government bonds, say.
2. It contacts a TP ICAP broker.
3. The broker finds a buyer among the firm's other clients and helps the two sides agree a price.
4. TP ICAP is paid commission.

TP ICAP does not bet its own money on markets. In 2025, about six pounds in every ten of its income came from trades where it only introduced the buyer and seller.

It has four parts:

- **Global Broking.** The largest part, with over half the income. Its brands are ICAP and Tullett Prebon. It covers interest rates, currencies, shares and company debt.
- **Energy and Commodities.** Oil, gas and power.
- **Liquidnet.** Helps asset managers and other investors trade large orders.
- **Parameta Solutions.** Sells the price data that the brokers' trades create.

A few facts to know:

- About 5,400 staff, around 2,700 of them brokers.
- 60 offices in 28 countries. The London base is 135 Bishopsgate.
- Formed in 2016, when Tullett Prebon bought ICAP's broking business. Its roots in London go back to 1866.
- The chief executive is Nicolas Breteau.

### How a broker earns money, and what a trainee does

A broker is paid on trades done, not on prices going up. TP ICAP says brokers get a salary, plus a large part that depends on their desk's income and on their conduct.

The London internship is reported as six weeks at 135 Bishopsgate, moving across several desks. Interns are then considered for a permanent job.

The London advert could not be read in full. The firm's adverts for other offices list this kind of work:

- Shadowing brokers while they trade.
- Entering trades into the system on time.
- Keeping price boards up to date.
- Giving live prices to traders by chat message.
- Sorting out queries when the two sides of a trade disagree.
- Talking to clients by phone and in person.

They say the firm wants five things. People who get on with clients quickly. The adverts call this "rapport". People who stay calm when things move fast. Care with detail. Acting without being asked. And a view on markets.

**Broker, trader, salesperson.** You may be asked the difference.

- A **trader** at a bank owns a position. That means the bank's own money is in the trade, and the trader carries the profit or loss.
- A **salesperson** at a bank sells that bank's prices to that bank's clients.
- A **broker** sits between many firms and takes no position.

The salesperson line is my plain summary, not the firm's wording.

### The firm's current values

TP ICAP calls them its "Triple-A" values. In its own Code of Conduct they mean:

- **Accountability.** Own your results. "Speak up when things are not right."
- **Adaptability.** Learn every day, including from mistakes.
- **Authenticity.** This is the firm's word for honesty. "Keep your promises." "Be honest, lead by example, and play by rules."

Older websites list a different set: honesty, integrity, respect and excellence. Do not quote that set as current.

Do not recite the three words in an answer. Show one of them through something you did.

### Dates worth knowing

Pick two. Do not list them all.

- **3 November 2026.** Income for the first nine months of 2026 was £1,912 million, up 9% after removing currency moves. Global Broking grew 16% in the latest quarter. The firm picked out shares and interest rates as strong this year.
- **6 August 2026.** Half-year results. The firm said it had expanded its junior and trainee broker programmes. It bought a firm called Vantage Capital Markets.
- **12 March 2026.** Income for 2025 was £2,353 million.

Its main rivals are BGC and Tradition. BGC has been growing fast. So do not call TP ICAP the unchallenged number one. Say what the firm itself says: its Global Broking arm is "the world's largest inter-dealer broker".

### What is known about the video interview, and what is not

**Known:**

- TP ICAP says its recruitment team reviews every application. It says technology supports human judgement and does not replace it.
- Candidates report the order as CV check, then HireVue, then an assessment day with a group task and interviews.

**Not known:**

- TP ICAP publishes nothing about the format.
- One anonymous report says four questions, one minute each, with no second try. You have been told three questions. The reports do not agree.

**Your invitation email is the authority.** So is the page inside the link that lists the number of questions. Read both before you practise. Until you know the answer time, practise at one minute and at two.

### Question themes candidates have reported

None of these is confirmed by the firm. All come from anonymous candidates.

- "What attracts you to broking?"
- "Why TP ICAP?"
- "What is a broker?" or "What is an interdealer broker?"
- "Tell me about yourself and why you think you are a good candidate."
- From later rounds: broking against trading, handling stress, a time someone disagreed.

So prepare for five families: why broking, why TP ICAP, what a broker does, about you, and pressure or disagreement. Expect them reworded and mixed, as in Part 7.

### Sam's three blocks again, in different words

These are the same blocks as in Part 7. The wording is different on purpose. That is how blocks should work.

> **Say it like this.** Why TP ICAP: "I want to learn where there's the widest range of broking. TP ICAP's Global Broking arm calls itself the world's largest of its kind, and the internship moves through several desks in six weeks. I pick things up fastest by sitting beside people doing the job, which is how I learned at the lettings agency."

> **Say it like this.** Why broking: "I like being the go-between. I spent last summer on the phone between landlords and tenants. The part I enjoyed most was getting two people who were stuck to agree. A broker does that between banks and funds, and is paid when the trade happens."

> **Say it like this.** Why this role: "As I understand it, an intern supports the desk: trades entered on time, prices kept current, queries sorted out. That's close to what I do now. My till balances to the penny after a busy Saturday, and as treasurer I chase every mismatch until it's explained."

### What to swap in from your own life

Keep the shape. Change every proof to yours.

- [ ] Where Sam says "lettings agency", put the experience that made you want to be the person in the middle.
- [ ] Where Sam says "my till balances to the penny", put your proof of accuracy under pressure.
- [ ] Where Sam says "I chase every mismatch", put your proof of sorting out queries.
- [ ] Where Sam says "Friday notebook", put what you really do to follow markets.
- [ ] Where Sam says "I learn fastest beside people doing the job", put your true reason for choosing this firm.

### How your own experience could connect to broking

These are possibilities. Use only the ones that are true.

- **You ran a client inbox for US institutional clients.** Broking is client service at speed. If you enjoyed owning those replies, that could be your "why broking" proof.
- **You moved fifty market openings forward by chasing documents from outside banks.** A trainee chases trade queries until they are sorted. This could show that you chase politely and do not let go.
- **You sat in meetings with brokerage firms.** You have seen brokers from the client's side. If you remember what made one good, that is rare material. Be accurate about what kind of broker they were.
- **You worked at an asset manager.** Asset managers are among TP ICAP's clients.
- **You spent a spring week on a trading floor.** Traders are a broker's clients. Running a pretend portfolio over eleven trades could show you understand the person on the other end of the phone. It also invites "why not trading?", so have that answer ready.
- **You debate.** The adverts ask for people who "think on their feet" and stay composed.
- **You pitched a stock to a student fund.** The adverts ask for a view on markets.

### Background for an honesty question

A broker is paid on trades. So the temptation in this job is to put income before doing the right thing. Know these facts. Do not raise them unless asked.

- **2013.** The Financial Conduct Authority fined ICAP Europe £14 million. Between 2006 and 2010, some of its brokers helped bank traders to rig a key published interest rate, called LIBOR.
- **2019.** It fined Tullett Prebon Europe £15.4 million. Between 2008 and 2010, brokers arranged trades with no real purpose to earn commission. Lavish client entertainment was part of it. The firm was also not open with the regulator.
- Both cases date from before TP ICAP was formed in 2016. The research found no later Financial Conduct Authority fine. That is not proof there is none.

What the firm's Code of Conduct says now:

- "Speak up when things are not right."
- "No one, at any level, has the authority to ask you to do something that is a breach of this Code."
- Client information is kept in strict confidence.
- Business calls and messages stay on the firm's recorded systems.
- Taking clients out is encouraged, within rules and with approval first. So do not say you would refuse all client hospitality.

Here is a TP ICAP-style question: "A client you get on well with asks what another client has been doing in the market. What would you do?"

> **Strong answer.** "I wouldn't tell them. What another client is doing is confidential, and the whole business rests on clients trusting us with it. I'd say so politely and directly: 'I can't talk about other clients, and I wouldn't talk about you to them either.' I'd keep the conversation on the firm's recorded lines, not a personal phone. Then I'd tell my desk head the same day, even though nothing went wrong, because they should know the client asked. If the client pushed again, or offered something in return, I'd take it to Compliance straight away. I'd rather lose a bit of goodwill with one client than lose every client's trust."

It follows the six words from Part 6. Priority, stop, tell, pass up. It also names the trade-off: one client's goodwill against every client's trust.

## Part 12: How to practise so the script cannot take over

### Why your old practice did not work

Saying the same answer to the same question again and again builds a script. It feels like progress. It trains the exact habit that is failing you.

What works is the opposite. A new question every time, so you have to read it. Then a check on whether you answered it.

### The practice portal built for you

A practice portal has been built alongside this guide. It is called the [HireVue Practice Room](https://claude.ai/artifact/FbfrGNoYHcAxtKQ4P8QWt8). Open it in Chrome or Edge on a computer. Here is what it does.

- **It shows a freshly worded question each time.** You cannot learn the questions, so you have to read them.
- **It runs the countdowns**, like the real thing.
- **It listens to your spoken answer and writes down what you said.**
- **It marks whether you answered each part of the question.**
- **It runs a "guess the question" check.** It tests whether your answer, on its own, points back to the question you were given.

One thing it cannot do. **It does not record video.** If you want to watch yourself, prop your phone beside the laptop and film at the same time.

### A daily routine: 20 to 30 minutes

1. **Warm up your voice.** Two minutes. Use the warm-up in Part 8.
2. **Read only. Five questions.** For each, say aloud the kind, the count and any limiting word. Do not answer. Five minutes.
3. **First sentences only. Three questions.** Say the first sentence and stop. Three minutes.
4. **Full answers. Three questions.** One try each. Use the real timings from your invitation. Ten minutes.
5. **Read your marks.** Write one line in a notebook: what was missed, and one thing to change tomorrow. Five minutes.

Rules for every session:

- Never answer the same question twice in a row.
- Never write an answer out in full.
- Say each block in different words from yesterday.
- Stop when the session ends. More tries do not mean better tries.

Once or twice a week, add pressure. Tell a friend they will see one of your recordings. In studies from sport and policing, practising under a little pressure helped people perform under it.

### What to do with the marks

- **A part was marked as missed.** Go back to Step 3. Count on your fingers. Give every part one sentence before any part gets two.
- **"Guess the question" came back as a different question.** A script took over. Do the first-sentence drill on ten questions.
- **You gave the wrong kind of answer.** Go back to Step 2. Learn the opening words.
- **You broke a limiting word.** Put the limiting word inside your first sentence, every time.
- **You do well with two minutes to prepare, and badly with thirty seconds.** Your reading is fine. The window is the barrier. See Part 10, and do more "read only" drills.
- **Everything is marked as answered, several days running.** The mismatch may not be your main problem. Then look at your CV, any online tests, and how early you apply.

A sensible target: every part answered in four of five answers, for five days in a row. That number is my rule of thumb, not a research finding.

If you film on your phone, watch one clip a day in two passes. First with the sound off: where do your eyes go? Then with the screen covered: do you sound as if you are reading?

### The days before TP ICAP

Squeeze this into fewer days if your deadline is close. Keep the order.

**The day the invitation arrives**

1. Read the email. Write down the number of questions, the preparation time, the answer time, the number of tries and the deadline.
2. If you want an adjustment, send the email from Part 10 today.
3. Open the link. Run the equipment test. Do the practice questions under the blue ribbon. Close the browser.

**Two days of building**

1. Spend one hour on the firm list in Part 7. Part 11 has most of it.
2. Write your blocks A, B and C on cards, in key words.
3. Write three of your real events on cards.
4. Say each block aloud three times, in three different wordings.

**Two or three days of portal practice**

1. Run the daily routine with TP ICAP themes.
2. Set the timer to what your invitation says.
3. Include one honesty question and one "what is a broker" question each day.

**The day before**

1. One short set of three questions. Read the marks. Stop.
2. Check the laptop, the charger, the light and the room.
3. Go to bed on time.

Do not leave the interview to the last hours of the deadline. Do not do it tired.

### Other tools, briefly

- **HireVue's own practice questions.** Inside your invitation link. Unlimited and private. They teach the buttons and the timer. They give no feedback.
- **Shortlist.Me.** Many UK universities pay for it. It records video. Its feedback covers speed, filler words and use of time. It does not check whether you answered the question.
- **Graduates First.** Paid. Its feedback is about the emotions it thinks you show. Ignore that part.
- **Yoodli.** A chat with a computer interviewer. Good on pace and filler words.
- **Bright Network.** A free one-off practice with four set questions and no feedback.
- **Finbound.** Aimed at finance. It says it scores the text of short timed answers. Its page does not say it checks each part of the question.
- **SimplyTK.** You already know this one. The research found nothing about it, so I cannot describe it.

The test of any tool is simple. Does it change the question every time? Does it check that every part was answered? None of the documented tools says it does both.

## Part 13: Cards to keep

### Card 1: Eight steps for every question

1. Read to the last word.
2. What kind of answer? One real event, what I would do, how I usually do it, a reason, an opinion.
3. Count the things asked. Fingers.
4. Find any limiting word.
5. Choose real material that fits.
6. First sentence uses the question's own words.
7. One chunk for each thing asked, in the order asked.
8. Say my last sentence. Stop.

My job is to give this marker what this question asks for.

### Card 2: The 30-second window

1. Read once, slowly, to the last word. Count the parts on my fingers.
2. Look again at the part with the asks.
3. Choose my material. The first true thing that fits.
4. Say my first sentence silently.
5. Breathe out. Eyes on the lens.

No writing. No "Start Recording" button. If a script pops up, ignore it and keep reading.

### Card 3: The day before and the hour before

**The day before**

- [ ] Invitation read: number of questions, preparation time, answer time, tries, deadline.
- [ ] Any adjustment agreed in writing.
- [ ] Blocks A, B and C on cards, in key words.
- [ ] One short practice set done. Then stop.
- [ ] Laptop on books, light in front, plain background.

**The hour before**

- [ ] Charger plugged in.
- [ ] Phone silent. Notifications off.
- [ ] Every other tab and window closed. Teams, Zoom and FaceTime closed.
- [ ] Face or dot stuck beside the lens.
- [ ] Voice warm-up done. Water nearby.
- [ ] One practice question under the blue ribbon. Sound checked.
- [ ] Own picture hidden, if the button is there.
- [ ] Cards put away. Nothing to read from.

### Card 4: Blocks to prepare for each firm

- [ ] Block A: why this firm. One reason, one firm fact, one proof from my life.
- [ ] Block B: why this industry. Where it started, what I did next.
- [ ] Block C: why this role. What the job produces, two things I do that match.
- [ ] Spare: what the job is, explained simply.
- [ ] Spare: how I follow markets.
- [ ] Three real events on cards. Each with three things I did, a result, a lesson.
- [ ] The six honesty words: priority, stop, tell, pass up, record, prevent.

Key words only. Never full sentences.

### Card 5: If my mind goes blank

1. Stop. Breathe out.
2. Look at the question, if it is still on screen.
3. Go back to the last thing I said. "So, to step back..."
4. Name the next part out loud. "On why I'd be good at it..."
5. If nothing comes, give one closing sentence and click "Done Answering".

Do not say sorry. Do not say "I'm nervous". A short complete answer beats a long unsure one.
