# How to answer any interview question

Every interview question is built the same way, however long it is.

Once you can see how it is built, you can answer one you have never seen before.

This guide shows you what to look for and how to build the answer. Then it works through twelve different questions so you can watch it happen.

- **Look for three things.** The asks. The kind of answer each one wants. The narrowing words.
- **Build the answer the same way every time.** Their words and your answer. One chunk for each ask, in order. Then stop.
- **Do not announce your plan.** Label each part as you reach it.
- **Prepare small pieces, not whole answers.** The question tells you which pieces to use.

One limit to know. No firm publishes its marking sheet. This method is built from how interview questions are written and marked elsewhere. It has not been tested on firms' own marking.

## Part 1: The idea

### A question is a form with boxes

Picture the person marking you. They have a form. The form has boxes.

Each thing the question asks for is one box.

Your job is to put something in every box, in the order the boxes are listed.

A plain answer that fills all three boxes beats a beautiful answer that fills one.

### Why a prepared answer fails

A prepared answer was built for a form you imagined at home.

The real form has different boxes. So your prepared answer fills the wrong ones, however good it sounds.

> **Remember.** You are not trying to give your best answer. You are trying to fill their boxes.

## Part 2: What to look for

Look for the same three things in every question. Always in this order.

### First: the asks

An ask is one thing the question wants from you.

Two kinds of word tell you there is an ask:

- **Instruction words:** explain, describe, tell us, give, share, outline.
- **Question words:** why, what, how, which, when, who.

Each one is a box.

Three more clues:

- **The word "and".** It often hides a second ask. "How did you engage and ensure deadlines were met?" is two asks.
- **Question marks.** Two question marks usually means at least two asks.
- **"In your response, please…"** Whatever follows is a list of boxes. Fill every one.

Some questions open with a sentence about the firm. That is background. It is not an ask. Skip it.

Count the asks on your fingers as you read.

> **Try it now.** Count the asks in each of these. The answers are underneath.
>
> A. "Why do you want this role?"
>
> B. "Tell us about yourself and why you applied."
>
> C. "Describe a time you failed. What did you do, and what would you do differently now?"

A has one ask. B has two. C has three: the time you failed, what you did, and what you would do differently.

### Second: the kind of answer

Each ask wants one kind of answer. There are seven kinds.

| The question says | It wants | It sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| "Tell me about a time", "Describe a project", "Give an example" | One real thing that happened | "Last March I…" |
| "Why…", "What attracts you", "What motivates you" | A reason about you, with proof | "Because… I know that from…" |
| "What would you do if", "How would you handle" | Your steps, in order | "First I would… then…" |
| "How do you…", "What is your approach" | The ways you usually do it | "I do three things…" |
| "Which…", "What… most", "What is your biggest" | One choice, and why | "The one I'd pick is…" |
| "What do you know about", "What does… do", "What is the difference" | An explanation in your own words | "Put simply…" |
| "Tell us about yourself" | A short introduction that leads here | "I'm… I've… and that's why…" |

Learn this table. On the day there is no time to work it out.

### The three mix-ups to avoid

**"How do you" is not "tell me about a time".** "How do you handle pressure?" wants your usual ways. One long story answers a different question.

**"What would you do" is not about the past.** It wants steps you would take. A story from last year does not fill the box.

**"Why this firm" is not "tell me about the firm".** Facts about the firm are not a reason. The reason has to be about you.

### Third: the narrowing words

A narrowing word cuts down which answers count.

Here are the common ones:

- **most**, **biggest**, **one**: pick a single thing.
- **first**: they want the order, and what comes first matters.
- **outside your studies**, **not on your CV**: your best work example does not count.
- **without being the leader**: a time you led does not count.
- **recently**: something from years ago does not count.
- **in particular**, **specifically**: a general answer does not count.
- **for an extended period**: a two-day task does not count.

They often sit at the end of the question. That is the part people stop reading.

> **Remember.** If your example breaks the narrowing word, it does not count, however good it is.

## Part 3: How to build the answer

There are four moves. They are the same for every question.

### Move 1: Their words, your answer

Your first sentence takes a few words from the question and adds your answer.

> **Say it like this.** Question: "Why do you want to work in asset management?" You: "I want to work in asset management because…"

> **Say it like this.** Question: "Tell me about a time you worked in a team without being the leader." You: "A time I helped a team without being the leader was…"

This does three things. It proves you read the question. It stops a prepared script from starting. It tells the marker which box you are filling.

### Move 2: One chunk for each ask, in order

A chunk is a few sentences that answer one ask.

Give each ask its own chunk. Keep the order the question used.

Open each chunk with a few of the question's words.

### Move 3: Point, then proof

Inside each chunk, say the point first. Then give proof from your own life.

Here is what goes in a chunk for each kind of answer.

| Kind | What goes in the chunk |
|---|---|
| One real thing that happened | What was going on, in a sentence or two. What you did, which gets most of the time. How it ended, in one sentence |
| A reason | The reason. Then something from your life that shows it is true |
| Your steps | Step one, step two, step three. Who you would tell. Why |
| The ways you usually do it | Two or three ways. A tiny example for each |
| One choice | The choice. Why. One piece of proof |
| An explanation | Plain words first. Then one detail that shows you understand |

### Move 4: Stop

When the last ask is answered, stop talking.

Decide your last sentence before you start. Say it. Then press the button.

### Do not announce your plan

You asked whether to start with "First I'll explain, then I'll describe".

Do not. It uses up time and tells the marker nothing.

> **Weak answer.** "First I'm going to explain why I applied, then I'll describe the area I'm interested in, and finally I'll talk about my skills."

> **Strong answer.** "I applied because…" Then later: "The area I'm most interested in is…" Then later: "The skills I'd bring are…"

What changed:

- The weak one spends eight seconds and has said nothing yet.
- The strong one starts answering in the first second.
- The labels still tell the marker where you are. They just arrive when you get there.

### How to share out the time

Split your time roughly evenly between the asks.

Give a little more to any ask that needs proof.

No ask should get less than a fifth of your time.

## Part 4: Twelve questions, worked

For each one you will see the asks, the kind, the narrowing words and the opening lines.

Four of them have a full answer from Sam. Sam is made up. Sam is a final-year student with a weekend job in a supermarket. Sam is the treasurer of the university hiking society and spent one summer in the office of a small lettings agency. Nothing about Sam is about you.

### 1. "Why do you want to work here?"

- **Asks:** one. Why here.
- **Kind:** a reason.
- **Narrowing words:** "here". This firm, not any firm.

> **Say it like this.** "I want to work here because…"

Then the reason, then proof from your life, then one thing that makes it this firm and not another.

### 2. "Why this firm, and why this role?"

- **Asks:** two. Why the firm. Why the role.
- **Kind:** two reasons.
- **Narrowing words:** none.

> **Strong answer.** "I want this firm because of a conversation. I met one of your analysts, Priya, at a careers fair. She said new joiners sit with clients from the first month. That is what I want, and most firms I asked said it takes a year.
>
> I want this role because it is the work I already enjoy. In my summer at a lettings agency I answered tenants' questions every day. The part I liked was finding the answer for them quickly. This role is that, with harder questions."

What makes it work:

- Two asks, two chunks, each opened with the question's words.
- Each reason is about Sam, and each has proof from Sam's life.
- The firm gets one fact. It is not a list of facts.

### 3. "Tell us about yourself and why you are a good candidate."

- **Asks:** two. Who you are. Why you are a good candidate.
- **Kind:** an introduction, then a reason.
- **Narrowing words:** none.

> **Say it like this.** "I'm a final-year business student, and two things I've done led me here…" Then later: "I think I'm a good candidate because…"

The trap is to tell your whole CV and never reach the second ask.

### 4. "Tell me about a time you worked in a team without being the leader. What did you learn?"

- **Asks:** two. The time. What you learned.
- **Kind:** one real thing that happened.
- **Narrowing words:** "without being the leader".

> **Strong answer.** "A time I helped a team without being the leader was our society's charity quiz last spring. Our events officer was running it. Two weeks before, we had sold only twenty tickets and needed eighty.
>
> I wasn't in charge, so I asked her what would help most. She said ticket sales. I made a list of every other society on campus. I messaged each one and offered them a team table. I kept a running count and sent it to her each evening.
>
> We sold ninety-four tickets.
>
> What I learned is that when you are not leading, the most useful thing is to take one problem off the leader completely."

What makes it work:

- The first sentence uses the narrowing words.
- Most of the time goes on what Sam did.
- "What I learned" opens the second chunk, so the marker cannot miss it.

### 5. "How do you handle pressure?"

- **Asks:** one. How you handle it.
- **Kind:** the ways you usually do it. Not one story.
- **Narrowing words:** none.

> **Strong answer.** "I handle pressure in three ways.
>
> First, I write down everything that is due and pick the one thing that matters most. On busy Saturdays at the supermarket that is always the queue, so I go to the tills before anything else.
>
> Second, I tell people early if something will be late. As treasurer I once told the committee a week ahead that the accounts needed two more days.
>
> Third, I take five minutes away from it. I think more clearly after."

What makes it work:

- It gives ways, in the present tense, because that is what "how do you" asks for.
- Each way has a tiny example, so it is not just a claim.

### 6. "What would you do if you realised you had sent a client the wrong information?"

- **Asks:** one. What you would do.
- **Kind:** your steps, in order.
- **Narrowing words:** none.

> **Strong answer.** "If I realised I had sent a client the wrong information, I would act straight away.
>
> First, I would tell my manager what I sent, to whom, and when. I would not try to fix it quietly on my own.
>
> Second, with my manager's agreement, I would send the client the correct information and say plainly that the first version was wrong.
>
> Third, I would check whether the client had already acted on it.
>
> Then I would work out how it happened, so it does not happen twice.
>
> I would do it in that order because a mistake found early is small. A mistake that is hidden gets bigger."

What makes it work:

- It is steps in the future tense. It is not a story.
- It says who Sam would tell. That is what this kind of question is checking.

### 7. "What is your biggest weakness, and what are you doing about it?"

- **Asks:** two. The weakness. What you are doing about it.
- **Kind:** one choice, then the ways you are working on it.
- **Narrowing words:** "biggest". Pick one, and make it real.

> **Say it like this.** "My biggest weakness is…" Then later: "What I'm doing about it is…"

The trap is a strength dressed up as a weakness, such as "I work too hard".

### 8. "What do you know about what we do, and why does it interest you?"

- **Asks:** two. What you know. Why it interests you.
- **Kind:** an explanation, then a reason.
- **Narrowing words:** none.

> **Say it like this.** "Put simply, you…" Then later: "It interests me because…"

The trap is to spend the whole answer on what the firm does. That is half the question.

### 9. "Tell us about something in the news recently that caught your attention, and why it matters to our business."

- **Asks:** two. The news. Why it matters to them.
- **Kind:** an explanation, then a reason.
- **Narrowing words:** "recently" and "to our business".

> **Say it like this.** "Something that caught my attention recently was…" Then later: "It matters to your business because…"

The second ask is where the marks are. A summary of the news fills only the first box.

### 10. "What is something about you that is not on your CV?"

- **Asks:** one.
- **Kind:** one choice.
- **Narrowing words:** "not on your CV".

> **Say it like this.** "Something that isn't on my CV is…"

Your best work story does not count here. They want to meet the person.

### 11. "Describe a time you had to persuade someone. In your response, please tell us the situation, what you did and the outcome."

- **Asks:** three, and they are printed for you. The situation. What you did. The outcome.
- **Kind:** one real thing that happened.
- **Narrowing words:** none.

> **Say it like this.** "A time I had to persuade someone was…" Then: "What I did was…" Then: "The outcome was…"

When a firm prints the list, use its words as your labels.

### 12. "What motivates you, and how has that shown up in something you have done outside your studies?"

- **Asks:** two. What motivates you. How it has shown up.
- **Kind:** a reason, then one real thing that happened.
- **Narrowing words:** "outside your studies".

> **Say it like this.** "What motivates me is…" Then later: "It showed up outside my studies when…"

A degree project does not count for the second ask.

## Part 5: What to prepare instead of answers

Prepare pieces. A piece is small enough to fit any question.

### Six real things that happened

For each one, know four parts:

1. What was going on, in two sentences.
2. Three things you did.
3. How it ended.
4. What you learned.

Your own experience gives you plenty to choose from. These are possibilities. Only you know which ones have a good story inside them.

- The portal you built at the asset manager.
- The 50 market openings you moved forward.
- Running the client inbox.
- The sales target model at the software company.
- The 60-day plan for new clients.
- Your stock pitch in the student investment fund.
- A debate you lost and what you changed.
- Something from being Head Boy.

Try to include one where something went wrong, and one from outside work and study.

### Three small blocks for each firm

1. Why this firm.
2. Why this role.
3. Why this kind of work.

Each block is one reason and one piece of proof from your life. Each takes about fifteen seconds to say.

Keep them separate. Never join them into one long answer at home. The question will tell you which ones to use and in what order.

### A few single items

- Two skills, each with proof.
- One real weakness, and what you are doing about it.
- One thing that is not on your CV.
- One recent news story, and your view on it.

> **Remember.** You are not learning words. You are learning what happened and what you think. The words come out fresh each time.

## Part 6: The 30 seconds

You cannot write in 30 seconds. Do not try.

1. Read to the last word. Count the asks on your fingers as you go.
2. Name the kind of answer for each ask.
3. Find the narrowing word.
4. Pick the piece that fits.
5. Say your first sentence in your head.
6. Breathe out. Look at the camera.

If the question is long, read it once, slowly. You will not have time to read it twice.

### If a prepared answer jumps into your head

That will happen. Ignore it and keep reading to the last word.

Then ask one thing: does it fill these boxes? Often only one small piece of it does. Use that piece.

### If your mind goes blank

Start with the question's own words. "A time I had to persuade someone was…"

Saying those words out loud usually brings something to mind.

Then pick the nearest true thing, even if it is small.

### If nothing you prepared fits

A small true example that fits the question beats a big one that does not.

If nothing fits at all, give the closest real thing. Say honestly how it differs. Then say what you would do in the case they asked about.

## Part 7: Check yourself

After every practice answer, ask four things.

- [ ] Did every ask get its own chunk?
- [ ] Did I give the kind of answer it wanted?
- [ ] Did I keep to the narrowing word?
- [ ] Could a friend guess the question from my answer alone?

## Where this comes from

The method comes from the second guide, "Answer the question they actually asked". That guide has the sources.

In short, it rests on three things:

- How interview questions are written. Each one is built to collect proof of one thing.
- Published marking schemes from the UK Civil Service and US public bodies. They give top marks only when every part is answered.
- Research on why prepared people answer the wrong question. A question that looks familiar pulls out the prepared answer.

Some of this is my best judgement and not tested fact. That includes the table of seven kinds, the time shares, and the advice for when nothing fits.

Sam's answers are made up to show the shape. Do not copy them.
