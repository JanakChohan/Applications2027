# Competency questions: model answers and the "why" layer

You said the part you are missing is the extra layer of why. You do the right things at work. You cannot put the thinking into words when the question comes.

You also said that when you try, you repeat yourself to fill the time.

This guide fixes both. It gives you ten model answers, each taken apart so you can see where the "why" sits.

- **A real "why" is something you noticed.** It is not a restatement of what you did.
- **One sentence carries it:** "I noticed this, so I did that, and not the other, because…"
- **Pressure and deadlines are not the same question.** One story can answer both, but you tell a different moment.
- **When you have said it, stop.** A shorter answer beats a repeated one.
- **Your house story is a good one.** Part 5 builds it out for five different questions.

One limit to know. No firm publishes how it marks. The model answers show what published marking schemes reward. They are not a firm's own answers.

## Part 1: The problem, and why it is common

### Is this a common problem?

Yes. It may be the most common one.

In a study of real job interviews, people gave one real event only about a quarter of the time. Mostly they said what they usually do.

A recruitment manager at HMRC, whose team marks thousands of interviews a month, says top marks go to people who explain their actions, their thought process, and why they made the decision. He says very few do.

So you are not unusual. You have found the thing most people never find.

### What a "why" really is

Most people think the "why" is the goal. It is not.

> **Weak answer.** "I set mini deadlines to make sure I met the overall deadline."

That "why" just says the action again. Of course you set deadlines to meet the deadline. It tells the marker nothing.

A real "why" points to something you noticed in that one situation.

> **Strong answer.** "I set my own deadline two weeks early, because I knew from the last trip that half the group pays late."

The reason is a fact about those people. Nobody else could have said it.

### The test

Ask: could this reason be true of any project, anywhere?

If yes, it is a circular why. It goes round in a circle back to the action.

If it is only true of this one situation, it is a real why.

### Why you repeat yourself

You told me you repeat things to fill time while you think.

That happens because you are trying to build the reason while you are talking. You never put it into words before, so it is not there to find.

The answer is not to think faster. It is to dig the reasons out at home, once, out loud. Part 5 shows you how.

And on the day: when you have said the thing, stop. In one experiment, people who kept talking after they had finished got lower marks than people who stopped.

> **Remember.** Silence costs nothing. Repeating yourself costs marks.

## Part 2: The decision sentence

Every story has a few moments where you chose one thing over another. Those moments are where the thinking lives.

One sentence shape carries all of it.

> **Say it like this.** "I noticed [something], so I [did this], and not [the other thing], because [the reason]."

It has four parts.

1. **What you noticed.** A fact about the situation or the people.
2. **What you did.**
3. **What you chose against.** This proves it was a decision.
4. **The reason.** Tied to what you noticed.

You do not need all four every time. But every story needs at least two of these sentences.

### An example

> **Say it like this.** "I noticed the reminders in the group chat were being ignored, so I messaged people one by one, and not as a group, because a message with your own name on it is harder to ignore."

> **Try it now.** Think of one thing you did last week. It can be small. Say it as a decision sentence. What did you notice? What did you do? What did you choose against? Why?

### How many do you need?

For a one-minute answer: two decision sentences.

For a two-minute answer: three.

That is the whole middle of your answer. You do not need more words. You need these sentences.

## Part 3: Are pressure and deadlines the same question?

No. They overlap, but they ask for different moments.

**A deadline question** asks how you organised the time. The moment to tell is early: how you planned.

**A pressure question** asks how you kept working well when it got hard. The moment to tell is the worst point: what you did right then.

One story can answer both. You pick a different moment from it.

### The ten families

Almost every competency question belongs to one of these ten. For each, this is the moment to pick from your story.

| The question is about | What it is really asking | The moment to tell |
|---|---|---|
| Teamwork | Do you make a team better? | When you saw what the team needed and did it |
| Leading | Can you take people with you? | When you made a call and explained it |
| Pressure | Do you stay useful when it is hard? | The worst point, and what you did right then |
| Deadlines and organising | Do you plan, or just hope? | When you set the plan, and when it slipped |
| Persuading | Can you change a mind? | When you worked out what the other person cared about |
| Disagreement | Can you disagree and stay useful? | When you raised it, and how |
| A mistake or setback | Do you own it and learn? | When you found it, and who you told |
| Initiative | Do you act without being asked? | When you spotted it and decided to act |
| Learning fast | Can you get up to speed? | How you chose to learn it |
| A difficult person | Do you stay calm and fair? | What you said first |

There is one more: doing the right thing when it is hard. It is answer ten below.

## Part 4: Ten model answers

Sam is made up. Sam works weekends at a supermarket, is treasurer of the hiking society, and spent a summer in the office of a small lettings agency. Nothing about Sam is about you.

Each answer takes about a minute to say. After each one, the decision sentences are pulled out so you can see them.

Do not copy these. Copy the shape.

### 1. Teamwork

**"Tell me about a time you worked well in a team."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I worked well in a team was the week before Christmas at the supermarket. A delivery came half a day late, and four of us had to fill the shelves before opening.
>
> I noticed our two newest people were both on the same aisle and going slowly. They were not being lazy. They did not know where things went. So I asked the supervisor if we could pair each of them with someone experienced, and not leave them together.
>
> I took the freezer section on my own, because I knew it best and it needed no explaining.
>
> The shelves were full twenty minutes before the doors opened."

**The why layer:**

- Noticed: the new people were slow because they did not know the layout.
- Did: paired them up. Chose against: leaving them together.
- Did: took the freezers alone. Because: it was the part Sam knew best.

### 2. Leading

**"Describe a time you led a group."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I led a group was a day hike for fourteen members. Our president was ill, so I stepped in.
>
> At the halfway point the cloud was coming down. I noticed two people were in trainers, not boots. So I decided to turn back, and not go on to the summit, because I would rather disappoint fourteen people than carry one of them down.
>
> I told the group the reason plainly. I did not just say we were turning round. Then I asked our two fastest walkers to go at the back, because the slow ones needed company more than the fast ones needed a lead.
>
> Everyone was down by three o'clock. Twelve of the fourteen booked the next hike."

**The why layer:**

- Noticed: the cloud, and two people in trainers.
- Did: turned back. Chose against: the summit. Because: safety over disappointment.
- Did: gave the reason. Did: put the fast walkers at the back, because the slow ones needed company.

### 3. Pressure

**"Tell me about a time you were under pressure."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I was under pressure was two weeks before our society's Easter trip. The hostel needed its deposit, and eighteen of the forty people had not paid me.
>
> My first thought was to send another reminder to the group. But I noticed the last three reminders had been ignored. So I stopped, and messaged the eighteen one by one.
>
> Three of them told me the full amount was the problem. So I let people pay in two halves, and not all at once, because a smaller sum that day was better than a promise for next week.
>
> I also told the president where we stood that evening, because if it failed she needed to hear it from me first.
>
> Thirty-eight had paid within twelve days. The deposit went in two days early."

**The why layer:**

- Noticed: group reminders were being ignored. Did: one by one.
- Noticed: the amount was the barrier. Did: two halves, and not all at once.
- Did: told the president that evening. Because: bad news should come from the person responsible.

Notice what is different from the deadline version. This one starts at the worst point and stays there.

### 4. Persuading

**"Tell me about a time you persuaded someone."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I persuaded someone was at the lettings agency. I wanted my manager to send rent reminders by text, not by letter.
>
> I noticed most of the late payers were under thirty. I guessed they did not open post.
>
> My manager liked the letters, because they left a record. So I did not ask her to change everything. I asked to try texts on twenty tenants for one month, because a small trial is easier to say yes to than a new system.
>
> I also kept sending the letters, so she lost nothing.
>
> In that group, late payments went from seven to two. She moved everyone to texts the month after."

**The why layer:**

- Noticed: late payers were young. Noticed: the manager cared about having a record.
- Did: asked for a small trial. Chose against: asking for the whole change.
- Did: kept the letters going. Because: it took away her reason to say no.

### 5. Disagreement

**"Describe a time you disagreed with someone."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I disagreed with someone was on the society committee. We had six hundred pounds left over. The president wanted to spend it on hoodies. I wanted to use it to make the next trip cheaper.
>
> I did not argue in the meeting, because she would have had to defend her idea in front of everyone. I asked for ten minutes with her afterwards.
>
> I knew she cared most about member numbers. So I brought one fact. Nine people had dropped out of the last trip because of the cost.
>
> I suggested we split it, and not choose one or the other, because she had a fair point too. People like the hoodies.
>
> We spent four hundred on the trip and two hundred on hoodies."

**The why layer:**

- Chose against: arguing in the meeting. Because: nobody backs down in front of a room.
- Noticed: she cared about member numbers. Did: brought a fact about members.
- Did: offered a split. Because: her idea had merit.

### 6. A mistake

**"Tell me about a mistake you made."**

> **Strong answer.** "A mistake I made was paying the same hostel invoice twice as treasurer. It was three hundred and twenty pounds.
>
> I found it a week later, when I checked the bank statement against my list.
>
> I told the president the same day, before I had fixed it, because it was the society's money and not mine to keep quiet about.
>
> Then I emailed the hostel with both payment references. I did not phone, because I wanted it in writing. The refund came in five days.
>
> Now I tick every invoice off a list before I pay it. I have not paid one twice since."

**The why layer:**

- Did: told the president before fixing it. Because: it was not Sam's money.
- Did: emailed. Chose against: phoning. Because: a written record.
- Changed: a tick list. That is the "what I learned" part, shown as an action.

### 7. Initiative

**"Tell me about a time you did something without being asked."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I did something without being asked was at the lettings agency. Keys kept going missing.
>
> I noticed it because I lost forty minutes looking for the same key twice in one week. Nobody owned the problem, so nobody had fixed it.
>
> I built a shared list of every key and who had it. Before I shared it, I showed the office manager, because it changed how four other people worked, and I did not want to spring it on them.
>
> I did it in my lunch breaks, and not in work time, because it was my idea and not my job.
>
> All four staff were using it within a week."

**The why layer:**

- Noticed: forty minutes lost, twice, and nobody owned it.
- Did: showed the manager first. Because: it changed other people's work.
- Did: lunch breaks. Chose against: work time.

### 8. Learning fast

**"Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I had to learn something quickly was my first week at the lettings agency. I had to use the booking system, and nobody had time to train me.
>
> There was a manual. But I know I learn better from seeing a real case. So I asked a colleague if I could watch her do three real bookings, and not read the manual first.
>
> I asked for twenty minutes, because a small, exact request is easy to say yes to.
>
> As I watched, I wrote my own one-page list of steps in my own words.
>
> I did my first booking alone on day three. Two later starters used my one-page list."

**The why layer:**

- Noticed: how Sam learns best. Did: watched real cases. Chose against: the manual.
- Did: asked for twenty minutes. Because: an exact request gets a yes.

### 9. A difficult person

**"Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult person."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I dealt with a difficult person was a customer at the supermarket who wanted a refund with no receipt. He was angry before he reached the till.
>
> I let him finish before I spoke, because interrupting an angry person makes them louder.
>
> Then I told him what I could do, and not what I could not. I could offer an exchange or a gift card.
>
> I did not call my supervisor straight away, because the rules let me do an exchange myself, and waiting would have made him angrier.
>
> He took the exchange. He was still annoyed, but he thanked me."

**The why layer:**

- Did: let him finish. Because: interrupting raises the temperature.
- Did: led with what was possible. Chose against: leading with the rule.
- Chose against: calling the supervisor. Because: Sam was allowed to fix it.

Notice the ending. He was still annoyed. A real ending is more believable than a perfect one.

### 10. Doing the right thing

**"Tell me about a time you did the right thing when it was hard."**

> **Strong answer.** "A time I did the right thing when it was hard was at the supermarket. A colleague I got on well with was giving his staff discount to friends.
>
> I spoke to him first, and not to the supervisor, because I wanted to give him the chance to stop. I told him I had seen it, and that I would have to say something if it carried on.
>
> It happened again the next weekend. So I told the supervisor what I had seen and when.
>
> It was hard because he stopped talking to me for a while. But the discount is the shop's money, and saying nothing would have made me part of it."

**The why layer:**

- Did: spoke to him first. Because: a fair chance.
- Did: told the supervisor the second time. Because: Sam had said so, and it was the shop's money.
- The cost is stated. That is what makes it "hard".

A caution for this one. In a regulated firm, serious things go to your manager or to Compliance straight away. You do not give a warning first. If the question is about a client or about breaking a rule at work, say you would report it at once.

### Your house story

You said you like this one. It is a good choice. It has a deadline, other people, money and a real risk of failing.

I do not know what happened. So dig it out with these questions. Say the answers out loud and record them on your phone.

- How many of you were there?
- When did you start looking? When did a contract have to be signed?
- How many houses did you see?
- What went wrong? Did a house fall through? Did someone pull out?
- What did the group disagree about? Rent? Distance? Rooms?
- What did you do that the others did not?
- For each thing you did: what had you noticed? What did you choose against?
- How did it end? Which house? How close to the deadline?

### The same house, five questions

Square brackets are things only you know.

**Deadlines.** Tell the planning.

> **Say it like this.** "The project was finding a house for [number] of us for third year. Contracts had to be signed by [date]. I noticed [what you saw about how fast houses were going], so I [what you set up], and not [what you chose against], because [your reason]."

**Leading.** Tell a call you made.

> **Say it like this.** "A time I led a group was as lead tenant. The moment that mattered was [the decision]. I decided [what], and I told the others [how you explained it], because [why]."

**Persuading.** Tell who you had to convince.

> **Say it like this.** "A time I persuaded someone was [a housemate, or the landlord]. They wanted [what]. I knew they cared most about [what], so I [what you offered or showed]."

**Disagreement.** Tell the argument.

> **Say it like this.** "A time I disagreed with someone was over [rent, distance, or rooms]. I did not [what you chose against]. I [what you did], because [why]."

**Pressure.** Tell the worst day.

> **Say it like this.** "A time I was under pressure was when [what went wrong], with [how many days] left before we had to sign. My first thought was [the obvious thing]. But I noticed [what], so I [what you did]."

One story. Five answers. A different moment each time.

A warning. Do not use the house for every question in one interview. Have a second story ready for each family.

### Changing how you think about it

Three things to hold on to.

**You are not being asked to impress.** You are being asked what happened and what you were thinking.

**Small and real beats big and vague.** A supermarket till with two real decisions beats a big project with none.

**The thinking is already yours.** You made those choices at the time. You only have to put them into words once, at home, and they will be there.
