# Two Goldman questions, worked through

You asked how to think when a question packs several things together.

This guide takes your two real Goldman questions. It walks through the thinking one step at a time. Then it shows the words.

- **A long question is a list.** Find the verbs. Each verb is one thing to answer.
- **Answer each thing in the order asked.** Say out loud which one you are on.
- **A project question does want a story.** But the question tells you which part of the story earns the marks.
- **You never say "STAR".** You tell what happened, in order, in your own words.

One limit to know. No firm publishes its marking sheet. What follows is the best reading of what each question asks for. It is not Goldman's own scheme.

## Part 1: The three-in-one question

### The question

This is the question as you remembered it.

> "Goldman Sachs Asset Management is the primary investing area within Goldman Sachs, delivering investment and advisory services across public and private markets to the world's leading organisations, financial advisors and individuals. Explain why you applied to Goldman Sachs Asset Management, describe which area of the business you are most interested in, and tell us what skills you possess that will make you most successful in the role."

It is about 65 words. Reading it once takes about 16 seconds. That is half of a 30-second window, so you get one careful read.

### Step 1: Split the background from the question

The first sentence is not a question. It is Goldman describing its own business.

Do not answer it. Do not repeat it back.

Keep two ideas from it, because they tell you how Goldman sees itself:

- It invests in **public and private markets**.
- It serves **three kinds of client**: large organisations, financial advisors and individuals.

The real question starts at the word "Explain".

### Step 2: Find the verbs and count them

Look for the words that tell you to do something.

1. **Explain** why you applied.
2. **Describe** which area you are most interested in.
3. **Tell us** what skills you have.

Three verbs. Three things to answer. Put up one finger for each as you read.

> **Remember.** Count the verbs, not the sentences. One sentence here holds three separate questions.

### Step 3: Work out what each one wants

| Part | What it wants | The word that limits it |
|---|---|---|
| Why you applied | A reason about you, with proof from your life | "Goldman Sachs Asset Management": this business, not the bank in general |
| Which area | One area, named, and why | "most": pick one |
| What skills | Two skills, each with proof, each tied to the job | "possess" and "in the role": show you already have them, and say why the role needs them |

None of the three asks for one long story. So this is not a story answer.

### Step 4: Know the trap

You have a "Why Goldman" answer ready. This question begins with "why you applied".

So the ready answer comes out. It runs for ninety seconds. The area and the skills never get said.

The marker has three boxes to fill. They can fill one.

I have not seen your recording, so I cannot be sure this is what happened. From what you have told me, it is the most likely thing.

### Step 5: Split the time

No part should get less than a fifth of your time.

| Part | If you have 2 minutes | If you have 90 seconds |
|---|---|---|
| Why you applied | 35 seconds | 25 seconds |
| Which area | 25 seconds | 20 seconds |
| What skills | 40 seconds | 30 seconds |

Skills get the most time because each one needs proof.

Stop about ten seconds before the clock does.

### Step 6: Say it

Start each part with a few of the question's own words. This tells the marker which box you are filling.

> **Say it like this.** "I applied to Goldman Sachs Asset Management because…"

> **Say it like this.** "The area I'm most interested in is…"

> **Say it like this.** "The skills that would make me most successful in the role are…"

Do not open with a summary of what you are about to say. Go straight into the first part.

### What a weak answer sounds like

> **Weak answer.** "Goldman Sachs is one of the world's leading firms, with a strong culture and a global reach. I have always been passionate about finance. Asset management is a growing industry and Goldman manages a huge amount of money across public and private markets. I admire the firm's values and I would love to learn from the best people…"

What is wrong with it:

- It answers only the first part. The area and the skills are missing.
- It is facts about Goldman. A reason has to be about you.
- Anyone could say it about any firm.

### Your version

This uses only things from your CV. Square brackets mark the few things only you know. Fill them in your own words.

> **Strong answer.** "I applied to Goldman Sachs Asset Management because of what I did last summer. I worked in client servicing at an asset manager and ran the inbox for US institutional clients. [One sentence: what you liked or noticed about that work.] I want to do that at a firm that covers public and private markets, for organisations, advisors and individuals. [One sentence: why Goldman in particular. A person you met, or one fact you can explain.]
>
> The area I'm most interested in is the client side, Client Solutions. [One sentence: what that team does that you want to do.]
>
> The two skills that would make me most successful in the role are seeing things through, and making a messy process simple. On the first: I moved 50 market openings forward by chasing documents from outside banks until each one was ready to trade. On the second: I built a portal that sends each client questionnaire to the right team. It replaced a 400-line spreadsheet. A client team needs both. Nothing gets dropped, and nothing is slower than it has to be."

What changed:

- Each part has its own short answer, in the order asked.
- Each part starts with the question's own words.
- The reason is about you. The facts about the firm are one sentence.
- Each skill has one thing you did as proof.

> **Try it now.** Say the three opening lines out loud, one after the other, with nothing in between. That is the skeleton. It takes about ten seconds. Do it three times.

## Part 2: The project question

### The question

> "Describe a project that required your attention for an extended period. How did you engage and ensure deadlines were met?"

It is 20 words. Reading it twice takes about ten seconds.

### Step 1: Find the verbs and count them

1. **Describe** a project.
2. How did you **engage**?
3. How did you **ensure deadlines were met**?

Three things again. The second sentence holds two of them.

### Step 2: Work out what kind of answer it wants

"Describe a project" asks for one real thing that happened. So yes, this one wants a story.

People call the story shape STAR. In plain words it is four things, in order:

1. What was going on.
2. What your job was.
3. What you did.
4. How it ended.

You never say "STAR". You never say "the situation was" or "my task was". You just tell it in that order, the way you would tell a friend.

### Step 3: See where the marks are

A normal story spends its time on the background and the ending.

This question does not ask about those. It asks two "how" questions. So the middle of your story splits in two:

- how you **stayed engaged**
- how you **made sure deadlines were met**

Give each one its own chunk and its own opening words.

### Step 4: Check the limiting words

**"For an extended period."** This means weeks or months. A task you finished in two days does not fit, however good it was.

**"Engage."** This word can be read two ways. It could mean how you kept yourself involved. It could mean how you worked with other people. I cannot tell you which Goldman means. The safe move is one sentence on each.

### Step 5: Guess the skill

My best guess is two things. Can you stick with long work? Are you organised and reliable?

So your proof should be things you did every week, not one heroic moment.

### Step 6: Split the time

| Chunk | If you have 2 minutes |
|---|---|
| The project and your job | 20 seconds |
| How you stayed engaged | 35 seconds |
| How you met the deadlines | 40 seconds |
| How it ended | 10 seconds |

### What a weak answer sounds like

> **Weak answer.** "I'm a very organised person and I always meet my deadlines. I use to-do lists and I plan my time carefully. In all my roles I've had to manage long projects, and I believe staying motivated is really important."

What is wrong with it:

- There is no project. It describes a habit, and the question asked for one real thing.
- "Extended period" is ignored.
- Nothing in it could be checked.

### A full example to hear the shape

Sam is made up. Sam is a final-year student and the treasurer of a student society. Nothing here is about you. It is here so you can hear a complete answer.

> **Strong answer.** "The project was moving my society's accounts onto a new system. It took the whole autumn term, about ten weeks, and I was the treasurer.
>
> To stay engaged for that long, I broke it into weekly pieces. Every Sunday I picked the one thing that had to be finished that week. I also showed the president my progress every fortnight. Having someone waiting for it kept me going.
>
> To make sure the deadlines were met, I worked backwards from the audit date. I set my own deadline two weeks before the real one. When the bank was slow to send statements, I chased them on the same day every week until they arrived.
>
> The accounts were ready nine days early, and the auditor passed them first time."

What makes it work:

- One real project, and it lasted ten weeks.
- "To stay engaged" and "to make sure the deadlines were met" each start a chunk. The marker cannot miss them.
- The proof is small weekly things Sam did. That is what long work looks like.
- It ends in one line and stops.

### Your version

Your 50 market openings could fit this question, if the work ran over several weeks. Your portal could fit too. Only you know which one took longer.

> **Say it like this.** "The project was getting 50 market openings ready to trade, at the asset manager where I worked last summer. It ran for [how many weeks]. My job was to chase documents from the outside banks until each one was ready.
>
> To stay engaged over that time, [what kept you on it every week. For example, a list you updated each morning, or a weekly count of how many were done].
>
> To make sure the deadlines were met, [what you did. For example, chasing each bank on a fixed day, or telling your manager early about the slow ones].
>
> By the end, [how many were ready, and what that allowed]."

Fill the brackets with what really happened. If you never tracked a number, say what you saw change.

## Part 3: The thinking, as one routine

Use the same eight steps on every question.

1. Read to the last word.
2. Skip any background sentence.
3. Find the verbs. Count them on your fingers.
4. For each one, ask what it wants: a reason, a choice, skills, or one real thing that happened.
5. Notice the limiting words. Here they were "most", "in the role" and "extended".
6. Start with the question's own words and your answer.
7. Give one chunk per part, in the order asked. Open each chunk with a few of the question's words.
8. Stop when the last part is answered.

> **Remember.** You are not choosing between a prepared answer and no preparation. You prepare small pieces. The question tells you which pieces to use and in what order.
