Part 1

What a hedge fund is, from zero

Start with what a fund is, then place hedge funds in the family of investors, then take the machine apart.

1.1 Start with a fund

A fund is a pot of money pooled from many investors and run by a professional manager. Investors put money in, the manager buys things with it (shares, bonds, currencies, buildings, loans), and investors own a slice of whatever the pot is worth. The manager charges a fee. That is all any fund is. What differs is who is allowed to invest, what the manager may buy, how the manager is paid, and how quickly investors can get their money back. Every type of investor on the "buy side" is a variation on those four dials.

Key term: buy side and sell side

The buy side is everyone who invests money: pension funds, asset managers, hedge funds, private equity. The sell side is the investment banks and brokers who sell them services: trading, research, financing, and introductions to companies [S176a]. Millennium is buy side. Goldman Sachs is sell side.

1.3 Hedge fund versus asset manager: the ten differences

People use "asset manager" to mean the big, traditional, long-only investment houses. The regulator's definition of a hedge fund shows the contrast: "Hedge funds pool investors' money and invest the money in an effort to make a positive return. Hedge funds typically have more flexible investment strategies than, for example, mutual funds. Many hedge funds seek to profit in all kinds of markets by using leverage (in other words, borrowing to increase investment exposure as well as risk), short-selling and other speculative investment practices that are not often used by mutual funds" [S151] Confirmed.

@@f2@@
Figure 2. Ten dials. Definitions from the SEC [S151] and Aurum [S175]; fee averages from HFR [S150d]; pay from a careers explainer [S140c]. Long-only figures are generic descriptions, not sourced numbers.
Say this when someone asks "what is a hedge fund?"

"A fund for professional investors that tries to make money whether markets go up or down. It can bet against things, borrow to make bets bigger, and charges a share of the profits. An ordinary fund just buys shares and hopes to beat the index."

1.4 Where the name comes from: the hedge

To hedge is to protect yourself against a loss. The first hedge fund, set up by Alfred Winslow Jones in 1949 [S175], bought shares it liked and simultaneously sold short shares it disliked, so that if the whole market fell, the losses on the longs were offset by gains on the shorts. What was left was the manager's skill at picking which companies would do better than which. Most hedge funds today still do some version of this.

@@f3@@
Figure 3. A worked hedge. Illustrative numbers; definitions of net and gross exposure and market neutrality from Aurum's primer [S175] and CFI [S176b].
Don't say this

"Hedge funds are risky because they gamble on the market." The classic hedge fund takes less market risk than an index fund. Its risks are different: leverage, crowding (everyone in the same trade), and being wrong about which company is better. Saying that precisely is the single fastest way to sound like you understand the industry.

1.5 How a hedge fund is built

@@f4@@
Figure 4. The machine. Roles from the SEC bulletin [S151], CFI on prime brokerage [S176c], Aurum [S175]; Millennium examples from its SEC filing [S70] and fund database [S114].
Key term: LP and GP

A hedge fund is usually a limited partnership. The investors are limited partners (LPs): they supply the money and can lose only what they put in. The manager is the general partner (GP): it runs the fund and takes the fees [S151]. When people say "Millennium", they usually mean the GP, the firm; the fund itself is a separate legal thing the firm manages.

1.7 Single-manager versus multi-manager

The biggest structural split in the industry today is not strategy but organisation. A single-manager fund has one house view: a founder or CIO decides, analysts feed ideas up. A multi-manager platform has no house view: it gives capital to dozens or hundreds of independent teams, each hedged and each cut if it loses more than a set amount, and its return is the sum of many small uncorrelated bets. Aurum's description: platforms run "anywhere from a handful to over a hundred independent 'pods', each with its own portfolio manager and analysts, running tightly hedged books under a firm-wide risk framework", with the result of "far higher leverage (gross exposure of typically 400 to 600%), higher turnover levels, and lower realised portfolio volatility" [S175] Confirmed.

@@f6@@
Figure 6. Single-manager versus multi-manager. Structural description from Aurum [S175]; corporate access implications inferred.
Key term: pass-through fees

Traditional funds charge "2 and 20": 2% of assets a year plus 20% of profits, and the manager pays its own costs out of the 2%. Multi-manager platforms instead bill investors for their actual costs, mostly the pay of the trading teams, with no cap, plus a performance fee. Bloomberg's analysis of regulatory filings found these pass-through expenses "typically range from 3% to 10% of net assets" [S156] Reported. Investors accept it when the net return after everything is good and steady. This is the fee debate in Part 2.3.

1.8 The words you need

AUM (assets under management): the total investor money a firm runs. The standard size measure. For private firms it is a press estimate that moves monthly.

Alpha and beta. Beta is return from simply being exposed to the market. Alpha is return from skill on top. Investors "are understandably reluctant to pay high fees for beta" [S175]. Index funds sell beta cheaply; hedge funds sell alpha dearly.

Sharpe ratio. Return above cash divided by how much the return bounces around. Return per unit of risk. Above 1 is good; multi-manager platforms target 2 or more and care about this far more than raw return.

Drawdown. The fall from a peak to a trough. Platforms enforce "drawdown triggers" on each team [S175]; press reports Millennium halves a PM's capital at a 5% loss and removes them at 7.5% [S104][S113] Reported, two outlets.

Volatility. How much returns swing. Multi-strategy funds run volatility about four times lower than world equities and a beta of about 0.24 [S172].

High-water mark. Performance fees are charged only on gains above the fund's previous peak, so investors never pay twice for recovering a loss [S176d].

Hurdle. A minimum return before performance fees apply. A "cash hurdle" means only returns above what cash pays are charged for. Texas Teachers' Joel Hinkhouse: "If you are a hedge fund and return 5.25%, you have not made us alpha. So, you should not take a carry for that" [S167a].

Lock-up, gate, redemption. Funds "typically limit opportunities to redeem" to monthly, quarterly or yearly and "often impose a 'lock-up' period of one year or more" [S151]. A gate caps how much can leave per period. Millennium's main share class allows 5% a quarter, so full exit takes five years [S111].

Netting risk. On a platform, investors pay winning teams' share of profit even in a year when losing teams drag the total down. Investors "assume the 'netting risk' of underperforming PMs" [S175].

De-grossing. Cutting both longs and shorts at once because risk limits bite. When many funds do it together, prices gap. See Part 2.6.

Part 2

What is happening to hedge funds right now

Nine forces, as of 4 September 2026, drawn from industry reports (HFR, Barclays, BNP Paribas, With Intelligence, Goldman Sachs as reported), regulator publications (Bank of England, Federal Reserve, IMF, FSB) and the trade press. The short answer to "is it fundraising, trust, or war?" is: fundraising is strong but narrow; trust is contested on fees; war and shocks tested the model twice this year and it mostly held.

2.2 Money: fundraising is strong, and narrow

If you only remember one thing: hedge funds are not short of money in 2026. HFR's industry capital series reads $5.15tn at the end of 2025, $5.22tn at the end of March 2026 and a record $5.6tn at the end of June, after a quarterly rise of about $409bn, "the largest increase in history", and "15 consecutive quarters" of growth [S150a][S150b][S150c] Confirmed. HFR's president: "The current environment is unequivocally the strongest for hedge fund capital growth since industry inception" [S150c]. Reuters reports that "every major hedge fund strategy brought in fresh capital during the first half, a first in five years" [S221]. Barclays' survey of 340-plus investors found net interest at its highest since 2018, and 43% expecting to be net allocators in the second half [S171][S139b] Reported.

@@f9@@
Figure 9. Industry capital and where new money goes. HFR [S150a][S150b][S150c]; With Intelligence for the alternative count [S162].

Who is giving. Family offices and private banks are "leading renewed enthusiasm" [S171]; named 2026 allocations include CalSTRS narrowing a $4bn underweight, Virginia's retirement system deploying $1.3bn, and North Carolina putting $500m with Balyasny [S162]. More than one in five allocation intentions in the first half targeted multi-strategy funds [S162]. What they want. Low correlation to equities, net returns above cash, and increasingly separately managed accounts for transparency: about a quarter of investors now use them [S171][S172].

Source uncertain

Inflow figures vary by an order of magnitude between providers because they count different fund universes. The direction (positive, concentrated in the largest firms) is consistent across HFR, With Intelligence and Barclays. The exact totals are not.

2.3 Trust: the fee fight

"Trust" in 2026 is mostly about money, not scandal. The issue is how much of what a fund earns reaches the investor. Multi-manager platforms pass their costs through, and those costs are large: Balyasny's main fund made 15.2% gross in 2023 and 2.8% net, after $768m of pass-through of which 87% was pay; ExodusPoint made 11% gross with 8.4% of pass-through; Citadel's three largest funds charged $12.5bn of pass-through between 2022 and September 2024, $11bn of it compensation [S156] Reported (Bloomberg analysis of SEC filings). BNP Paribas data, as reported, suggests investors kept about 41 cents of each gross dollar at multi-strategy funds in 2023, down from 54 cents in 2021, though a second report quotes the same series differently, so use a range [S157].

@@f10@@
Figure 10. What investors keep. BNP Paribas via Hedgeweek [S157]; Bloomberg via Hedgeweek [S156]; Texas Teachers coalition [S167a]; LCH Investments [S134]. All press estimates; no fund publishes these figures.

The defence is simple and, so far, winning: net returns after everything have been good. HFR's average fund made 12.5% in 2025, the best year since 2009 [S150a]; multi-strategy funds run a beta to equities of about 0.24 [S172]; and capacity at the best firms is rationed, so investors queue. Texas Tech's Clinton Huff: "Everyone brings up the fees, but all I care about are net returns" [S160a]. The counter-evidence is the mid-tier: at Eisler, "the bulk of the losses stemmed from pass-through expenses, including staff compensation and operational costs charged directly to investors, rather than trading performance" [S161] Reported. Transparency is the other trust lever: about 25% of investors now use separately managed accounts, which let them see positions, and half of funds offer them [S171].

Your opinion goes here

Is pass-through fair? One view: fair while net returns beat what the investor could get elsewhere for the same volatility, and only then. The other: investors bear netting risk and fund the talent war, so hurdles are the minimum. Pick one and say what would change your mind.

2.4 Talent: the industry's biggest cost

Because platforms pay each team on its own profit and pass the bill to investors, the price of portfolio managers has become the central economic fact of the model. Reported examples: Point72 hired one PM with a package of "tens of millions" over five years after competition from Citadel, Millennium and Balyasny; Millennium hired a PM who had generated $250m in 2024 on a "multimillion-dollar guaranteed deal"; another, hired by Millennium from Citadel, was "intercepted by Balyasny before sit-out period ended" and "Never worked a day at Millennium" [S160a] Reported (WSJ via Hedgeweek). Steve Cohen: "We're seeing a lot of firms competing for great talent, no different than baseball" [S160a]. By 2026 "Top managers are now commanding guaranteed nine-figure payouts and multi-year contracts" [S160b], and the fights have gone to court: Schonfeld sued a PM who reversed a move for $11m; Millennium is seeking about $2.5m from a Hong Kong PM who abandoned an offer [S232][S233].

The response is to grow talent rather than buy it. Citadel's COO: "You can't only hire from the outside. You need to develop even more talent internally now" [S168]. Millennium took 190 interns in 2025 from about 50,000 applications and launched an investing internship for 2027 [S271][S168]. That is why structured internship programmes, including the one you applied to, exist.

2.5 Leverage: why central banks are watching

@@f11@@
Figure 11. Leverage numbers. IMF [S152]; Federal Reserve [S155a]; Bank of England [S154a][S154b][S154c][S240][S241]; FSB [S153]. All primary regulator documents.
Key term: repo and haircut

A repo (repurchase agreement) is selling a bond and agreeing to buy it back later at a slightly higher price: economically a secured loan with the bond as collateral. The haircut is the safety cushion the lender demands. The BoE and FSB both note hedge fund repo is often done at "zero or near-zero" haircuts [S153][S154a], which is why a proposal for minimum haircuts is a real constraint.

2.6 War and shocks: the two stress tests of 2026

@@f12@@
Figure 12. 2026 shock timeline. Sources as marked: Hedgeweek round-ups [S225][S223][S220], Bank of England [S240][S241], ECB [S247], Fed and BoE decisions [S244][S246], FinanceFeeds compilation [S238], data vendors [S249][S250]. The Situational Awareness figures are a secondary compilation with wide ranges.
Key term: crowding

Many funds holding the same positions. Harmless until a shock, when everyone tries to sell the same thing to the same buyers at once. The IMF says financial stress "has become more correlated with hedge fund losses since 2020" [S152]. Crowding is why platforms value information their rivals do not have, which is one reason in-house corporate access desks exist.

2.7 Performance: who won and who lost

Figure 13. Who won and lost. Every bar is a press estimate; dates as labelled. Sources [S127][S145l][S161][S222][S220][S249].

Three readings of this chart, all defensible. First: 2025 was excellent for almost everyone and the industry's best year since 2009 [S150a]. Second: in 2026 the big platforms are doing what they promise, positive returns with a fraction of the market's swings, in a year when the market itself is the winner; "Most of the funds remain behind the US equity market this year" [S220]. Third: dispersion is enormous. Point72 at +14.5% and Balyasny at +0.5% are the same business model. Which teams you have, and how much crowded AI exposure they carried into July, decided the year.

2.8 The shake-out: the middle disappears

The model is not dying; the number of firms that can afford it is shrinking. Multi-manager AUM hit a record $428bn across 57 platforms, up 16% in 2025 after a pause in 2023 and 2024 [S158] Reported (Goldman Sachs data via secondary sources). At the same time Eisler wound down after -14.3% [S161]; Atlantic Wolf ($3bn), Taproot, Eminence ($8bn) and AB Arya closed in 2026 [S162]; liquidations jumped to 129 in the first quarter [S150d]. Even a record launch struggled: Jain Global raised $5.3bn in 2024, made about $1.8bn of gross trading profit but only 3.7% net in 2025, and in April 2026 agreed to return about $6bn of outside money and run Millennium's capital alone [S143][S229] Reported. The top two took opposite paths to the same capacity problem: Citadel returned about $5bn of 2025 profits and shrank to about $67bn [S165]; Millennium is raising a record ~$20bn while already at about $92bn [S221]. LCH Investments' Rick Sopher on the top firms: they "have periodically either closed to new subscriptions or returned capital, recognising that carefully controlling asset growth is a good basis for generating significant gains" [S134].

2.9 Technology and AI

Barclays finds 55% of investors have put AI into their investment process and 75% into non-investment workflows; discretionary managers are "embedding quantitative tools to expand coverage capacity without proportional headcount" [S171]. Quant multi-strategy is the most wanted strategy among allocators [S139b]. Systematic funds have handled shocks better: up 4% in March 2025 while stock-pickers lost 2.4% [S163]; trend-followers "outpaced discretionary global macro" in 2026 for the first time since 2022 [S162]. Millennium announced an AI lab in June and an Anthropic co-development of a "digital risk analyst" in August, with its CIO stressing "keeping human judgment at the center" [S99][S117]. The friction: 69% of large managers say broker data licensing blocks AI use [S209].

2.10 Geography: London and the Gulf

London is Europe's hedge fund capital and Millennium's largest office outside the US, with a co-head of global equities relocated there and a senior equities recruiter hired from Schonfeld in May 2026 [S231]. Two pressures work against it. First, tax: "a $20m buyout is tax-free in Dubai" against roughly $9m of UK tax, per an eFinancialCareers analysis, and one manager says "This is why the UK government is talking about imposing an exit tax" [S236] Reported, anonymous. Brevan Howard's Abu Dhabi office grew from 5 people in 2023 to 150 by late 2025 [S145m]; funds kept Gulf offices open through March's missile attacks [S235]. Second, the shrinking listed market: 23 London IPOs in 2025 and 7 in the first half of 2026, recovering from a near-dead 2024 but small, while CRH, Smurfit Westrock and Flutter all moved to New York-only listings in 2026 [S254][S255]. Fewer listed companies means fewer things for equity hedge funds to trade in London and scarcer meetings with their management.

How to talk about it

Three lengths of explanation, ten lines you can use, five traps, and the opinions worth forming.

@@f14@@
Figure 14. Three lengths of explanation. Figures as sourced in Parts 1 and 2 [S150a][S150c][S158][S157][S220][S225][S238][S240].

3.1 Ten lines you can use

  1. "The classic hedge fund takes less market risk than an index fund. Its risks are leverage and crowding, not the market."
  2. "Absolute return versus relative return is the real difference. Everything else follows from it."
  3. "Hedge funds are lightly regulated on what they may buy and heavily regulated on conduct, because their investors are professionals."
  4. "The industry is at a record $5.6tn, and almost all the new money is going to firms over $5bn."
  5. "Multi-manager platforms are about 8% of hedge fund assets but a far bigger share of market activity, because they run four to six times leverage."
  6. "Pass-through means investors pay the fund's actual costs. That is fine while net returns are good, which is why the fee fight is really an argument about performance."
  7. "2026 had two stress tests: the Iran shock in March and the AI reversal in July. Both were leverage-and-crowding events, and the big platforms mostly absorbed them."
  8. "Citadel gave money back and Millennium is raising a record amount. Two answers to the same capacity problem."
  9. "Regulators are not proposing to cap hedge fund leverage. They are squeezing it through the banks that lend to hedge funds, which favours the biggest funds."
  10. "Eisler is the cautionary tale: a $3 to 4bn platform whose losses came from costs, not trades."

3.2 Five traps

  • Quoting a private firm's numbers as fact. Say "press estimates" or "its own website says". Millennium's AUM moved from about $70bn to $92bn in under two years across different sources.
  • "Hedge funds beat the market." They do not aim to. In 2026 almost none did. Explain what they do aim for.
  • "MiFID II banned bundled research." It required separate payment; the UK and EU have since partly reversed that. If you raise regulation, get the dates right (Part 5 of the main guide).
  • Treating all hedge funds as one thing. Bridgewater (+34% in 2025) and Brevan Howard (+0.8%) are both macro funds. Name the type.
  • Using "edge" carelessly. Near a listed company, "edge" sounds like inside information. Say "differentiated view" or "better analysis".

3.3 Opinions worth forming

Your opinion goes here

Has the multi-manager model peaked? For: outflows in 2024, investors keeping 41c, closures. Against: record AUM, +16% growth, top firms oversubscribed. A defensible middle: "peaked for the mid-tier, not the top." Is the industry good for markets? For: liquidity, price discovery, absorbing Treasury issuance (Cayman funds bought 37% of net issuance since 2022 [S155b]). Against: crowded, leveraged unwinds amplify shocks, as the BoE found [S154b]. Should London worry? For: tax, listings, Gulf competition. Against: Millennium and others are building out London equities now. Pick sides, and say what evidence would move you.

Part 4

Glossary

Every term used above, in plain English.

Absolute return
Aiming for a positive return regardless of what markets do. The hedge fund goal.
Accredited investor
Someone with enough income or assets to be allowed into a hedge fund. The law assumes they can bear losses.
Activist
A fund that buys a stake then pushes the company to change.
Allocator
An institution deciding where to invest large pools: pension fund, endowment, sovereign fund. Hedge funds' customers.
Alpha
Return from skill, above what the market gave you.
AUM
Assets under management. The investor money a firm runs.
Basis trade
Buying a government bond and selling its future, with borrowed money, to capture a tiny price gap. Huge in size, watched by the Fed.
Beta
Return from simply being exposed to the market.
Buy side
Firms that invest money. Sell side: firms that sell them services.
Cash hurdle
Performance fees charged only on returns above what cash pays.
CTA / trend follower
A computer-driven fund that follows price trends in futures.
Crowding
Many funds in the same trade. Dangerous in a shock.
De-grossing
Cutting longs and shorts together to reduce risk, usually under pressure.
Drawdown
The fall from a peak to a trough in value.
Event-driven
Betting on corporate events: mergers, bankruptcies, spin-offs.
Feeder and master fund
Investors put money into a feeder (often in the Cayman Islands), which invests in a master fund that holds the positions.
FSB
Financial Stability Board, the international body coordinating regulators on systemic risk.
Gate
A cap on how much money can leave a fund per period.
GP / LP
General partner (the manager) and limited partners (the investors).
Gross exposure
Longs plus shorts. How much money is at work. A measure of leverage.
Haircut
The safety cushion a lender demands against collateral in a repo.
Hedge
A position taken to offset a risk. Short B to hedge long A.
High-water mark
Performance fees only on gains above the previous peak.
Leverage
Borrowing, or derivatives, to hold more than your capital. Amplifies gains and losses.
Lock-up
A period during which investors cannot withdraw.
Long / short
Own it and profit if it rises / borrow and sell it and profit if it falls.
Long-only
A manager that only buys. Most traditional asset managers.
Macro
Betting on countries: rates, currencies, bonds, commodities.
Market neutral
Net exposure near zero, so the market's moves roughly cancel out.
Multi-manager / multi-strategy / pod shop / platform
One firm running many independent teams under central risk limits.
Net exposure
Longs minus shorts. How much you move with the market.
Netting risk
Investors pay winning teams even when losing teams drag the total down.
Pass-through
Investors pay the fund's actual operating costs plus a performance fee, instead of a fixed management fee.
Performance fee / carry
The manager's share of profits, often 20%.
PM
Portfolio manager. Runs a book, paid on its profit.
Pod
A PM plus analysts, running an independent book inside a platform.
Prime broker
A bank that lends a fund cash and securities, holds its assets and clears its trades. The source of leverage.
Quant / systematic
Trading decided by models and data rather than human judgement.
Relative return
Aiming to beat an index. The asset-manager goal.
Relative value / arbitrage
Exploiting small price gaps between related instruments, usually with leverage.
Repo
Selling a bond and agreeing to buy it back: a secured loan.
Sharpe ratio
Return above cash per unit of volatility. Return per unit of risk.
SMA
Separately managed account: an investor's own account run by the manager, giving full sight of positions.
2 and 20
2% of assets plus 20% of profits. The classic fee; averages are now lower.
Volatility
How much returns swing. Lower is calmer.
