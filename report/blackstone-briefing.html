One caveat, stated up front

You asked for "insider information." Everything in this report is drawn from public sources — SEC filings, quarterly earnings calls, press releases and mainstream financial press. That is not a limitation in practice: almost nobody at a networking evening expects you to know anything, so knowing what was said on the Q2 2026 earnings call already puts you well ahead. What it does mean is that you should never claim inside knowledge. The credible move is the opposite: cite the public record precisely and ask the person in front of you for the colour that isn't in it.

Blackstone in ninety seconds

Six paragraphs that will let you hold a conversation. Everything after this is depth on one of these six points.

1. What it is

Blackstone is the world's largest alternative asset manager. It does not primarily invest its own money. It raises money from pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurance companies and — increasingly — wealthy individuals, puts that money into things that are not publicly listed stocks and bonds (buildings, private companies, private loans, infrastructure), and charges fees for doing so. As at 30 June 2026 it manages $1.35 trillion.

2. How it is organised

Four reporting segments: Real Estate ($314bn), Private Equity ($454bn), Credit & Insurance ($469bn) and Multi-Asset Investing ($109bn). Real Estate is the historic crown jewel; Credit & Insurance is now the biggest and the growth engine; Private Equity is the highest-profile. A single central spine — one capital-raising organisation, one legal function, one technology group — serves all four. That "one firm" model is the thing Blackstone people are proudest of.

3. How it earns

Two ways. Management fees — roughly 1–1.5% a year on capital, charged whether markets go up or down. And performance fees ("carried interest") — typically 20% of the profit above a hurdle, which is lumpy and cyclical. The firm has spent fifteen years deliberately making the first stream bigger and more predictable relative to the second. Watch the acronym FRE (Fee-Related Earnings) — it is the number the market pays for.

4. What it is betting on right now

AI infrastructure. Blackstone is the largest data centre owner in the world and, by its own description, "one of the largest private capital providers in the AI ecosystem." Its data centre platform is worth roughly $185 billion, up from $130bn at the start of 2026. In 2026 alone it launched a data-centre REIT (BXDC), a neocloud venture with Google, an enterprise AI venture with Anthropic, and a $35 billion financing platform with Broadcom — the largest private credit investment ever made.

5. What is hurting

Private credit wobbled badly in early 2026. Two US bankruptcies (First Brands, Tricolor) triggered a panic; investors in semi-liquid credit funds rushed for the exit; Blackstone's flagship BCRED fund faced a record 7.9% redemption request and had to gate withdrawals. Senior executives put $150 million of their own money in to help meet redemptions. The alternative managers' shares fell roughly 30%. By Q2 the pressure was easing — but this is the live issue in the industry.

6. Where it is going

Two structural pushes. Insurance — managing money for life insurers is now a $290bn business and Blackstone runs the largest third-party platform in the sector. And individual investors — private wealth AUM is a record $324bn, and the next frontier is the US 401(k) retirement market, which a 2025 executive order has begun to open up. Both are attempts to find capital that behaves like permanent capital.

The single most useful sentence to have ready

"The thing that strikes me about Blackstone is that it is really two businesses in one: an investing business that has to be brilliant at buying assets, and a capital-raising business that has to be brilliant at keeping money. The second one is the part that has actually changed most in the last five years — perpetual capital going from a niche to $555 billion." — true, non-obvious, and it opens directly onto the client-facing side you care about.

Part 0

Foundations

The assumptions the rest of this report rests on. If you already know what a limited partner is, skim to §0.5 — but the vocabulary section is the one that stops you being caught out.

0.1What Blackstone actually is

Start with what it is not. Blackstone is not a bank: it does not take deposits and its business is not making loans off its own balance sheet. It is not a hedge fund in the classic sense: it does not mainly trade liquid securities. And it is not, despite the shorthand, "a private equity firm" any more — private equity is now barely a third of what it does.

Blackstone is an asset manager. Its product is a promise: give us your money for a defined period, and we will invest it in assets you cannot easily buy yourself, and generate a return you could not generate on your own. In exchange, we take a fee for managing it and a share of the profit if we do well.

The word "alternative" simply means not publicly traded stocks and bonds. Real estate. Companies bought outright. Loans made privately to businesses rather than through the bond market. Infrastructure — data centres, pipelines, energy. Hedge fund strategies. That is the whole of the definition, and it is worth saying plainly because the industry rarely does.

The mental model that unlocks everything

Blackstone is best understood as a machine for converting other people's capital into fee income. Every strategic decision the firm has made in twenty years — going public, building credit, buying into insurance, courting individual investors, creating funds that never expire — is an attempt to make that fee income larger, more durable and more predictable. Hold that idea and the rest of the report explains itself.

0.2The GP / LP bargain

Almost all of Blackstone's business runs through a structure that has barely changed since the 1980s. Investors — called Limited Partners, or LPs — commit money to a fund. Blackstone acts as the General Partner, or GP: it makes every investment decision, and it is the one on the hook operationally. LPs are passive by design; that is what "limited" means. They get economics and information rights, not control.

Blackstone also puts its own money in alongside — typically 1–3% of a fund — so that the firm and its employees lose money when the LPs do. That alignment is a genuine feature and it is the standard answer to "how do we know you care?"

0.3The fund lifecycle, and why patience is structural

A traditional Blackstone fund — a "drawdown" or "closed-end" fund — has a life of about ten to twelve years, and it moves through phases that determine what everyone in the building is doing at any given moment.

Why this matters for the roles you are interested in

Because of the J-curve, an investor relations or client solutions professional spends much of their life explaining why a fund that looks bad is fine. Being able to hold a client's confidence through years 2–5 of a fund is the actual skill. That is a much better thing to say you find interesting than "I like talking to people."

0.4How the fees really work

"Two and twenty" is the shorthand: a 2% annual management fee and 20% of profits. Blackstone's real numbers are lower and more varied — closer to 1.0–1.5% on average across the firm, because credit and core-plus real estate strategies carry thinner fees than flagship buyout funds — but the shape is right.

The vocabulary you cannot fumble

Eight terms do almost all the work. Misusing any of them marks you instantly; using them precisely marks you just as fast, in the other direction.

0.5The AUM ladder — three numbers, not one

"Assets under management" is the headline, but it is the least useful of the three AUM figures Blackstone reports, because a great deal of it is not currently earning a fee. Understanding the ladder below is genuinely differentiating.

0.6The glossary that matters

Dry powder
Money investors have committed but Blackstone has not yet spent. $228.1bn at Q2 2026. It is an asset, not an embarrassment: it is optionality in a dislocation, and it is future fee revenue.
Perpetual capital
Vehicles with no fixed end date — BREIT, BCRED, BXPE, insurance mandates. Investors can usually redeem periodically within limits, but Blackstone never has to wind the vehicle up and re-raise it. $555.6bn.
Fee-Related Earnings (FRE)
Management fees plus fee-related performance revenues, minus the costs of running the business. The recurring, predictable profit. $1.8bn in Q2 2026, +22% year on year, at a 58.6% margin. This is the number analysts capitalise.
Distributable Earnings (DE)
FRE plus realised performance revenues and realised principal investment income. The cash actually available to pay shareholders. $2.0bn in Q2 2026, +26%. Blackstone pays out essentially all of it as dividends.
Net accrued performance revenue
Carried interest that has been earned on paper but not yet received in cash — a store of future earnings that can still evaporate. $7.5bn, or about $6.00 a share, the highest in four years.
Deployment vs realisations
Money going out into new investments ($34.2bn in Q2) versus money coming back from exits ($31.8bn in Q2; $144.5bn over the last twelve months). Realisations drive carried interest — and therefore drive whether the year is a good one.
Inflows
New money raised. $68.3bn in Q2 2026; roughly $260bn over twelve months. This is the direct scoreboard for the client-facing organisation.
Semi-liquid / non-traded vehicle
A perpetual fund sold to individuals that offers limited monthly or quarterly liquidity — typically capped at 5% of net asset value per quarter. The cap is a feature that prevents forced selling. When redemption requests exceed it, the fund "gates" and fills requests pro rata. This is exactly what happened to BCRED in early 2026.
The three mistakes that will give you away
  • Calling Blackstone "BlackRock." They are different firms, they are not related, and the confusion is a well-worn irritation. (BlackRock was in fact spun out of Blackstone in 1994 — a good fact, a terrible mix-up.)
  • Saying "Blackstone's private equity arm" as though that is the whole firm. Private equity is 34% of AUM and Credit & Insurance is larger.
  • Treating dry powder as idle cash sitting in Blackstone's bank account. It is undrawn commitments from investors — Blackstone does not hold it.

Part 1

How a $400,000 boutique became a $1.35 trillion machine

History is not decoration here. Four decisions made between 1985 and 2021 explain almost everything about how Blackstone behaves today — including why it is so obsessive about not losing money, and why it is so aggressive about fee durability.

1.1The four decisions that still shape behaviour

Decision 1 · Advisory before capital

Because Blackstone began as an advisory firm, its founding culture was relationship-led rather than trading-led. It sold trust before it sold returns. That inheritance is still visible in how heavily the firm invests in client coverage — and it is a direct line to the roles you are interested in.

Decision 2 · Downside first

Schwarzman has said for decades that the firm's job is not losing money. Investment committee discussion begins with what can go wrong, and any partner can raise an objection regardless of seniority. Blackstone's marketing point is that it has never had a fund lose LP capital in its flagship strategies — a claim to state carefully, but the philosophy is real and quotable.

Decision 3 · Go public, then behave like it

The 2007 IPO changed the objective function. A private partnership optimises for carried interest; a listed company optimises for recurring, forecastable earnings. Almost everything since — credit, insurance, perpetual vehicles, retail — is a rational response to being a public company that is valued on FRE.

Decision 4 · Build the brand as the moat

Jon Gray attributes today's fundraising momentum to "the strength of the brand we've built." In a business where the product is a promise about the future, brand is not marketing — it is the thing that lets Blackstone raise $260bn in twelve months while smaller managers struggle to close a fund at all.

Which part actually matters most?

Depends entirely on which question you are asking. Size, profit and momentum give three different answers — and knowing that they differ is the point.

2.2Size is not the same as earnings

This is the single most useful structural insight in the report. Credit & Insurance is Blackstone's largest segment by assets — and its third largest contributor to earnings. Private Equity is the reverse. The reason is fee rates: a private credit mandate for an insurance company might earn 30–50 basis points; a flagship buyout fund earns 1.5% plus carry. Blackstone is deliberately trading fee rate for volume and durability.

By size

Credit & Insurance. $469bn, and the firm has explicitly named it the vehicle for the "next $1 trillion." It also captures the structural shift of lending away from banks.

By profit

Private Equity. $981m of segment earnings in a single quarter, up 31%. Highest fee rates, the carried interest, and the deals that get written about.

By identity

Real Estate. Even at $314bn and shrinking, it is the franchise Blackstone is most famous for, the one Jon Gray built, and the one that owns the data centres.

A question this sets up beautifully

"Credit and Insurance is now the largest segment by AUM but contributes a much smaller share of earnings, because the fee rate is lower. How does the firm think about that trade — is the bet that volume and durability more than compensate for the fee compression?"

Part 3

The money machine

Blackstone has three revenue streams and two profit measures. If you understand which of them the market pays for and why, you understand the firm's entire strategy.

3.1Three ways money arrives

3.2FRE versus DE — the two numbers everyone quotes

These get used interchangeably by people who should know better. They are not the same thing, and the difference is the whole story of what Blackstone has been trying to become.

Fee-Related Earnings — $1.8bn

What it is: profit from management fees and recurring performance fees, after the costs of running the firm. It excludes anything that depends on selling an asset.

Why it matters: it is recurring. A pound of FRE is worth far more to the share price than a pound of carried interest, because it will probably be there next year too. Analysts value Blackstone primarily on a multiple of FRE.

Q2 2026: $1.43 per share, up 22%, at a 58.6% margin — flat on the prior year, which management was pressed on: the richer revenue mix has not yet produced extra operating leverage.

Distributable Earnings — $2.0bn

What it is: FRE plus realised carried interest and realised gains on Blackstone's own money, less tax and interest. It is the cash genuinely available to shareholders.

Why it matters: Blackstone pays out essentially 100% of it as a dividend — a yield roughly four times the S&P 500's — plus a modest buyback. The dividend therefore moves with realisations.

Q2 2026: $1.52 per share, up 26%. Net realisations of $414m, up 27%; gross performance revenues $731m, up 32%.

The strategic point hiding in that comparison

FRE is $1.8bn of the $2.0bn of distributable earnings — roughly 90%. Twenty years ago that ratio would have been far lower and the firm's earnings would have swung violently with the exit cycle. Deliberately shifting the mix from carry to fees is what has made Blackstone investable as a listed company. It is also the reason perpetual capital, insurance mandates and individual investors matter so much: all three produce fee income that does not depend on selling anything.

3.3Where the growth is coming from

What management has told the market to expect

On the Q2 2026 call, CFO Michael Chae guided to mid-single-digit base management fee growth in Q3, stepping up to double-digit growth in 2027. The bridge to that acceleration: private equity funds activated during 2026 earning a full year of fees, perpetual strategies continuing to scale, credit stabilising with $84bn of dry powder, and real estate fees finally ceasing to decline. On realisations, he flagged a slower Q3 but a strong Q4 and 2027, helped by eight IPOs on file globally and roughly a third of the corporate private equity portfolio now publicly traded.

4.1How it is built: three lanes of risk

Real estate is not one business but three, sold to different investors with different appetites. Understanding the three lanes is the fastest way to sound informed about property with anyone in the room.

4.2Where it stands right now

The honest picture

Real estate has been Blackstone's problem child since interest rates rose in 2022, and the numbers show it: segment AUM fell 3% year on year, and base management fees declined as opportunistic funds harvested and institutional core-plus faced redemptions. Fund-level returns have reset hard — BREP IX has come down to around a 7% net IRR from a peak near 47%, and BREP X sits near 10%. Those are respectable absolute numbers and a brutal relative disappointment against what investors were shown in 2021.

Segment earnings nonetheless rose 32%, because harvesting older funds crystallises carried interest even as the asset base shrinks. Both facts are true at once, and being able to hold them together is a sign you have actually read the numbers.

Why Gray thinks the turn is here

  • Logistics is accelerating. Leasing at Link, Blackstone's US warehouse platform, rose 26%. Gray calls logistics his favourite part of the real estate ecosystem.
  • Hotels are recovering. US revenue per available room up around 5%.
  • Even office is bottoming. New York vacancy has compressed from 21.5% to 14.5%, with seven straight quarters of positive net absorption nationally — though Gray is explicit that over-levered office owners remain in deep trouble. His formulation is worth borrowing: "the recovery is real, just unevenly distributed."
  • Financing has reopened. CMBS issuance up 23%.
  • Borrowing costs are down roughly 40% from their peaks.
The tension to be aware of

Private real estate marks have lagged listed REITs year to date. That gap is the crux of the bear argument: either private valuations are about to catch up, or they were never marked down far enough in the first place. Blackstone's answer is that BREIT has outperformed the public REIT index by roughly 40% since inception and that public markets are noisier, not wiser. It is a genuinely open question, and asking about it politely will land well.

Secondaries buy fund stakes from investors who need liquidity. If you want one example of Blackstone thinking structurally rather than deal-by-deal, this is it.

4.4The idea underneath it

BXCI exists because of a structural shift in how the world finances itself. After 2008, bank regulation made it expensive for banks to hold loans to mid-sized companies on their balance sheets. That lending did not disappear — it moved to asset managers funded by long-dated money. Blackstone's insight, and Apollo's before it, was that the natural funder of long-dated loans is a life insurer, whose liabilities are decades long.

4.5The insurance franchise — the strategically important bit

Blackstone manages $290bn for insurance companies and runs what it describes as the largest third-party-focused platform in the sector. The client count has grown to roughly 40 dedicated insurance relationships, nearly double two years ago. The largest is Corebridge — AIG's former life and retirement business — where Blackstone managed $71.2bn of book value at the end of 2025, rising to a contracted $92.5bn by the third quarter of 2027. Corebridge is now merging with Equitable Holdings to form a group with more than $1.5tn of assets under management and administration, which makes that relationship larger still.

In 2026 Blackstone added a partnership with Nippon Life of Japan, covering around $10bn of private credit deployment over several years. Gray describes the whole trend as "structural" — insurers adopting private credit as a competitive necessity rather than an experiment.

The distinction worth knowing cold

Apollo owns its insurer (Athene). Blackstone manages money for insurers it does not own. That single difference — balance-sheet-heavy versus capital-light — is the defining strategic fork in the industry, and it comes up in Part 8. Blackstone's argument is that it can serve forty insurers precisely because it is not competing with any of them.

4.6BCRED, and what happened in early 2026

BCRED — the Blackstone Private Credit Fund — is the world's largest private credit fund at roughly $82bn. It is a non-traded business development company sold largely to individuals through financial advisers, offering quarterly liquidity capped at 5% of net asset value. In Q2 2026 it took $1bn of gross sales but suffered $1.2bn of net outflows, with repurchase requests exceeding the 5% cap and roughly half of requests filled. The full story of how that happened is in Part 7 — it is the most important live issue at the firm and worth reading before you go.

Part 4 · Segment four

Multi-Asset Investing (BXMA)

$108.6bn · up 21% · $98m of segment earnings, up 35%. The smallest segment, the fastest-growing, and the one almost nobody outside the firm can describe.

4.7What it actually does

BXMA was until recently called BAAM — Blackstone Alternative Asset Management — and it is the world's largest discretionary allocator to hedge funds. It does not run the trading strategies itself. Instead, a large institution hands BXMA a mandate and a risk objective, and BXMA constructs a bespoke portfolio of external hedge fund managers across equities, credit, macro and quantitative strategies, monitors them, negotiates fees on the client's behalf and reallocates as conditions change.

It also seeds emerging managers — providing early capital in exchange for a share of their economics — which gives Blackstone an early view of talent across the industry.

The segment is led by Joe Dowling, who ran Brown University's endowment before joining. That background matters: this business is closer to an outsourced chief investment officer than to a fund manager.

Why it is growing so fast

Institutions increasingly want one counterparty to solve a whole problem rather than twenty relationships to manage. BXMA sells solutions, not products, which is a much stickier proposition. On 1 July 2026 the business recorded $4.8bn of inflows in a single month — its best ever.

The newest development is BXHF, a perpetual multi-strategy hedge fund built for wealth clients, which began accepting subscriptions in summer 2026. It is the same playbook the firm ran with BREIT and BCRED, applied to liquid strategies.

A question almost nobody asks

"Multi-Asset Investing is the smallest segment but the fastest growing, and it just had its best month of inflows ever. Is that mostly institutions consolidating relationships, or is something changing in what clients want from hedge fund exposure?" — this signals that you read past the first three segments, which almost no student does.

Part 5 · the part you asked about

The client-facing franchise

Blackstone has two engines. One buys assets. The other raises and keeps the money that buys them. The second engine is younger, growing faster, and is where investor relations and client solutions live.

It is worth stating plainly why this side of the firm has become so important. An investment team can only deploy what it has been given. In a world where every large manager can hire good dealmakers, the scarce capability is gathering and retaining capital at scale — and increasingly, gathering it from investors who have never bought a private fund before. Blackstone raised roughly $260 billion in the twelve months to June 2026. No investment team creates that; a distribution organisation does.

Part 5 · continued

Institutional Client Solutions

The classic investor relations function, at the largest scale in the industry.

5.2What the job actually involves

Blackstone's Institutional Client Solutions organisation is not one team. It is a set of teams, some organised by client type and some by product. Job postings show, for example, a Private Capital Group covering single and multi-family offices, sophisticated registered investment advisers, and endowments and foundations — explicitly positioned to work across every product Blackstone offers rather than selling one fund. Separately there are ICS teams sitting inside BXMA and inside BXCI, each with their own client base and technical demands.

That structure tells you something important: Blackstone deliberately mixes relationship coverage (one person owns the client across everything) with product specialism (someone who genuinely understands credit sells credit). Reconciling those two is one of the perennial organisational tensions in any asset manager, and asking how they balance it is a legitimately sophisticated question.

What an analyst on these teams actually does

  • Coordinating responses to client enquiries — a pension fund asks why a fund's mark moved; you find out, and you package the answer.
  • Preparing performance update calls — the materials, the numbers, the anticipated questions.
  • Owning the contact cadence so relationships do not go quiet between fundraises.
  • Producing client materials with the investment teams — this is where you learn the actual portfolios.
  • Due diligence questionnaires (DDQs) — long, exacting documents from institutional investors and their consultants.
  • Supporting fund closings, including subscription documents and legal review.
  • Market and competitor research to inform how a fund is positioned.
  • Cross-functional projects to build out newer business lines.

Team sizes are small and flat — one posting describes working alongside roughly twelve people of varying seniority. That is the norm: a client-facing analyst gets real exposure to senior people and to clients much earlier than an equivalent hire in a large bank.

The honest framing of the role, which will impress

An investor relations professional at a firm like Blackstone is not a salesperson in the conventional sense. The product sells itself in good years. The job is credibility management across a decade: being the person who explains a disappointing quarter without spin, who knows the portfolio well enough to answer a CIO's question without going away to check, and who is still trusted when the next fund comes to market. Say something close to that and you will sound like you have thought about the work rather than the title.

Private Wealth Solutions

$324bn and growing 16% a year. The most consequential thing Blackstone has built in a decade, led by Joan Solotar, a member of the firm's Management Committee.

5.4The strategic logic

Institutional investors have been allocating to private markets for forty years and are, broadly, full. A large US pension might already hold 15% of its portfolio in alternatives. Individual investors — even wealthy ones — typically hold a low single-digit percentage. If that share moves even a few points, the flow of money dwarfs anything the institutional channel can add.

The obstacle was structural. A traditional ten-year drawdown fund with capital calls at unpredictable moments and no way out is simply unsuitable for an individual. So Blackstone re-engineered the product. The result is the perpetual, semi-liquid vehicle — and understanding its mechanics is genuinely the most useful technical knowledge you can bring to a conversation with anyone on this side of the firm.

5.6The skills that separate people in client-facing roles

Investment fluency, not investment work

You will not build the model. You must be able to explain, unprompted and accurately, why a fund's return moved, what the portfolio is exposed to, and what would have to happen for the thesis to fail. Precision under pressure

A number sent to a sovereign wealth fund is effectively a representation about the firm. Client-facing analysts operate under a level of accuracy discipline closer to compliance than to sales. Errors are expensive and remembered.

Comfort delivering bad news

The defining moment of the role is a quarter where a fund is down, a gate has been hit, or a headline has run. The people who thrive are those who call the client first rather than waiting to be asked.

Institutional navigation

Most of the work is getting the right answer out of a busy deal team, a legal team and a finance team, and assembling it into something a client can act on. Internal credibility is what makes external credibility possible.

Part 6

Leadership, governance and culture

Who runs what, why the firm is shaped this way, and how decisions actually get made.

6.1Why the firm is structured this way

One firm, deliberately

Blackstone could have grown as a holding company of semi-independent boutiques. It chose not to. The segments share a capital-raising organisation, a technology group, a portfolio operations team and a single brand. The commercial payoff is cross-sell: a real estate client can be introduced to credit without a new sales relationship being built from scratch. The cultural payoff is that information travels — the credit team's view of a sector informs the buyout team's underwriting.

The 2023 integration of credit and insurance

Blackstone merged its credit arm (the descendant of GSO) with Blackstone Insurance Solutions to create BXCI. The logic is elegant: insurance provides long-dated liabilities looking for yield; private credit originates long-dated assets that provide it. Running them as one unit means the people raising insurance money and the people originating loans share a single view of supply and demand. The firm explicitly framed the move as the vehicle for its "next $1 trillion."

How decisions get made

Blackstone's investment process is built around written analysis and structured debate. Deal teams produce a memo; an investment committee interrogates it; the discussion starts with what can go wrong rather than what could go right. The firm's own description of its culture emphasises "rigorous debate, written analysis, a strong focus on downside risk, and a culture where the best ideas win regardless of hierarchy."

The important consequence is that anyone in the room can object. Schwarzman has built four decades of messaging around not losing money, and the committee format exists to make caution socially acceptable rather than career-limiting. If you want a single question that shows you understand the firm as an organisation rather than a brand, ask how that actually works in practice when a deal has momentum behind it.

Governance you should know

Blackstone converted from a partnership to a corporation in 2019, which opened it to index funds and broadened its shareholder base considerably. Schwarzman retains substantial control; this is a founder-led public company, not a widely-held one.

7.3The retirement frontier — potentially the biggest prize of all

Part 10

The criticisms, and how to hold them

You do not need to raise any of this tonight. You do need to not be blindsided by it, and to be able to discuss it fairly if a conversation turns serious.

10.1Housing and the landlord question

Blackstone is one of the largest residential landlords in the world, with roughly 350,000 US rental units, and this attracts sustained criticism. The specific charges: aggressive rent increases — at some San Diego properties acquired in 2021, rents rose about 38% against a market average near 20% — and the use of algorithmic pricing software. In December 2025 the US Department of Justice reached a settlement with Blackstone's LivCor subsidiary over allegations that it and other landlords set rents using shared competitively-sensitive information through common pricing algorithms. The RealPage software at the centre of that case is now banned in several US states and cities. A UN special rapporteur has previously criticised the firm's role in housing affordability, and US legislators have proposed bills to restrict private equity ownership of rental housing.

The counter-argument, which Blackstone and its defenders make, is that institutional owners are a small share of the total housing stock, that they add supply through development and renovation rather than removing it, and that housing costs are driven overwhelmingly by planning restrictions and undersupply. Both things can be true: institutional capital is not the cause of a housing shortage, and individual landlords can still behave badly within one.

10.2The private credit critique

The serious version of this argument is not that private credit will blow up tomorrow. It is that: assets are valued by the manager rather than by a market; the sector grew enormously during a period of unusually low defaults; leverage has migrated somewhere less visible to regulators rather than disappearing; and semi-liquid vehicles create a liquidity mismatch between what investors think they own and what the fund actually holds. Coverage in 2026 has been pointed — one widely-read piece described private credit's "zero-loss fantasy" coming to an end as defaults and fund exits rose.

The counter-argument is that gates worked exactly as designed, that direct lending's actual loss experience through the episode was far better than the headlines implied, that Tricolor and First Brands were not middle-market direct lending failures at all, and that a lender who holds the whole loan has more incentive to underwrite carefully than one who originates to distribute.

10.3The structural critiques

Fees and net returns

The long-running academic argument is that after fees, the median private equity fund does not beat public equity on a risk- and leverage-adjusted basis. Blackstone's response is that it is not the median fund — and its flagship track record supports that — but the general point about dispersion in the industry is well founded.

Leverage and employment

Buyouts use debt, and debt makes companies fragile. The critique is that this transfers risk onto employees and suppliers. The defence is that operational improvement, not leverage, has driven the majority of returns in modern buyouts, and that Blackstone's portfolio operations group exists precisely to make that true.

Retail suitability

The strongest live critique. Selling illiquid, hard-to-value, higher-fee assets to individuals — and potentially into default retirement accounts — is a genuine policy question that has not been settled, and the 2026 redemption episode is the exhibit for the prosecution.

Systemic opacity

As lending moves from regulated banks to asset managers, supervisors see less. Blackstone would argue that risk sitting in long-dated, unlevered or lightly-levered funds is safer than the same risk sitting in a deposit-funded bank. That is a reasonable argument, and it has not yet been tested by a real recession.

How to handle this if it comes up

The winning posture is neither defensive nor critical — it is interested. "I've read the criticism about semi-liquid vehicles and retail suitability, and I can see both sides — the gates clearly protected the investors who stayed, but the episode also showed how fast sentiment moves through that channel. How does the firm think about that internally?" That is a serious question asked respectfully, and it is the kind of thing that makes someone remember you.
