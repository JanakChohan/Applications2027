Part 0 — Read this first

0.1 The five-minute version

S&P Global sells judgement about risk and value, packaged as data. It does not lend, trade, underwrite or manage money. It has no balance-sheet risk to speak of. What it owns is a set of reference points that the rest of the financial system has agreed to organise itself around — a credit rating, an index level, a barrel price, a company record — and it charges for access to them.

That produces an unusual economic shape. In FY2025 the firm did $15.336bn of revenue at a ~51% adjusted operating margin, and the two most profitable divisions — Indices (71% adjusted margin) and Ratings (65%) — are essentially toll booths on activity that happens whether or not S&P Global does anything more that day. It has raised its dividend for 53 consecutive years. There are perhaps a dozen businesses in the world with this profile.

As of 1 July 2026 the company has four divisions, and they are exactly the four the event covers: Market Intelligence, Ratings, S&P Dow Jones Indices, and Energy (which the job advert still calls by its old name, Commodity Insights). Mobility was spun out as a separate listed company on 1 July 2026. So "the four divisions" in the advert is not a selection — it is the whole firm.

The thing to internalise before you write a word

Commercial roles at S&P Global are not generic B2B sales. You are selling either (a) a regulated product where the people who make it are legally walled off from you, (b) a benchmark whose value is its neutrality, or (c) a data platform fighting Bloomberg for a seat on a trading floor. Each of those has a different commercial logic. Almost nobody applying to this event will know that. Say it and you are immediately in the top decile.

Part 1 — What S&P Global actually is (from zero)

1.1 The one-sentence answer

S&P Global is an information company that sells the reference points financial markets use to price risk.

Unpack that. Every financial transaction requires the two sides to agree on some facts they did not themselves produce. When a company issues a bond, buyer and seller need a shared view of default risk — that is a credit rating. When a pension fund buys "the US stock market", somebody has to define what that is and maintain the list — that is an index. When a refinery in Rotterdam buys a cargo of North Sea crude, the contract does not name a number; it names a published price assessment. When an analyst models a company, they need standardised financials for it and its peers — that is a data platform.

S&P Global produces all four. That is the whole company. Everything else — the AI tools, the events, the research — is either a delivery mechanism for those four things or an attempt to sell more of them.

Why this is a good business, stated plainly

The reference point is worth almost nothing to produce a second time, and enormously valuable to be the one everybody uses. Once the market has coordinated on S&P's number, a competitor with an equally good number has no product, because the value was never accuracy alone — it was shared acceptance. This is why margins are 65–71% in Ratings and Indices and why they have been for decades.

1.3 What "Essential Intelligence" means in practice

You will hear the phrase constantly on 17 September. It is a marketing wrapper, but it encodes a real strategic claim, and you should be able to translate it rather than repeat it.

The claim is: our data is not a commodity because it is not observable elsewhere. Three of the four divisions produce information that does not exist until S&P Global creates it —

  • A credit rating is an opinion. Nobody else has it until S&P publishes it.
  • A Platts price assessment is a constructed number derived from a proprietary process. There is no "true" Dated Brent price sitting on an exchange to look up.
  • An index level is defined by S&P's own rules and committee decisions.

Only Market Intelligence is largely in the business of collecting and standardising facts that exist independently — and that is precisely why its margin is 34% rather than 65%. That single observation explains most of the firm's financial structure, and it is the sort of thing that will make a commercial leader look up.

2.3 Division deep dive: S&P Global Ratings

$4.72bn FY2025 65% adj. margin Most regulated

What it does

It publishes forward-looking opinions on the creditworthiness of debt issuers and individual debt instruments, expressed on the familiar scale — AAA, AA+, AA … BBB− (the investment-grade floor) … down through BB+ and below to D. A rating is an opinion on relative likelihood of default (and, for some instruments, expected loss), not a buy recommendation and not a price.

How the money works — the issuer-pays model

This is the mechanism most applicants cannot explain, and it is worth being precise.

  1. A company, bank, government or structured vehicle wants to raise debt. Investors will demand a materially higher yield for unrated paper, and many institutional buyers are mandated — by their own investment policy, or by capital regulation — to hold only rated instruments above a threshold.
  2. So the issuer (usually via its investment bank) commissions the rating and pays for it. Fees are typically scaled to issue size in basis points, with floors and caps.
  3. That new-issue fee is transaction revenue. It is one-off and it is entirely dependent on issuance volumes, which is why Ratings revenue swings with the debt cycle. Q2 2026 transaction revenue was $746m, up 25% year on year, on a strong issuance backdrop.
  4. The rating then has to be surveilled for the life of the instrument — reviewed, affirmed, upgraded, downgraded, put on CreditWatch. Issuers pay ongoing annual fees, and large repeat issuers negotiate frequent-issuer programmes that bundle a year's ratings. This is non-transaction revenue: $593m in Q2 2026, +8%. It is the ballast.
  5. Separately, investors pay subscriptions for ratings research and data — RatingsDirect, RatingsXpress and the credit research feeds, largely delivered through Market Intelligence's distribution.
The conflict of interest, and how it is managed — know this cold

The obvious objection to issuer-pays is that the person paying wants a high rating. This is not a hypothetical: mis-rated structured products were central to the 2008 crisis and cost S&P a $1.5bn DoJ settlement in 2015.

The answer, now enforced by regulation, is analytical separation. Under the EU Credit Rating Agency Regulation (1060/2009), supervised directly by ESMA, and equivalent SEC rules for NRSROs, commercial staff are structurally walled off from the rating process. Fee discussions cannot involve analysts; analysts' compensation cannot be linked to the revenue from the issuers they rate; rating committees, not salespeople, decide outcomes. S&P Global Ratings Europe Limited is the ESMA-registered Dublin entity; the UK business is FCA-registered.

Why you should care: a commercial role in Ratings is genuinely different from any other sales job you will ever have. You cannot promise an outcome, you cannot discount for a result, and your product's entire value depends on your inability to influence it. If you can articulate that at the event you will be the only one who does.

The moat

Three of the "Big Three" — S&P, Moody's and Fitch — hold roughly 95% of the global market, with S&P and Moody's each around 40% and Fitch 15–20%. The barrier is not analytical skill; it is regulatory recognition plus incumbency in mandates. Bank capital rules, insurance solvency rules and thousands of individual fund mandates are written referencing ratings from recognised agencies. Rewriting those documents to admit a new entrant is a coordination problem no start-up can solve.

Where growth is coming from

  • Private credit. This is the live story. As lending migrated from banks to direct lenders, the debt still needed ratings for the structures that hold it. S&P's credit estimates — lighter-touch assessments used in middle-market CLOs — have more than doubled since 2021, from about 1,200 to over 2,800. The private-credit CLO market was around $155bn outstanding as of October 2025, roughly 16% of the $977bn US CLO market. Add BDCs, interval funds, collateralised fund obligations and fund ratings.
  • Sovereign and geopolitical repricing. S&P downgraded France from AA− to A+ on 20 October 2025, citing fiscal and political uncertainty, with public debt projected to rise from 112% of GDP (2024) to 121% by 2028. The AAA club is now down to roughly eleven sovereigns.

2.4 Division deep dive: S&P Dow Jones Indices

$1.85bn FY2025 71% adj. margin Highest margin in the firm

What it does

It defines, calculates, maintains and licenses indices — the S&P 500, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the S&P/ASX 200, the VIX methodology, thousands of others. An index is a rulebook plus a maintained constituent list plus a calculated level. That is genuinely all it is, and it is one of the best businesses in the world.

How the money works — three streams

  1. Asset-linked fees (the big one). An ETF or fund that tracks an S&P index pays a licence fee calculated as basis points on assets under management. For the S&P 500 the widely-cited structure is around 3 basis points of AUM plus a fixed component (roughly $600,000 in the SPY arrangement). More than 95% of licensing fees are AUM-percentage-based rather than flat. The consequence: when SPY held around $400bn, S&P DJI collected well over $120m from that single fund.
  2. Exchange-traded derivatives royalties. CME pays a per-contract royalty on E-mini S&P 500 futures and options; Cboe on VIX products. Volume-driven, and volumes spike precisely when markets are volatile — a natural hedge against the AUM stream falling in a selloff.
  3. Data and custom indices. Subscriptions to index constituent and level data, plus bespoke index construction for asset managers and banks (custom benchmarks, structured-product underlyings, direct indexing).
Why the margin is 71%

The marginal cost of the ten-billionth dollar of AUM tracking the S&P 500 is approximately zero. The index is calculated once, whether one fund tracks it or four hundred do. Revenue scales with the market; cost does not. This is the purest operating leverage in the whole company — and it is why Indices grew 14% in FY2025 and 20% in Q2 2026 while Ratings grew 8%.

The one genuinely contentious feature: the Index Committee

Unlike FTSE Russell's largely mechanical rules, S&P 500 membership is decided by a committee applying published criteria with discretion. The committee can and does exercise judgement about timing and suitability. This is a real point of differentiation — S&P argues committee governance produces a more stable, more investable, lower-turnover benchmark; critics argue it is opaque discretion over trillions of dollars of passive flows. This is excellent material for a panel question (§7.2).

Competitive position

The 2025 indexing industry was worth a record $7.2bn. S&P DJI led with $1.85bn, MSCI's index business followed at roughly $1.79bn, FTSE Russell third at about $1.29bn. Those three account for more than 80% of European passive equity fund assets. This is a tight oligopoly with S&P narrowly in front — largely because it owns the single most important index in the world.

What is new

  • S&P Pantera Digital Asset Index (21 July 2026) — an 18-asset institutional benchmark that pointedly excludes Bitcoin and meme coins, screening instead for real-world usage, measurable revenue, liquidity and listing standards. Top holdings include Ethereum, BNB, Solana, Tron and Hyperliquid.
  • S&P Kaiko Digital Asset Indices (announced 1 September 2026 — the day this report was written). A co-branded suite combining Kaiko's crypto-native reference rates and data infrastructure with S&P DJI's index administration, licensing and distribution. Over 4,000 rates and indices at launch, for ETPs, futures, options and structured products.
Use this

The Kaiko deal is sixteen days old at the time of the event. Almost nobody in the room will have seen it. It is also a perfect illustration of the commercial model: S&P contributed distribution, licensing and benchmark administration — not data — while Kaiko contributed the data. That is the firm monetising its brand and its regulatory standing as a benchmark administrator. Referencing it demonstrates you understand what S&P actually sells.

Be able to explain this — it is the most common thing candidates get wrong by assuming all of S&P Global is a 65%-margin machine.

Market share, honestly stated

Bloomberg, LSEG, FactSet and S&P Global Market Intelligence together represent roughly $33.4bn of 2025 revenue and cover around 95% of investment banking and private equity seats globally. Within that, Capital IQ holds roughly 6% share against FactSet's ~4.5% — with Bloomberg dominant. So Market Intelligence is a strong number three or four in a market it does not lead. Saying this out loud is a credibility move. Everyone else will claim S&P Global is number one at everything; the firm's own leaders know Market Intelligence is the challenger, and the strategy is built around that fact.

How it is trying to win

  • Price and position, not feature parity. Capital IQ Pro competes on cost per seat and on depth of fundamentals rather than on matching a Bloomberg Terminal function for function.
  • Cross-sell the unique assets. Nobody else can bundle credit ratings data, Platts benchmarks, S&P index data and company fundamentals in one enterprise agreement. That is the single strongest commercial argument the firm has (§3.3).
  • Agent-native architecture. S&P is exposing Capital IQ Pro data and capabilities through Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers so that clients' own AI agents can consume it. Strategically this is a bet that in an agentic world the winner is whoever holds the licensed, rights-cleared underlying data — not whoever owns the prettiest screen. It is also a risk: it decouples revenue from seats.
  • Pruning. Enterprise Data Management and thinkFolio were sold to Symphony Technology Group on 12 January 2026 for a $172m pre-tax gain. Software that is not data is being cut.

2.6 Division deep dive: S&P Global Energy (formerly Commodity Insights)

$2.30bn FY2025 48% adj. margin Renamed Nov 2025

What it does

It serves producers, consumers, traders and intermediaries across energy, chemicals, shipping, metals, carbon and agriculture. Since the November 2025 rebrand the division presents four capabilities:

  • Platts — price assessments and news. The crown jewel.
  • CERA — research and advisory (and CERAWeek, the industry's flagship conference).
  • Horizons — energy expansion and sustainability solutions.
  • Events — a genuine revenue line, not marketing.

How Platts actually works — the Market on Close mechanism

This is the single most technically interesting thing in the entire company, and it is almost never mentioned by candidates.

There is no exchange that tells you the price of physical North Sea crude. Cargoes trade bilaterally, irregularly, in varying grades. So Platts constructs the price through a structured process:

  1. The MOC window. Platts runs a defined assessment window ending at precisely 16:30:00 London time. Participants submit bids, offers and expressions of interest through Platts' system.
  2. Transparency by publication. Platts publishes those bids, offers and confirmed trades. A participant who bids must be willing to transact — you cannot post a price you would not honour.
  3. Assessment. Platts assesses the tradable, repeatable value at the instant of close. Dated Brent reflects the most competitive of the qualifying light North Sea grades for cargoes loading 10–21 days forward.
  4. Editorial judgement. Where the window is thin, Platts editors apply methodology-governed judgement — normalising for grade, timing and location. The combination of structured observation and judgement is the defining design feature, and the enduring source of academic and industry debate about it.
Why this matters commercially

Dated Brent and its siblings are written into the contracts. National oil companies set Official Selling Prices as a differential to a Platts assessment. Derivatives settle against it. Long-term supply agreements reference it. Hundreds of billions of dollars of annual physical crude trade price off a number that a team of editors publishes at 16:30 London time.

So the product is not "data". The product is the market's agreement to use this number. A competitor with a more accurate assessment still has nothing, because contracts already name Platts. Say this and you have demonstrated you understand network effects in a way no textbook answer does.

Competition

Argus Media (founded 1970, privately held) is the principal global rival. Its methodology differs meaningfully: Argus North Sea Dated reflects a basket of five North Sea grades — Brent, Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk and Troll — assessed FOB, together with US WTI Midland, triangulated against contracts for difference and forwards. OPIS (US refined products, now owned by News Corp) is the third major price reporting agency — and it ended up at News Corp precisely because the DoJ forced its divestiture from the IHS Markit deal (§4.1). Knowing that connection is a genuine differentiator.

The current problem, and the current fix

Energy is the softest division right now: +3% adjusted in Q2 2026, with mid-single-digit growth in Platts offset by weak Global Trading Services volumes, hit by sanctions, Middle East conflict and unusual volatility. Guidance for FY2026 is 4.5–6%, the lowest of the four.

The strategic response, announced 24 April 2026, is to stop selling software and double down on proprietary data: the geoscience and petroleum engineering software portfolio is being sold to SLB (closing H2 2026 or early 2027), with a continuing partnership under which S&P distributes its data through the divested tools and co-develops upstream AI models. In its place S&P is launching Titan, an AI-native upstream data platform covering 113 countries and targeting roughly 110,000 users across 4,000 client organisations.

2.7 Mobility — and why its absence matters

S&P Global Mobility (automotive data: VIN-level vehicle records, forecasting, dealer and aftermarket analytics) was spun off on 1 July 2026 as Mobility Global Inc., ticker MBGL, distributed one-for-one to S&P Global shareholders. It did $1.747bn of revenue in FY2025.

The logic: it served automotive clients, not financial ones, so it shared no customers, no sales force and no distribution with the rest of the firm. Removing it makes S&P Global a pure financial-and-commodity-markets information company. This is a commercial-strategy story, not a finance story — the firm removed the one division that could not be cross-sold. That is exactly the logic a commercial hire should notice.

2.10 What actually goes wrong — the risks worth naming

  • Issuance cyclicality. Roughly a fifth of firm revenue depends on debt being issued. A rates shock or a credit freeze hits transaction revenue hard and immediately.
  • Market beta in Indices. Asset-linked fees fall with markets. A 20% drawdown mechanically reduces the highest-margin revenue in the firm.
  • AI cuts both ways. S&P's own filings flag that "AI presents new and evolving risks". If clients' agents can synthesise research from cheaper sources, the research and desktop layer commoditises. S&P's answer is that licensed, rights-cleared, audited data becomes more valuable when everything else is generated — but that is a thesis, not a fact.
  • Regulatory tail risk in Ratings. ESMA has previously found compliance failings at S&P Global Ratings Europe. The licence to operate is a permission, not a property right.
  • Free and cheap substitutes. The 10-K explicitly warns that "increased availability of free or relatively inexpensive information sources may materially reduce demand" — most directly a Market Intelligence risk.
  • Geopolitics in Energy. Sanctions regimes and conflict directly suppressed Global Trading Services volumes in Q2 2026. This is not abstract.

Being able to name two real risks unprompted is one of the fastest ways to signal that you have read primary sources rather than a careers page.

Practical allocation: spend 30% of your time on the CV, 30% on Q1, 40% on Q2.

6.5 The pitch challenge — the only competitive element on the day

The advert: "Participants work in small groups to develop and practise a pitch for a flagship S&P Global product, such as Capital IQ or Ratings. Commercial leaders provide feedback, with selected groups presenting their pitches on stage."

Two things follow. First, groups are selected to present — so there is a visible win condition. Second, you are assessed on how you behave in the group at least as much as on the pitch. Commercial leaders watching a group exercise are looking for: does this person structure the problem, do they bring others in, do they concede a better idea gracefully, can they be concise under time pressure.

The framework to bring — five boxes, five minutes

  1. Who is the buyer, precisely? Not "a bank" — "the head of a mid-market debt origination team with eight analysts". Everything else follows from this.
  2. What is the job they are failing at today? "My analysts spend six hours building comps that should take one, and the data is inconsistent between decks."
  3. What does the product do about it, in their language? Two capabilities maximum. Resist listing features.
  4. Why us and not the incumbent? Name the alternative honestly. For Capital IQ Pro: depth of private-company and transaction data, price per seat, and the bundling nobody else has (ratings + index + Platts + fundamentals in one agreement). For Ratings: coverage, investor recognition, market access — never a promised outcome.
  5. What is the ask? A trial, a pilot with two users, a meeting with the data team. Close on something concrete.
The move that wins the room

Whichever product your group draws, quantify the value in the client's own currency. "Eight analysts, six hours a week each, at a fully-loaded cost of roughly £60 an hour, is about £150,000 of analyst time a year on work this automates" is a different conversation from "it's a comprehensive platform". Commercial leaders have watched a thousand feature lists. Almost nobody in a graduate group exercise does the arithmetic out loud.

And if you draw Ratings, remember what you cannot pitch: you sell access to capital markets, investor recognition and a credible programme — never a rating outcome. Naming that boundary unprompted, in front of commercial leaders from a regulated business, is the strongest single signal available to you on the day.

Part 7 — Your two answers, drafted

7.1 Question 1 — "Why are you interested in attending this event?" (max 100 words)

What the question is really asking

Not "why do you want a job". It is asking: is this person's interest specific, and will they be a good participant? With 100 words there is no room for a narrative arc. The winning structure is: a specific and slightly unusual understanding of the firm → what you want from the room → a note of genuine curiosity. The failure mode is the generic opener — "S&P Global is a global leader in data and analytics and I am passionate about financial markets" — which is what most of the pile will say.

Option A — the technical opener (recommended)

S&P Global sells the reference points markets organise themselves around — a rating, an index level, a Platts assessment — and I want to understand how those get sold. Commercially that is several jobs: licensing to a handful of ETF issuers, negotiating fees on a product you are legally walled off from influencing, and displacing Bloomberg on a research desk. I want to hear from leaders who do each. I am also curious how the Chief Client Office turns four divisions into one enterprise conversation, as with Barclays. An afternoon with commercial leaders and graduates is the fastest way to test that.

100 words. Why it works: the first sentence demonstrates you understand the business model rather than the marketing. The middle sentence proves you know commercial work here is not one job. "Chief Client Office" and "Barclays" are specific, checkable and current. The close answers the actual question asked.

Option B — the warmer version, if you want the sift reader on side

Most people my age could not tell you what S&P Global does. I could not either, until I looked properly and found that a number its editors publish at 16:30 each day prices much of the world's physical crude, and that a committee decides what belongs in the S&P 500. That is unusually concrete, and I want to know how you sell something whose value is the market's agreement to use it. I want to hear commercial leaders explain that, meet graduates a few years ahead of me, and pressure-test whether this is the career I think it is.

99 words. Use this if you would rather lead with honesty and curiosity than with technical vocabulary. It handles the "I had no idea what this firm was" starting point head-on and converts it into a virtue. Slightly higher risk, higher warmth.

Which to send

Option A. It carries more information per word, and the sift is looking for demonstrated interest, which A evidences and B mostly asserts. Take one sentence of B's voice into A if A reads too clipped when you say it aloud. Whichever you use, rewrite it in your own idiom before submitting — a 100-word answer that does not sound like you will be exposed the moment somebody asks you about it in person, and a panel of graduates on 17 September may well do exactly that.

Things to cut if you write your own

  • "Passionate about financial markets" — unfalsifiable and universal.
  • "Global leader in data and analytics" — their own boilerplate handed back to them.
  • Anything about your degree or grades. The CV covers it; the 100 words are too scarce.
  • "I would love the opportunity to…" — spends five words saying nothing.
  • Naming all four divisions with no distinguishing comment. Listing is not knowledge.

Part 9 — Questions they will ask you, and how to answer

"So, what do you know about S&P Global?"

Answer: "It sells the reference points markets price risk against — a credit rating, an index level, a Platts assessment, a company record. Four divisions since Mobility spun out in July. What surprised me is how different the four businesses are commercially: Indices licenses to a handful of very large counterparties, Ratings has commercial teams legally separated from the analysts, and Market Intelligence is a challenger fighting Bloomberg on a research desk. I wanted to hear how that works in practice, which is partly why I applied."

Why it works: it answers in one breath, demonstrates structure, and hands them three threads to pull on. Never list the four divisions and stop.

"Why commercial, and not analytical or credit?"

Answer: "Because the interesting problem here is not producing the intelligence, it is getting a client to change how they work. S&P has assets nobody else can bundle — ratings, indices, Platts and fundamentals in one agreement. But breadth only creates value if the divisions actually sell together, which is presumably why the Chief Client Office exists. That is the problem I want to work on. And I would rather be in the conversation with the client than one step behind it."

"Why S&P Global rather than a bank or a consultancy?"

Answer: "Two reasons. First, I would rather learn the infrastructure than one seat on top of it — ratings, indices and benchmarks sit underneath every bank and every asset manager, so understanding them travels. Second, this is a growing business with a commercial function it is actively building out rather than one that has been fixed for thirty years. That is a better place to be early."

Do not disparage banking. Some of the people asking will have come from it.

"Which division interests you most?"

Answer (adapt to who you are talking to, but commit): "Indices, for the economics — asset-linked fees on a product with essentially zero marginal cost, which is why it grew 20% last quarter. But commercially I think Market Intelligence is the harder and more interesting job, because you are the challenger and you have to win on value rather than incumbency. I would want to understand both before choosing."

Committing to a view and then qualifying it reads as judgement. Refusing to choose reads as evasion.

"What do you think the biggest challenge facing the firm is?"

Answer: "Honestly, what AI does to the value of a research desktop. If clients' agents can assemble analysis from anywhere, a per-seat platform fee is harder to defend. S&P's answer seems to be to bet that licensed, rights-cleared, auditable data becomes more valuable when everything else is generated — which is why they are exposing Capital IQ Pro through MCP servers rather than defending the screen. I think that is the right bet, but it changes what a commercial team is actually selling."

This is the answer that will get you remembered. It names a real risk, credits the firm's response, and lands on the commercial implication.

"Sell me this pen / pitch me Capital IQ Pro in a minute."

Structure: "Who am I talking to?" — always ask first; the question tests whether you qualify before you pitch. Then: buyer → the job they are failing at → two capabilities → the honest alternative → a concrete ask. Quantify in their currency (§6.5).

"Tell me about a time you persuaded someone."

Structure: situation, the specific resistance, what you did differently because of that resistance, the measurable outcome, what you would do differently. Sixty to ninety seconds. The middle section is the whole answer — most people describe what they wanted rather than what the other person needed.

Three questions to ask them at the reception

  • To a graduate: "What is the thing about the job that nobody tells you in the recruitment process?"
  • To a commercial leader: "When you look at a graduate after twelve months, what separates the ones who are going to be good at this?"
  • To a recruiter: "What does the 2027 process look like from here, and is there anything I should be doing between now and then?" — this is the one that converts an event into an application.
