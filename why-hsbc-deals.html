Your goal, delivered. You wanted three distinct, somewhat technical, non-generic reasons to want HSBC — the kind most candidates can't say. Below are exactly three, each built on real, named deals (who the client was, what the transaction was, why it was impressive, and how it shows relationship-building), each tied back to the business structure, and each ending with a line you could actually say. Pick the two or three examples that genuinely click with you and make them yours.

Tags: OFFICIAL = from HSBC, a counterparty, or a regulator · CORROBORATED = reputable trade press or the client itself · INFERRED = my analysis. Bracketed numbers like [1] link to the source list. A glossary sits at the end.

First, the one idea that ties it all together

In Corporate and Institutional Banking, a "deal" is rarely a one-off. The Relationship Manager (the client-facing banker who owns the relationship) lands a client with one product, then expands — bringing in trade finance, payments, currency, capital-raising, custody — until the client runs much of its financial life through HSBC. The three reasons below are really three versions of the same question: what can HSBC do for a client that a rival genuinely can't? The deals are the proof. INFERRED

REASON 1 — The two-ended network you cannot rent

The reason, stated technically: HSBC doesn't just lend to a company — it plugs into the company's whole cross-border operation: its overseas suppliers, its international payments, its currency exposure, its offshore fund-raising. Because HSBC has a licensed, funded presence at both ends of a trade or capital flow across about 56 markets, it can bank a client's whole map, not one country. That's why 62% of HSBC's revenue comes from clients it banks in more than one country OFFICIAL[31] — a number a domestic bank structurally cannot reach. This sits in the Global Trade Solutions (trade-finance) and Global Payments Solutions (payments and cash-management) teams, funded by HSBC's cheap deposit base. OFFICIAL[30]

The deals that prove it:

  • PVH Corporation (owner of Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger) — a supplier-financing programme wired to ESG. In 2022 HSBC built PVH a sustainable supply-chain finance programme: HSBC pays PVH's suppliers early, at cheaper rates for suppliers that score better on environmental and social standards (safe conditions, fair pay, no forced or child labour) — billed as the first such programme tied to both environmental and social measures, not just carbon. OFFICIAL[1] Why it's a relationship story: HSBC isn't financing one company — it's financing PVH's entire global supplier ecosystem and turning a bank product into a lever for the client's own sustainability strategy. That is deep embedding. ("Supply-chain finance" = the bank pays a company's suppliers early and gets repaid by the company later, easing cash flow for everyone.)

  • Vingroup / VinFast (Vietnam's largest private company and its electric-vehicle arm) — a relationship that compounded over years. In 2022 HSBC helped arrange a US$500 million green syndicated loan (up from a planned US$400 million on strong demand) — Vingroup's first international green syndicated loan. CORROBORATED[6] Crucially, this was about HSBC's fifth financing for Vingroup since 2018, each bigger and more sophisticated, following a world-first sustainable exchangeable bond for its Vinpearl arm. Why it's a relationship story: this is the textbook "land and expand" — a frontier-market champion connected to international capital, coming back to HSBC again and again. ("Syndicated loan" = a big loan shared among several banks so no one bank carries all the risk. "Green loan" = the money must fund environmentally beneficial projects.)

  • Ant International (the international arm of Alipay) — real-time global treasury. In 2025 HSBC launched its Tokenised Deposit Service — 24/7 instant corporate payments — with Ant International as its first client, letting Ant move money between its own accounts instantly around the clock. OFFICIAL[3] Why it's a relationship story: HSBC co-built the product around a named client's actual problem (a global tech firm needing money to move at any hour), then scaled it — the deepest kind of client partnership. (More on the technology in Reason 3.)

  • Cotton Growers Services (an agricultural joint venture) and HP Inc. — the everyday plumbing that locks clients in. HSBC gave Cotton Growers Services the first "TradePay" facility in Australia — document-free trade loans that draw down in under a minute through HSBC's online portal, so the client can pay suppliers just in time. OFFICIAL[8] For HP Inc., HSBC deployed a custom cash-flow forecasting tool across multiple Asian markets, saving "hundreds of man-hours." OFFICIAL[7] Why it's a relationship story: these aren't glamorous, but they embed HSBC in a client's daily operations — and once a company's payments and trade run on HSBC's rails across many countries, it almost never leaves. HSBC is the number-one trade-finance bank in the world, nine years running. CORROBORATED[10]

Ties to structure: this is HSBC's cheap-deposit-funded transaction banking flywheel — capital-light, sticky, recurring fee income — and it's the biggest single reason Corporate and Institutional Banking is the group's largest profit contributor. INFERRED[30]

A line you could say: "What draws me to the client-facing side of HSBC is that it doesn't just lend to a company — it plugs into the company's whole cross-border operation. The PVH supply-chain programme financed an entire supplier network against social and environmental standards; the Vingroup relationship grew across five financings since 2018. That's relationship-building a domestic bank simply can't replicate — and it shows up in the fact that 62% of revenue comes from multi-country clients."

REASON 2 — The China "licence-and-plumbing" moat

The reason, stated technically: HSBC is arguably the Western bank most deeply embedded in China's capital markets and in the international use of the Chinese currency (the renminbi). It holds the onshore licences to underwrite bonds inside China, it leads bonds in offshore Chinese currency in Hong Kong, and it was live on day one of the specialist "plumbing" that lets global investors access and hedge Chinese bonds. Very few non-Chinese banks can do all three. This lives in the Markets and Securities Services and debt-financing teams, anchored by HSBC's Hong Kong home. OFFICIAL[11]

Quick glossary for this section: a panda bond is a bond that a foreign issuer sells inside mainland China, in onshore Chinese currency. A dim sum bond is a bond in offshore Chinese currency, issued outside the mainland (usually Hong Kong). A bookrunner is the bank that builds investor demand and prices the bond. To issue a panda bond you need a Chinese underwriting licence — a real barrier.

The deals that prove it:

  • HSBC's own panda bond — proof the licence works end to end. In November 2024 HSBC issued a 4.5-billion-renminbi (about US$630 million), 3-year panda bond inside China's interbank bond market, with HSBC's own China arm as lead underwriter — reportedly the lowest interest rate (coupon) for a 3-year panda bond by a foreign commercial bank, and 1.88 times oversubscribed. OFFICIAL[11] Why it's impressive: HSBC is (by its own account) the only foreign bank able to underwrite across both of China's onshore bond venues and the offshore Hong Kong market — self-issuing proves the machinery works.

  • Egypt's sovereign panda bond — a landmark "bridge" trade. In 2023 HSBC was a joint lead underwriter and bookrunner on the Arab Republic of Egypt's 3.5-billion-renminbi sustainable panda bond, guaranteed by two multilateral development banks (the African Development Bank and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank). CORROBORATED[12][13] Why it's impressive: it was Africa's first panda bond and the first sovereign panda jointly guaranteed by two development banks — a government from one continent raising Chinese money, with HSBC as the bridge. ("Sovereign" = a national government as the borrower.)

  • BMW's panda bond — opening China's market to global companies. HSBC was a joint bookrunner on BMW's 2021 panda bond, which HSBC calls the first publicly-syndicated panda bond from a foreign multinational company, with roughly half the orders coming from outside mainland China. OFFICIAL[14] (Slightly before the main window, but a clean "first" worth knowing.)

  • Swire Properties' green dim sum bond — anchoring the offshore market. In 2023 HSBC was a joint global coordinator on Swire Properties' 3.2-billion-renminbi green dim sum bond in Hong Kong — the first Hong Kong company to sell a public renminbi green bond and the largest corporate green dim sum bond at the time. CORROBORATED[15]

  • Swap Connect and Bond Connect Repo — being first on the plumbing. When Swap Connect launched in 2023 (the channel that lets foreign investors hedge the interest-rate risk on their Chinese bonds), HSBC executed inaugural day-one trades as both market-maker and clearing broker for offshore funds. CORROBORATED[16] In 2025 HSBC did some of the first offshore trades using mainland Chinese bonds as collateral to raise offshore funding ("Bond Connect Repo"). OFFICIAL[17] Why it's impressive: these are the rails, not the deals — being live first shows HSBC is built into the market, not just selling into it.

  • And the same model, other axis — the Middle East. HSBC won International Financing Review's "Middle East Bond House" as global coordinator on almost every big Gulf transaction, including Qatar's US$2.5 billion green bond (the first from a Gulf state) and a US$12 billion Saudi Arabia issue. CORROBORATED[18] Why it matters: it proves the "connect global capital to emerging-market governments" model isn't a China one-off.

Ties to structure: this is the Hong Kong + China anchor of the group — the reason HSBC's whole strategy leans East — expressed through the capital-markets and Markets teams inside Corporate and Institutional Banking. INFERRED

A line you could say: "The reason I find HSBC's model technically special is its position in China's capital markets. It underwrote its own panda bond onshore, led Egypt's Africa-first sovereign panda, and was live on day one of Swap Connect — the plumbing that lets foreign investors hedge Chinese-bond risk. That's a licence-and-infrastructure moat almost no Western bank has, and it's the client-facing edge when a company wants to move capital between China and the world."

REASON 3 — Owning the whole digital-asset stack, in the hub where it's happening

The reason, stated technically: HSBC is one of very few banks that owns the entire digital-assets stack — a proprietary platform to issue tokenised (digital) bonds, a licence to issue regulated digital money, live tokenised-deposit payment rails plugged into Hong Kong's central-bank project, and the traditional custody network that safe-keeps the assets underneath. And it's doing it in Hong Kong, the hub pushing hardest on this. This sits in Markets and Securities Services and Global Payments Solutions. OFFICIAL[29]

Quick glossary: tokenisation means representing a real asset (a bond, a deposit, gold) as a digital token on a shared electronic ledger so it can be issued and settled electronically. Custody means safe-keeping clients' investments and handling the paperwork. A stablecoin is a digital token pegged one-to-one to a currency and fully backed by reserves.

The deals that prove it:

  • The Hong Kong Government's digital green bond — a world first, on HSBC's own platform. In February 2024 the Hong Kong government issued about HK$6 billion (roughly US$770 million) of green bonds natively on HSBC's Orion platform, across four currencies at once (Hong Kong dollar, offshore Chinese currency, US dollar and euro) — the world's first multi-currency digitally-native bond, and the largest digital bond at the time. CORROBORATED[19][20] Why it's a relationship story: a government trusted HSBC's own technology end to end, and HSBC connected that platform into the market's infrastructure so global investors could buy in — the bank as both the technology provider and the distribution bridge.

  • The European Investment Bank's digital bond — the blue-chip first. HSBC ran the account-keeping and was a joint lead on the European Investment Bank's £50 million digital bond in 2023 — the world's first digital bond in pound sterling and the debut live deal on Orion. OFFICIAL[21]

  • The Hong Kong Mortgage Corporation's HK$12 billion digital bond — scale. In 2026 HSBC took multiple lead roles on a HK$12 billion digital bond on Orion, billed as the world's largest-ever, with over US$3.5 billion of digital bonds already issued on Orion before it. OFFICIAL[22] Why it matters: first-of-a-kind is nice; repeatable at scale is a business.

  • Ant International and tokenised deposits — a client story from sandbox to live. HSBC moved from a 2024 test with Ant International under Hong Kong's central-bank "Ensemble" project, to a 2025 live product, to the first live cross-bank tokenised-deposit transfer — a real HK$3.8 million (about US$489,000) payment for Ant into an account at another bank. OFFICIAL[23][24] Why it's a relationship story: the whole arc was pulled through by one named client's needs — exactly the client-facing, co-creation work you're drawn to. (A "tokenised deposit" is ordinary insured bank-deposit money issued as a digital token so it can move instantly and around the clock.)

  • The custody network underneath — the unglamorous moat. None of the above works without safe-keeping. In 2024 U.S. Bank appointed HSBC as its local safe-keeping arm (sub-custodian) across Hong Kong, South Korea and Taiwan OFFICIAL[27], and in 2025 Allianz Australia picked HSBC as custodian and fund administrator after a competitive tender. CORROBORATED[28] Why it matters: a rival global bank and a global insurer both chose HSBC as the trusted local operator — the clearest proof of its Asia custody-network leadership, the base that the flashy digital stuff is built on.

Ties to structure: this is Markets and Securities Services plus payments, showcased in HSBC's Hong Kong/Asia stronghold — capital-light, sticky, and forward-looking. INFERRED

A line you could say: "The third reason is that HSBC owns the whole digital-asset stack in the hub where it's actually happening. Hong Kong's government issued the world's first multi-currency digital bond on HSBC's own Orion platform; HSBC built tokenised-deposit rails live with Ant International; and it still runs the custody network that other global banks like U.S. Bank plug into. That combination of proprietary innovation and the safe-keeping base underneath — in Asia — is genuinely rare."

How to use these without sounding rehearsed

  • Pick, don't recite. In a two-minute video answer you have room for one reason plus one deal, told naturally. Reason 1 is the safest for a general "why HSBC / why Corporate and Institutional Banking"; Reason 2 is the most impressive if you're comfortable with the China angle; Reason 3 if you want to sound forward-looking. INFERRED
  • Lead with the client, not the jargon. "HSBC financed Calvin Klein's entire supplier network against social standards" lands better than a string of product names.
  • Make it personal. The genuine bit is why it appeals to you — e.g. "I like that the relationship compounds over years, like Vingroup's five financings." That's the part no one can copy.
  • Keep a number in your pocket (62% multi-country revenue; number-one trade bank nine years; world's-first multi-currency digital bond) — one concrete figure signals you've actually read.

Glossary

  • Corporate and Institutional Banking — HSBC's wholesale bank for big companies, governments and financial institutions.
  • Relationship Manager — the client-facing banker who owns a client relationship and brings in specialists.
  • Land and expand — win a client with one product, then sell them more over time.
  • Transaction banking — the everyday plumbing: payments, cash management and trade finance.
  • Trade finance / supply-chain finance — products that help companies buy and sell across borders and pay suppliers; supply-chain finance pays a company's suppliers early.
  • Syndicated loan — a large loan shared among several banks.
  • Green / sustainability-linked loan — a loan whose use or interest rate is tied to environmental or sustainability goals.
  • Renminbi (also called the yuan) — China's currency. Onshore = used inside mainland China; offshore = used outside, mostly in Hong Kong.
  • Panda bond — a bond a foreign issuer sells inside mainland China, in onshore renminbi.
  • Dim sum bond — a bond in offshore renminbi, issued outside the mainland (usually Hong Kong).
  • Bookrunner / lead underwriter / global coordinator — the bank(s) that build investor demand, price the bond and lead the sale.
  • Coupon — the interest rate a bond pays. Tenor — the bond's life/maturity.
  • Sovereign / supranational — a national government / a multi-country institution (as a borrower).
  • Bond Connect / Swap Connect — channels that let foreign investors buy mainland Chinese bonds, and hedge their interest-rate risk, via Hong Kong.
  • Tokenisation / digital bond — representing an asset as a digital token on a shared ledger; a bond issued that way.
  • HSBC Orion — HSBC's own platform for issuing and settling digital bonds.
  • Tokenised deposit — insured bank-deposit money issued as a digital token so it can move instantly, 24/7.
  • Stablecoin — a digital token pegged one-to-one to a currency and fully backed by reserves.
  • Custody / sub-custodian — safe-keeping investors' assets; a local bank a global custodian hires to hold assets in a specific market.
  • Assets under custody — the total value of client assets a custodian safe-keeps.
