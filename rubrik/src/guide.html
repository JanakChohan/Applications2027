1Explain it like I'm 12

The one-sentence bottom line

Rubrik keeps a sealed spare copy of everything a big organisation runs on, so that when hackers break things, the organisation can put itself back together in hours instead of weeks.

"Everything" used to mean files and databases. Today it also means the login system (who is allowed into what) and, most recently, the actions of AI assistants that do work on a company's behalf.

An everyday analogy

Think of a save point in a video game. You save before the boss fight. If it goes badly, you reload and carry on from just before things went wrong.

Rubrik is a save point for a whole company. The difference is that it keeps the saves in a safe the burglar cannot open, even if the burglar is already inside the building.

That second part matters. Modern attackers hunt down and delete the backups first, so the victim has no choice but to pay. A save you cannot delete removes their leverage.

Rubrik also adds a security guard who inspects the saves. The guard can tell you which save is from before the break-in, so you do not reload the burglar along with your data.

A real-life story, start to finish

This example is made up, but each step matches how a real deal and a real recovery work. Meet Harlow & Finch, a UK clothing retailer with 300 shops.

  1. The scare. Marks & Spencer is hit by hackers and loses about £300 million of profit. Harlow & Finch's board asks its IT director one question: "Could that be us?"
  2. The uncomfortable answer. The IT director checks. The current backups sit on the same network as everything else. A full rebuild has never been tested. The honest estimate is three to four weeks.
  3. Rubrik steps in. A reseller introduces Rubrik. Two people turn up: a salesperson, and a Sales Engineer (SE), who is the technical expert. The SE asks hard questions and finds the gaps.
  4. The trial. The SE runs a "proof of concept": Rubrik protects a slice of the retailer's real systems for a few weeks. The team practises a recovery and times it.
  5. Money changes hands. The retailer signs a three-year subscription, sized by how much data it protects. It pays the reseller, who keeps a margin and pays Rubrik.
  6. Quiet months. Rubrik takes sealed copies every few hours. It follows simple rules the retailer set, such as "copy the tills system every four hours and keep it for a year".
  7. The bad day. At 2am on a Friday, criminals who tricked the help desk into resetting a password scramble the servers and the login system. Shops cannot take card payments.
  8. The recovery. Rubrik has already flagged the odd activity. The team searches the copies, finds the last clean one, and restores the login system first, then tills, then warehouse. No ransom is paid.
  9. The follow-on. Shaken but trading, the retailer adds Rubrik for its email and its login system. That extra spend from existing customers is how Rubrik grows.

What Rubrik is NOT

  • Not a burglar alarm. Antivirus and firewalls try to keep attackers out. Rubrik assumes they will get in and focuses on getting you back up. It partners with the alarm companies, such as CrowdStrike and Microsoft.
  • Not a storage locker. Dropbox or Google Drive hold your files. Rubrik holds protected copies with a history, plus the tools to judge which copy is safe.
  • Not an AI model maker. It does not build chatbots like OpenAI or Anthropic. It watches, limits and undoes what other companies' AI assistants do.
  • Not an insurer. It does not pay your ransom. It does offer a recovery warranty on its top plan, but the product is the recovery, not a payout.
  • Not for consumers. You cannot buy it for your laptop.
  • Not "Rubik". It is spelled Rubrik, with a second R.

2Everything they sell

Rubrik sells software by subscription, grouped into two platforms. A platform just means several products that share one screen, one login and one set of rules.

A. Data Protection (the foundation)

What it is: scheduled, sealed copies of a company's systems, wherever those systems live.

  • In the company's own buildings: servers, databases (Oracle, Microsoft SQL Server, SAP), shared file stores.
  • In rented cloud space: Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud. Google BigQuery was added in November 2026.
  • In online apps: Microsoft 365 (email, Teams, SharePoint), Salesforce, Jira.
  • In code stores: GitHub and Azure DevOps, where software teams keep their code.

How it works: the customer writes a short rule, for example "copy every four hours, keep for a year, hold a second copy in another country". Rubrik calls a rule like this an SLA Domain. The software then does the rest.

Copies are written to storage that only allows adding, never editing. The industry word is immutable, which simply means "cannot be changed". Even an administrator with a stolen password cannot scramble them.

Why Rubrik sells it: it is the entry ticket. Every other product relies on having these copies and their history.

B. Attack Recovery (finding the clean copy fast)

What it is: tools that inspect the copies for signs of an attack and tell you which one is safe to restore.

  • Anomaly Detection: compares each new copy with the last. A sudden wave of scrambled or deleted files is flagged.
  • Threat Hunting: searches the copies for the fingerprints of known malicious files.
  • Turbo Threat Hunting: the fast version. Rubrik says it can check up to 75,000 backups in under a minute (its own test).
  • Clean-room recovery: an isolated space to rebuild and test systems before reconnecting them.
  • Autonomous Business Recovery: restores a whole application in the right order. The cloud version is in private preview.
Why "turbo" is possible

The slow way is to open every backup and scan it. Rubrik instead records a fingerprint (a "hash") of each file at the moment it takes the copy. Hunting for a bad file then becomes a quick lookup in an index, not a trawl.

Why Rubrik sells it: in a real attack, copying data back is rarely the slow part. Working out which copy to trust is. That turns backup into a security purchase.

C. Sensitive-Data Mapping

What it is: a map of where a company's sensitive information sits, and who can reach it. The industry name is Data Security Posture Management, or DSPM.

How it works: it scans stored data, labels things like card numbers or patient records, and shows which people and apps have access. The technology came from Laminar, bought in 2023.

Why Rubrik sells it: firms switching on AI helpers like Microsoft Copilot suddenly need to know what those helpers can read. It also tells a victim what was exposed after a break-in.

D. Identity Resilience (protecting the login system)

What it is: backup and recovery for the systems that decide who can log in to what. The three big ones are Microsoft Active Directory, Microsoft Entra ID and Okta.

Why it matters: the login system is the master key cabinet. If it is down, nobody can sign in to anything, including the tools needed to fix the problem. Attackers know this and go for it first.

  • Forest recovery: rebuilds an entire Active Directory with a guided five-step process. Rubrik says Microsoft's own manual method runs to 29 steps and over 100 pages.
  • Identity-First Recovery: lifts only the directory's data out of the backup and places it on a brand-new Windows machine. Anything hiding in the old machine is left behind.
  • Okta recovery: restores deleted users, groups, apps and rules straight into the live Okta account.
  • Microsoft Defender link (March 2026): Microsoft spots a compromised account, Rubrik reverses just that account's changes.

The newest idea: Roll Forward

Old-style recovery forces a painful trade. Go back three weeks and you are clean, but you lose every new starter, leaver and password change since. Stay where you are and the attacker's hidden accounts stay too.

Identity Roll Forward (announced June 2026, private preview) restores the clean copy, then replays only the legitimate changes and drops the attacker's.

Identity Continuity (also private preview) covers the gap while you recover. If the main login provider goes down, it switches people to a standby one. It comes from Strata, a company Rubrik bought in June 2026.

Why Rubrik sells it: most break-ins now start with a stolen or tricked login, not a clever virus. And no other system can be restored until logins work again.

E. Rubrik Agent Cloud (supervising AI assistants)

An AI agent is an AI assistant that does not just answer questions. It takes actions: it edits files, changes databases, sends messages. That is useful, and risky, because it acts at machine speed.

Agent Cloud launched in October 2025. Rubrik describes it in four parts:

  • Watch (Agent Observability): a live list of every agent a company runs, what each can touch, and what each is doing.
  • Permit (Agent Identity, August 2026): an agent gets a short-lived pass for one single action, then the pass expires. No agent holds a permanent key.
  • Stop (Agent Runtime Security): a small AI model called SAGE judges the intent of each action before it runs and blocks ones that break the rules.
  • Undo (Agent Rewind, August 2025): if an agent does damage, trace exactly what it changed and reverse only that.

In June 2026 Rubrik released a version built for Anthropic's Claude Code, a popular AI coding assistant. It keeps sealed copies of code stores and of the settings files that control how the assistant behaves.

Why Rubrik sells it: "undo" is what Rubrik already does well. Agents are a new thing that needs undoing. It also moves Rubrik from the security budget into the much larger AI budget.

F. Rubrik AI and Annapurna

Rubrik AI (June 2026) lets a customer state a goal in plain words, such as "recover the payments system cleanly". The assistant then plans and runs the steps inside Rubrik's products.

Annapurna feeds a company's stored documents to its own AI tools, while respecting who is allowed to see what.

G. Services around the software

  • Support and a Ransomware Response Team: help during a live attack.
  • Professional Services: paid help to install and set up the products.
  • UK Sovereign Cyber Recovery Cloud (March 2026): a rebuild space kept entirely inside the UK, run with Rackspace.

3How they make money

The mechanics of one deal

A customer signs a contract, usually for three years, to use Rubrik's software. The price depends mainly on three things.

  • How much is protected. For servers and files, this is measured by the volume of data stored. For apps like Microsoft 365, it is per user.
  • Which plan. There are three tiers: Foundation, Business and Enterprise. Higher tiers unlock the attack-recovery tools.
  • Which extras. Identity, cloud, online-app and AI-agent products are added on top.

Rubrik does not publish prices. Every deal is quoted and discounted. Outside price lists suggest list prices of roughly $130 to $230 per terabyte per month before discounts, but treat that as a rough guide only.

Where the profit comes from

Software costs a lot to build and little to copy. Once the product exists, each extra customer mostly adds revenue. Rubrik's main running costs are staff, cloud hosting bills and the margin it gives to resellers.

Two terms you will hear constantly

Subscription ARR means annual recurring revenue: the yearly value of all live subscriptions. It is the scoreboard number. At 31 July 2026 it was $1.66 billion, up 33% in a year.

Land and expand means win a customer with one product, then sell them more. Nearly half of Rubrik's 2,000 European-region customers use three or more.

Is Rubrik profitable?

It depends which measure you use, and this is worth understanding properly.

  • Cash: yes. It took in $65.7 million more cash than it spent last quarter. It expects $323m to $333m for the full year.
  • Official accounting profit: no. It reported a loss of $61.8 million last quarter. Much of that gap is staff pay given in shares, which costs no cash.
  • Cushion: $1.75 billion in cash and short-term investments.

Who the money passes through

Rubrik rarely bills the customer directly. It sells through partners: resellers, distributors, consulting firms, and the Amazon, Microsoft and Google online stores.

4Who buys from them, and why

The customers

Large organisations with a lot to lose from being offline. Rubrik had over 6,000 customers when it listed its shares in 2024. At July 2026, 3,084 of them paid over $100,000 a year, up 23%.

  • Banks and insurers: regulators set hard limits on how long key services may be down.
  • Hospitals and health bodies: downtime can harm patients. UK examples include Northumbria Healthcare NHS Trust.
  • Government and councils: for example Brent Council in London.
  • Retailers and manufacturers: every hour offline is lost sales or a stopped production line.

Why Rubrik focuses on them

Big organisations have huge amounts of data, strict rules to follow, and enough money to pay a premium for safety. They also buy more products over time, which suits the land-and-expand model.

The urgent, expensive problem

Being switched off by criminals. Ransomware is software that scrambles a company's systems until a ransom is paid. The damage from the downtime usually dwarfs the ransom itself.

  • Marks & Spencer (2025): around £300 million knocked off profit, with online orders halted for weeks.
  • Jaguar Land Rover (2025): production stopped. The UK's Cyber Monitoring Centre put the cost to the UK economy at about £1.9 billion.
  • Synnovis (2024): an attack on an NHS blood-testing supplier led to thousands of postponed appointments in London.

Four things turn that fear into a purchase:

  • Attackers go for the backups first. Ordinary backups can be deleted by anyone with an administrator password.
  • Regulators now ask for proof. Banks must show they can recover inside a set time, not just say so.
  • Cyber insurers demand it. No tested recovery plan often means a higher premium or no cover.
  • Boards are asking about AI. Rubrik's own survey says 86% of IT leaders expect AI agents to outrun their safety controls within a year.

Why it is worth the price tag

Rubrik is one of the more expensive options. The argument is simple arithmetic: a few hundred thousand pounds a year against tens of millions per week of downtime.

It also replaces several older tools with one, which saves staff time. And on the top plan it backs its claim with a recovery warranty of up to $10 million, subject to conditions.

5History, size and how it runs

The founding

Rubrik was founded in January 2014 in Palo Alto, California, by four engineers and investors: Bipul Sinha, Arvind Jain, Soham Mazumdar and Arvind Nithrakashyap.

Sinha was a venture capitalist at Lightspeed, which became the first backer. Their view was that backup software was old, fiddly and built from many parts. They put it all in one box you could set up in under an hour.

The big turn came around 2020 to 2022. As ransomware exploded, Rubrik stopped calling itself a backup company and rebuilt its pitch around security. It also moved from selling boxes to selling subscriptions.

  • Designed for attacks from day one. Copies have been unchangeable since the first product. Rivals had to add this to older designs.
  • One system, not a bundle. Data, logins, cloud and AI agents share one screen and one rulebook. Buyers want fewer suppliers.
  • The history of the data. Because it holds copies over time, Rubrik can compare today with last week. That is what powers its attack detection.
  • Speed of new products. Okta recovery, Agent Cloud, Agent Identity and Roll Forward all appeared within about a year.

Why this strategy: backup alone is a slow-growing market where price wins. Security and AI budgets are bigger and growing. Moving right on the map above lets Rubrik charge more and grow faster.

How it defends the position

  • More products per customer. A customer using four Rubrik products is far harder for a rival to remove.
  • Buying to fill gaps: Laminar, Predibase and Strata.
  • Alliances: Microsoft (also a shareholder), CrowdStrike, Okta, Amazon, Google.
  • The warranty: putting money behind the recovery promise.
Honest weak spots (good to know for interviews)

It is usually the priciest choice. It still reports an accounting loss. Several headline features are in preview, not on full sale. And in AI agent safety it faces far larger security companies in a market that is not yet proven.

8Big-picture trends

Trends helping Rubrik

  • Attackers log in, they do not break in. Groups such as Scattered Spider phone help desks and talk their way into password resets. That pushes spending toward login recovery.
  • AI agents are arriving faster than the rules for them. Rubrik's survey found only 23% of IT leaders can see every agent running in their organisation.
  • Regulators want evidence. See the box below.
  • Buyers want fewer suppliers. Tight budgets favour one product that does four jobs.
  • The VMware shake-up. After Broadcom bought VMware and raised prices, many firms are moving systems elsewhere. A big move is a natural moment to rethink backup.
  • Data must stay in-country. Governments increasingly require this, which suits Rubrik's UK-only recovery service.
The rules worth knowing by name

DORA: an EU law for financial firms, in force since January 2025. It requires tested recovery plans.

NIS2: an EU law widening cyber duties to many more sectors.

UK Cyber Security and Resilience Bill: the UK's update. It was in its final House of Lords stages in late October 2026. Check whether it has become law.

Bank of England resilience rules: since March 2025, UK banks must stay inside a stated maximum downtime for key services.

Trends hurting or threatening Rubrik

  • Everyone is converging. Veeam bought Securiti. Cohesity absorbed Veritas. Commvault sells login recovery. The gap Rubrik opened is narrowing.
  • Security giants are moving in. CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks and Microsoft want the AI-agent safety market too, and already own the customer relationships.
  • Cloud providers offer basic backup free or cheaply. Good enough for some buyers.
  • AI spending could cool. If the AI boom slows, the Agent Cloud bet pays off later than hoped.
  • High expectations. The share price assumes fast growth continues. A weak quarter would be punished.

How Rubrik is adapting

  • Widening the definition of "recovery" from data, to logins, to AI actions, so it is not stuck in a price war over backup.
  • Partnering with would-be rivals. It feeds its findings into CrowdStrike and Microsoft tools so it is seen as a complement.
  • Buying specialists to enter new areas quickly.
  • Investing locally in the UK to meet in-country rules and sit closer to regulated customers.

9Your application question: answer ideas

The question

"Pick one Rubrik product or capability. Who would you pitch it to, and what's the first question you'd ask them?"

What they are really testing

The advert says SEs "ask the hard questions that uncover a prospect's real business pain". So the first question you choose matters more than the product you choose.

Most applicants will write some version of: "Rubrik Security Cloud, to a security chief, asking how confident they are about ransomware." That answer names the whole company, the obvious buyer, and a question about feelings.

The recipe for a standout answer

  • One narrow capability. Name a specific feature, not a platform.
  • One specific person. A job title, an industry, and a reason they care this month.
  • One question with a factual answer. Not "how confident are you?" but something they must go and check. The pause while they think is the pitch.
  • One line on what the answer tells you. This shows you understand discovery.

Idea 1 (my top pick): Identity-First Recovery

Pitch to: the Head of Infrastructure at a UK retailer, in the year after Marks & Spencer.

"If every domain controller were encrypted tonight, what would your backup admins log in with?"

Why it lands: a domain controller is a server that runs the login system. Many backup tools check passwords against that same login system. So when it is down, the people who should restore it cannot sign in. Most teams have never tested this.

The technical depth: Rubrik's controls are hosted outside the customer's network with their own sign-in. Identity-First Recovery lifts only the directory database (a file called NTDS.dit) and its settings folder (SYSVOL) onto a freshly built Windows server.

So anything hidden in the old server's operating system is not restored. You can then add the second question: "Do you want it back clean, or current?" That opens Roll Forward.

Why nobody else will say it: it names an actual file, a non-obvious buyer, and a trap the customer has not spotted.

Idea 2: Agent Rewind for Claude Code

Pitch to: the VP of Engineering at a fast-growing software firm that has just given AI coding assistants to every developer.

"If a coding agent force-pushed over your main branch and deleted the others at 2am, what would you restore from that the agent's own access could not also have deleted?"

Why it lands: a force-push overwrites shared code history. The normal undo lives in the same place as the damage, under the same permissions the agent used. Engineering leaders assume version control is their safety net.

The technical depth: Rubrik keeps unchangeable copies of GitHub and Azure DevOps outside the code store. It also tracks the files that steer the assistant (CLAUDE.md, settings, tool permissions) and flags unexpected changes to them.

Why nobody else will say it: you pitch to the person who wants agents to go faster, not the person who wants to block them. It also links neatly to the internship's AI project.

Idea 3: Turbo Threat Hunting

Pitch to: the Head of Operational Resilience at a UK bank, who reports recovery times to the regulator.

"In your last recovery test, how much of the clock went on restoring data, and how much went on deciding which backup you could trust?"

Why it lands: almost nobody measures the second number, and it is usually the larger one. Banks must stay inside a stated maximum downtime, so that hidden time is a regulatory problem.

The technical depth: the usual method opens and scans each backup in turn. Rubrik stores a fingerprint of every file when the backup is taken, so the hunt is an index lookup. Rubrik's test figure is 75,000 backups in under a minute.

The line to use: "Recovery is a search problem before it is a storage problem."

Idea 4: Agent Identity

Pitch to: the Head of Identity at an insurer already using Okta or Microsoft Entra.

"How many of your AI agents are holding a key right now that never expires?"

Why it lands: identity teams spent ten years removing permanent passwords from people. Agents are quietly bringing them back, and the honest answer is usually "we do not know".

The technical depth: each action an agent requests passes three checks. The last one issues a pass valid for that single action. It plugs into the Okta or Entra set-up the firm already has, so no new directory is needed.

Watch out: Rubrik has not said clearly whether this is on full sale yet. Say so if you use it.

Idea 5: UK Sovereign Cyber Recovery Cloud

Pitch to: the technology chief of an NHS trust.

"If you had to rebuild your patient record system after an attack, where would you rebuild it while investigators still have your data centre sealed off?"

Why it lands: after an attack, the original site is a crime scene and may still be infected. You need somewhere else to rebuild, and for patient data that somewhere must be in the UK.

The technical depth: the service, run with Rackspace since March 2026, is an isolated rebuild space inside UK borders that is switched on only during a recovery.

Why nobody else will say it: it is a UK-only offering, for a London role, that most applicants will not know exists.

Idea 6: Autonomous Business Recovery

Pitch to: the Head of Site Reliability at a fintech running entirely on Amazon Web Services.

"Where is the order in which your systems must come back written down, and when was that document last true?"

Why it lands: cloud systems change weekly, so written recovery plans go stale almost at once.

The technical depth: the customer tags one starting resource. Rubrik follows the connections outward to map everything it depends on, then restores network first, then computing, then data. Private preview only.

A draft you can adapt (Idea 1)

Longer version (about 150 words)

Identity-First Recovery, pitched to the Head of Infrastructure at a UK retailer. After M&S, their board has already asked "could that be us?"

My first question: "If every domain controller were encrypted tonight, what would your backup admins log in with?"

Most backup consoles authenticate against the same Active Directory they are meant to restore, so the honest answer is often a long pause. That pause tells me where the conversation needs to go.

From there I would show that Rubrik's control plane sits outside the domain, and that Identity-First Recovery lifts only NTDS.dit and SYSVOL from an immutable backup onto a freshly built Windows server. Whatever was hiding in the old operating system stays behind.

Then the second question: do you want your directory back clean, or current? Roll Forward, now in private preview, is Rubrik's attempt to make that a false choice.

Shorter version (about 60 words)

Identity-First Recovery, to the Head of Infrastructure at a UK retailer. First question: "If every domain controller were encrypted tonight, what would your backup admins log in with?" Most backup tools authenticate against the directory they are meant to restore. Rubrik sits outside the domain and rebuilds NTDS.dit and SYSVOL on a clean server, leaving the attacker's foothold behind.
