Three questions, 800 words in total. This part covers how they are probably weighted, what each one tests, what a stand-out answer does, and a draft frame for each with placeholders for your own story.

A1. Is there an official weighting?

No. Weatherbys does not publish how the form is scored. Its careers pages and the 2027 advert give no weighting or marking scheme [S9][S14] Confirmed. No UK private bank that I could find publishes its rubric [S173] Reported. Anyone who gives you an exact percentage is guessing. What follows is a reasoned estimate, labelled Inferred.

How UK employers usually mark forms like this

  • Each answer is scored against a behaviour. The best public example is the UK Civil Service scale, which runs from 1 ("not demonstrated") to 7 ("outstanding"). The usual pass mark is 4, which means "adequate positive evidence and any negative evidence would not cause concern" [S173]. Other employers use a 0 to 3 scale per competency [S174].
  • Markers look for three things. Evidence of the behaviour, your own part in it ("I", not "we"), and a result you can point to [S173][S175].
  • Screeners are wary of generic text. In the ISE 2025 survey, 48% of employers worried that students' AI use misrepresents their skills [S172]. Specific, personal detail is the best defence.
  • At a small firm, a person probably reads every form. Weatherbys hired five Associate Private Bankers in 2025 [S4], so the intern cohort is likely small. That favours answers with a real voice over keyword stuffing Inferred.

A2. Question 1: "Why are you interested in a career in private banking and wealth management?" (250 words)

What is really being tested

  • Do you know what the job is? Many applicants confuse private banking with investment banking or stock-picking. A private banker at Weatherbys manages a portfolio of wealthy clients (the Senior Private Banker advert says 100+) across "banking, lending and investments" [S24]. Most of the bank's income is interest from lending and deposits, not investment fees [S4].
  • Is your interest real and earned? Have you acted on it?
  • Have you done research that a casual applicant would not?
Key term: private banking versus wealth management
Private banking is personal banking for wealthy people: accounts, deposits, tailored loans and mortgages, all through one named banker. Wealth management is managing and planning their investments, usually for a fee based on the money managed. Weatherbys does both through one banker, with its in-house Investment & Wealth Advice (IWA) team behind them [S9][S163]. The question names both, so your answer should touch both.

The structure (about 250 words)

Word budgets are guides. Total 250. The question asks about the career, so block 3 must stay under half the answer: it is "why this career, shown through this firm".

Stand-out moves for Q1

  1. Start with a moment, not a statement. Avoid "I have always been passionate about finance." Use a short scene with a person in it: a family member's mortgage, a farm's succession question, a manager at a part-time job, a client you served.
  2. Name the mechanism you find interesting. For example, judgement over automation. Weatherbys says lending decisions are made by "a person who understands complex HNW income structures" rather than by "automated credit scoring" [S17]. The CEO says: "I try and leave as much judgement and discretion with the front line as I possibly can, because that's actually what clients want" [S15].
  3. Use one live issue that affects Weatherbys' clients. The best one: since 6 April 2026, full agricultural and business property relief from inheritance tax covers only the first £2.5m per person (£5m per couple) [S152]. Farms, estates and family businesses are Weatherbys segments [S16][S19]. A second option: falling rates cut the bank's interest margin from 4.53% to 4.05%, which is why advice and investment income matter [S4].
  4. Show that you understand the business and not only the brand. One number is enough. Pick NPS 84 [S4], the "double in size" plan [S4], or the CEO's 10,000 to 15,000 relationship ceiling [S15].
  5. End on the long term. The APB route, a named qualification (CeMAP, IAD or PCIAM, all supported by the bank [S9]), and the idea of being a family's banker for decades. This fits a seven-generation family firm.
Don't say this in Q1
"I want to work with high-net-worth individuals" (sounds like you want proximity to money). "Fast-paced environment" (says nothing). "Prestigious" (a value judgement about the firm, not about you). Anything about trading or markets as your main motive. A list of five adjectives. Horse-racing enthusiasm you do not genuinely have: the Racing Bank is a real part of the firm and a marker who rides will spot it.

A3. Question 2: "Tell us about a time you built a strong relationship with someone. How did you do it?" (300 words)

What is really being tested

This is the job itself. A Weatherbys banker's career depends on whether clients trust them enough to bring the next loan, refer a friend, or stay through a bad year. The bank describes success in a banker's first year as "a strong internal network" and "a growing base of engaged clients" [S9]. Notice the second half of the question: "How did you do it?" More than half your words should go on method, not on the setting.

2.1 The founding insight: a business built on being trusted with other people's money

In 1770 the Jockey Club appointed James Weatherby, a Northumbrian solicitor, as its Secretary, "Keeper of the Matchbook and Stakeholder" [S5] Confirmed. A stakeholder literally held the stakes, the money riding on a race, until the result was known. The family then kept racing's records: the first Racing Calendar in 1773 and the General Stud Book in 1791 [S5]. So the business began as the neutral, trusted keeper of money and records for a close community. That is still the pitch of a private bank 256 years later.

The bank came much later. Weatherbys got its banking licence from the Bank of England in 1994. Sir Johnny Weatherby was "instrumental in obtaining our banking licence" [S4]. In 2005 the business split into Weatherbys Ltd (racing services) and Weatherbys Banking Group. In 2006 it formed the Private Bank and the Racing Bank and opened in central London [S5].

Why the firm is built this way

Four questions: why the structure, how the teams work together, what clients are really buying, and what the rest of the group does. This is the material that makes "why Weatherbys" more than adjectives.

3.1 One banker, the whole client: why that design?

Most large banks split a wealthy client across specialists: a mortgage team, an investment manager, a call centre for daily banking. Weatherbys gives one banker the whole relationship, with specialists behind them rather than in front of the client [S9][S16].

Sources: "Named private banker and assistant from day one" [S16]; holistic across lending, IWA investment and daily banking [S9]; human credit decisions [S17]. The left panel is a stylised alternative, not a description of any named bank [Inferred].

The theory behind it

  1. Wealthy clients' affairs are complex, and the complexity lives between products. A farm owner's mortgage, inheritance plan and business account all interact. Only someone who sees all three can spot the problem or opportunity Inferred.
  2. Human credit decisions win the clients that computers reject. The CEO's example is racing syndicates: "You can try and open an account for it with a High Street bank, but it would be close to impossible. The computer says: 'No!'" [S15]. Lending is "flexible underwriting rather than fixed criteria" with no upper age limit, and land and investment portfolios are accepted as security [S17].
  3. The relationship is the product. Board member Rory Tapner: "Pushing products won't work for the type of clients Weatherbys seeks to attract" [S4]. The careers page: "exceptional service, trust and holistic advice rather than product pushing" [S9].
  4. Low risk follows from knowing the client. The bank says it is conservative because it knows its borrowers, and its loans average about 34% loan-to-value [S4] Inferred link.

What the design costs

  • It does not scale infinitely. The CEO says "the upper limit for being able to be so flexible is 10,000 to 15,000 private banking relationships" [S15]. Growth means hiring bankers, which is expensive. That is one reason costs rose 19% in 2025 [S4].
  • Key-person risk. If a banker leaves, clients may follow. That is why the bank invests in culture and in growing its own bankers through the APB programme Inferred.
  • Judgement must be controlled. Discretion at the front line needs strong credit oversight, and the bank has a board Risk Committee chaired by Jane Millar [S4].

5.2 The structural forces reshaping UK private banking

  • Consolidation. In H1 2026, UK financial services deal value rose from £4.2bn to £33.7bn, and wealth and asset management deals from £200m to £22.7bn (EY data) [S159]. Legacy names have gone: Child & Co's last branch closed in June 2022, and Adam & Co's banking moved into Coutts in 2022 [S123][S120].
  • Deposit competition. Hoare's grew deposits 5% "despite the headwind of quantitative tightening and competition from tax-advantaged gilts" [S105]. Hampden grew deposits 36% in a year [S128].
  • Wealth leaving the UK. HMRC data shows non-dom taxpayers fell from 83,100 to 81,900 in the last year of the old regime [S149]. Henley projected a net loss of 16,500 millionaires in 2025, but its projections are disputed [S161].

5.3 Three competing views of the next three to five years

View A: scale wins

Bank-owned groups combine banking, investment and advice. They spread technology and regulatory costs across more clients and squeeze smaller independents. Evidence: NatWest plus Evelyn at about £131bn of client assets; the M&A surge [S103][S159].

View B: boutiques win

When big brands raise minimums and standardise, clients who want a named banker and flexible lending move to independents. Evidence: Weatherbys NPS 84, Hampden +36% deposits, Investec adding 5,000 UK clients; the lighter SDDT capital regime from 2027 [S4][S128][S113][S143].

View C: tax and policy decide

Tax changes shift value from holding deposits to advice, succession and lending around tax events. Rates stay volatile. Evidence: APR/BPR cap, pensions in IHT, and the September hike votes [S152][S154][S102].
