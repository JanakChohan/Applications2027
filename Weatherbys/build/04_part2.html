The firm as a business, from the inside

Where it came from, how the money moves, what each party gives and gets, how risk is controlled, and what has changed in the last two years.

2.1 The founding insight: a business built on being trusted with other people's money

In 1770 the Jockey Club appointed James Weatherby, a Northumbrian solicitor, as its Secretary, "Keeper of the Matchbook and Stakeholder" [S5] Confirmed. A stakeholder literally held the stakes, the money riding on a race, until the result was known. The family then kept racing's records: the first Racing Calendar in 1773 and the General Stud Book in 1791 [S5]. So the business began as the neutral, trusted keeper of money and records for a close community. That is still the pitch of a private bank 256 years later.

The bank came much later. Weatherbys got its banking licence from the Bank of England in 1994. Sir Johnny Weatherby was "instrumental in obtaining our banking licence" [S4]. In 2005 the business split into Weatherbys Ltd (racing services) and Weatherbys Banking Group. In 2006 it formed the Private Bank and the Racing Bank and opened in central London [S5].

2.2 Who owns it and how the group fits together

  • Private and family-owned. The bank calls itself "a seventh generation, family-owned bank" [S4]. Max Weatherby joined in early 2026 as "the first member of the eighth generation" [S4]. Roger and Johnny Weatherby stopped being registered controllers of the holding company in May 2024, when trustee-controllers were registered [S7].
  • Why private ownership matters for your research. There are no analyst reports, share price or earnings calls. The best sources are the annual report, Companies House, Pillar 3 disclosures and trade press. Quoting the annual report shows effort most applicants will not make.
  • Leadership. Chairman Roger Weatherby (CEO 2000 to 2023, chairman since March 2024). CEO Quentin Marshall (since September 2023, ex-Coutts and UBS). Non-executive directors include Rory Tapner (CEO of Coutts 2010 to 2015, joined October 2025) and Aileen Wallace (fintech, January 2026) [S4][S15] Confirmed.

2.4 Why profit halved, and why that is a good thing to understand

Two things happened at once. Rates fell, which squeezed the margin on the bank's large pool of deposits. At the same time, the bank chose to spend more: costs rose 19%, including over £8m on technology, new bankers, a Business Bank and a new London office [S4]. The CEO frames this as investment for the plan to "double in size over the next five years" [S4].

Your opinion goes here
Is it right to invest heavily while margins are falling? For: independents win share when big banks raise minimums and standardise service (Part 8); the balance sheet is very strong (Section 2.6). Against: ROE of 8.9% is below the bank's own aspiration, and if rates fall further the margin squeeze continues. A balanced line: "It's a bet that service and new segments grow fee and lending income faster than rates erode the deposit margin. The 30% growth in investment assets under advice suggests it is starting to work." [S4]

2.6 The risk architecture: why the balance sheet is so conservative

  • Private client loans are low-risk. They are "secured principally on residential properties at an average loan to value ratio of approximately 34%" [S4]. House prices would have to fall about two-thirds before the average loan lost money Inferred.
  • Liquidity is huge. A liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) of 1,080% means the bank holds about ten times the cash-like assets needed to survive a 30-day run. That matters to clients with deposits well above the £120,000 compensation limit [S145].
  • The CEO's words: "Managing risk is fundamental to any Bank. For us, it is second nature. We understand our top priority is keeping our clients' money safe" [S4].
Key terms
CET1 ratio: the bank's highest-quality capital (mainly shareholders' equity) as a share of risk-weighted assets. It is the main cushion against losses.
Liquidity coverage ratio (LCR): liquid assets divided by the cash the bank could lose in a 30-day stress. It must be at least 100%.
Impairment: money set aside for loans that may not be repaid. Weatherbys' charge was £4.6m in both 2024 and 2025.

2.8 Things that should not surprise you

  • No regulatory fines or enforcement found in searches of public sources Reported. One Financial Ombudsman decision appeared in search results but was not read [S32].
  • Profit before one-offs halved in 2025 (Section 2.4). Know the reason; do not be thrown if asked.
  • Gender pay gap: median 31.4%, mean 36.2% (2025 report). HR Director Tom O'Reilly writes that the bank "must do more" [S34]. If diversity comes up, acknowledge it calmly; it is common in private banking because senior banker roles are male-dominated.
  • Self-description as "traditional and hierarchical". The CEO's own words [S15]. Do not call the firm "stuffy", but do not pretend it is a start-up.
