The scenario playbook. One scenario for every line of the job description, easy to hard, with what to say and why. Then the composure set: two PMs shouting, a PM asking you to break a rule, a CFO saying too much. Then how they will grill the CV, and a two-page cheat sheet.
Prepared: 5 September 2026, for Round 1 on Monday 7 September, 10:00 to 10:30, Webex, two interviewers.
What this is: a short, to-the-point guide to holding your frame when the interviewers are trying to break it. Read Part 1 twice. Read the scenario cards once and say the answers out loud. Sleep with the cheat sheet.
How to use the answers: they are scaffolds, not scripts. The interviewers want to hear your thinking in your words. Every card gives you the shape of a good answer and the reason it is good, so that when they twist the scenario you can rebuild the answer rather than recite it.

Part 1

How they test you, and how to hold your frame

A scenario interview is not a knowledge test. It is a test of whether your thinking stays orderly when someone is pushing on it. Order beats brilliance. Here is the order.

1.1 What "breaking your frame" means

Your frame is the set of priorities you reason from. The interviewers will invent a situation, watch you reason, then change the situation, interrupt, disagree or go silent to see whether your priorities hold or whether you start agreeing with whoever is loudest. A candidate whose frame breaks says different things under pressure than they said thirty seconds earlier. A candidate whose frame holds gives the same priorities in a different situation, calmly, and says what they do not know. The job description asks for exactly this: "a strong sense of prioritization and urgency" and the ability "to prioritize and execute in a fast-paced, changing environment" [S40].

Two things make frame-holding easier than it sounds. First, a corporate access desk has a natural order of priorities that you can learn in a minute (Figure 2). Second, almost every scenario can be answered with the same four steps (Figure 1). If you use the same steps every time, you cannot be pushed off them, because you are not improvising a structure, only filling one in.

1.2 What wins when things collide

Every hard scenario is a collision between two good things: a PM's urgency against a rule, one team's request against another's, speed against accuracy. You need a fixed order for which wins. This is the order a corporate access desk actually runs on, and you can state it out loud in the interview.

How to stay calm, physically

Before you speak: breathe out slowly, not in. Exhaling drops your heart rate; inhaling raises it. While they push: slow your speech by 10%, drop your pitch slightly, keep your hands still and visible. If you lose the thread: say "Let me take that in order" and go to step 1. If you make a mistake: "Actually, I'd correct that:" and give the correction. Self-correction under pressure is one of the strongest signals you can send. Keep a pen and paper off camera; write the scenario's two or three facts as they give them; look up when you answer.

A. Client service, with urgency

"Support the Corporate Access team in delivering a high level of client service to Portfolio Managers and Analysts, with a strong sense of prioritization and urgency, primarily across EMEA." [S40]
A1. An analyst emails at 16:50 asking for a call with a company's IR team "this week"Easy
The trap
Promising the meeting. You do not control the company's diary, and a promise you cannot keep is worse than a clear "here's what I'll do".
Say
"I'd reply within the hour so they know it's in hand, and ask two things: what they want to cover and whether a group call would do if a one-on-one isn't available. Then I'd check whether a broker already has that company on a calendar this week, and if not route it to the sector specialist for an originated request. I'd tell the analyst what I'd found by end of day, even if the answer is 'not this week, but here are the options'."
Why it works
Acknowledge fast, qualify the request, use both channels (broker-sourced and originated are the two the desk runs [S40][S42]), and close the loop with the client at a stated time. Urgency is shown by the reply time, not by promising outcomes.
The twist
"The analyst says the PM needs it before results on Thursday." Companies restrict what they say close to results [S22]. Say so: "I'd flag that IR may be in a quiet period, so I'd offer the call for after results as the realistic option and tell the PM today."
A2. Three PMs message you in the same hour: a room for tomorrow, a conference request that closes tonight, and a "quick" data pullMedium
The trap
Doing them in the order they arrived, or by seniority of the PM. Also: not telling anyone they are waiting.
Say
"I'd sort by deadline and reversibility. The conference request closes tonight and can't be reopened, so that's first. The room for tomorrow is second: it takes five minutes and a delay costs a meeting. The data pull is third, and I'd message that PM straight away with a time I'll have it, so they're not chasing. If all three genuinely couldn't be done, I'd tell my associate which one I was going to drop and why, before dropping it."
Why it works
It shows a rule (deadline, then reversibility, then effort), it communicates to the person who is waiting, and it escalates a real conflict rather than silently failing. This is the "prioritization and urgency" line of the job description made concrete [S40].
The twist
"The data pull PM is the most senior person on the floor." Hold: "Seniority matters for how I communicate, not for the order. I'd still do the closing deadline first, and I'd call rather than message that PM so they hear it from me."
A3. A PM says, in front of his analysts, that "corporate access got us nothing this quarter"Hard
The trap
Defending yourself, or agreeing. Both are about you. The PM's complaint is about his coverage.
Say
"I wouldn't argue it in the room. I'd say 'Understood, let me come back with what we did and didn't get you', and then pull the team's consumption data: meetings attended, requests made, requests we couldn't fill and why. I'd bring that to him one-to-one, ask what he actually wanted that quarter, and turn it into a plan for the next one. If the data showed we had failed, I'd say so."
Why it works
The consumption report exists exactly for this conversation; the posting calls it a report "for strategy sessions with Portfolio Managers and Analysts" [S40]. You take the heat, move the discussion to facts, and treat the PM as a client with a legitimate grievance rather than an opponent. Not defending yourself is the frame.
The twist
"The data shows his team made two requests all quarter." Do not gloat. "Then the conversation is about how we market events to his team, not about what we failed to deliver. I'd ask what would make him request more."

B. Internal sales and target marketing

"Support internal sales and target marketing efforts." [S40]
B1. A broker sends a European healthcare conference agenda with 80 companies. What do you do with it?Easy
The trap
Forwarding it to everyone. That is noise, and noise trains PMs to ignore the desk.
Say
"I'd load it into the portal first, so it's on the forward calendar. Then I'd target: which teams cover healthcare, which of the 80 companies are names they hold or have asked about before, and I'd send those teams a short note with the relevant companies pulled out and the request deadline. Booking opens weeks ahead and fills fast, so the deadline is the headline."
Why it works
"Target marketing" means knowing which team wants what and pushing the right event to the right desk before the window closes. Conference booking typically opens four to six weeks out and slots fill quickly [S30]. Relevance and deadline are the two things a PM needs from you.
The twist
"How do you know which teams cover healthcare?" "From the portal's history of requests and attendance, and from asking my associate. I'd build that map in my first two weeks; it's the whole basis of the job."
B2. You marketed an event to twelve teams and nobody replied. The deadline is tomorrow.Medium
The trap
Sending the same email again, louder. Or assuming they are not interested.
Say
"Silence usually means the email didn't land, not that nobody cares. I'd call or message the two or three teams most likely to want it, with one line: 'X and Y are presenting, slots close tomorrow at noon, do you want me to put in a request?' I'd make it a yes/no question. Then I'd note who was reached so the associate knows what's been done before the deadline."
Why it works
Changing channel and reducing the ask to a binary is basic internal sales. A multi-manager corporate access head describes engagement as planned "through carefully coordinated quarterly plans rather than ad hoc conference attendance" [S10]; your job is to make the ad hoc easy when it comes.
The twist
"One PM says 'stop spamming me'." "I'd apologise once, ask how he'd like to hear about events, and record the preference. A PM telling you how to reach him is a gift."
B3. A PM asks, casually, "who else on the floor is going to the meeting with that CFO?"Hard
The trap
Answering. It sounds harmless. It is another team's research agenda and possibly its position.
Say
"I'd keep it light but not answer: 'I can't share other teams' requests, but I can tell you the format is a small group and I'll make sure you get a slot.' If he pushed, I'd say it's the same rule that protects his own requests. I wouldn't make it a lecture."
Why it works
Teams compete for capital and do not share ideas [S313]; the desk sees every team's demand and must not become the leak. The rule applies symmetrically, which is why you can state it without sounding pious. This is the single most likely "small" trap in the interview.
The twist
"He says 'I only want to know if it's a long or a short'." Same answer, warmer. "That's exactly the thing I can't share. Happy to get you in the room, though."

C. Communication channels and streamlined processes

"Implement internal and external communication channels and help streamline processes for sell-side broker-sourced and Millennium-originated Corporate Access events." [S40]
C1. The shared corporate access inbox has 200 unread emails on Monday morningEasy
The trap
Starting at the top and reading in order.
Say
"I'd triage in three passes. First, anything with a deadline today or a confirmation for today's meetings. Second, broker calendars and new events, which go into the portal. Third, everything else. I'd use simple rules or folders so the same sorting happens automatically next Monday, and I'd tell my associate the first-pass items by 9:30."
Why it works
The posting says interns will be "handling various databases and email correspondence" and "working to increase efficiencies" [S40]. Triage by deadline plus a repeatable rule is the whole answer.
The twist
"An email from a company's IR team says the CFO can do 15:00 today." That is a first-pass item: confirm receipt to IR within minutes, then find the requesting team. IR teams remember who replies quickly.
C2. A broker keeps sending requests to a PM directly instead of through the deskMedium
The trap
Telling the PM off, or letting it slide because "the PM got his meeting".
Say
"The problem isn't the PM, it's that the meeting won't be in the record. I'd call the broker, thank them for the access and ask them to copy the desk so we can log attendance and give them credit in the broker vote; framing it as in their interest works better than as a rule. Then I'd tell the PM I'd picked it up and ask him to forward anything similar so it counts."
Why it works
The record matters for compliance and for the broker vote, which decides how the firm rewards brokers [S43][S39]. You fix the process by aligning incentives, not by policing.
The twist
"The broker says the PM asked them to keep it off the desk." Now it is a level-1 question. "I'd stop, not confront anyone, and tell my associate the same day. That's not an intern's call."
C3. You changed the request template to make it faster. A week later a request was missed because a field you removed mattered.Hard
The trap
Explaining why the change was reasonable. It was, and that is not the point.
Say
"First the PM: I'd tell them what was missed and what I'm doing to recover it, today. Then the fix: restore the field. Then my associate: what happened, that it was my change, and what I'd do differently, which is test a change on one week's requests before rolling it out and ask who else relies on the field. I'd rather own it in one sentence than have it come out later."
Why it works
Order: client, fix, disclosure, prevention. A senior Millennium risk leader on what he had to learn: "how to handle being wrong and respond with perspective" [S384]. Owning a mistake fast is a frame move; explaining it away is a break.
The twist
"Your associate is annoyed and says you shouldn't have touched it." "Fair. I'd ask what the process is for proposing changes and use it next time. I'd still want to keep improving the process; I'd just do it with the team, not to the team."

D. The proprietary portal

"Update and maintain the internal proprietary Corporate Access portal to manage forward-looking event marketing and requests." [S40]
D1. You notice the portal shows a conference on the wrong dateEasy
The trap
Fixing it silently. If requests were made against the wrong date, someone is about to miss something.
Say
"I'd check the source, the broker's invitation, to be sure which is right. Fix the date. Then check who had requested or been marketed the event and tell them the corrected date, because a wrong date in a PM's diary is a missed meeting. Then I'd look at whether other entries from the same broker file are wrong too."
Why it works
Verify, fix, notify the affected, check for the pattern. The portal is the desk's single source of truth for "forward-looking event marketing and requests" [S40]; a wrong entry propagates.
The twist
"It turns out the broker moved the date and didn't tell us." "Then I'd ask the broker for a confirmation email whenever a date changes, and I'd add a weekly check of upcoming events against broker calendars."
D2. Two hours before a PM strategy session you find the meeting counts in the report are wrongMedium
The trap
Sending it anyway and hoping nobody checks. Or hiding for two hours trying to fix everything.
Say
"Tell my associate immediately, with what's wrong and my best estimate of how long a fix takes. Then find the cause: usually duplicates, a date filter or attendance not logged. If I can fix it in time, I re-run it and reconcile totals against the raw data before it goes anywhere. If not, we send the report with the affected numbers clearly marked and a corrected version after. A wrong number in front of a PM is worse than a late one."
Why it works
Disclose first, then fix, then reconcile. The job asks for "strong attention to detail" and the ability to "manipulate data" [S40]; the mature version of both is knowing a number is wrong before the client does.
The twist
"The associate says just send it, the PM won't notice." Hold politely: "I'd rather mark the two numbers I'm not sure of. If he does notice, the whole report loses credibility, and it's the desk's name on it."
D3. A PM asks you to export another team's meeting history from the portal "to see what good coverage looks like"Hard
The trap
It sounds like a learning request. It is another team's research trail.
Say
"I'd say I can't share another team's data, but I can do something better: show him his own team's coverage against an anonymised benchmark, like average meetings per analyst across the fundamental teams, if the desk produces one, or build him a plan from the sector calendar. If he insisted on the specific team, I'd take it to my associate rather than refuse on my own authority."
Why it works
Same principle as B3: the centre aggregates everything and attributes nothing across the wall. Offering an aggregate or a plan gives the PM what he actually needs. Escalating a persistent request is right; an intern should not be the one arguing with a PM about data rights.
The twist
"He says the other PM is his friend and wouldn't mind." "Then the other PM can send it to him directly. It still shouldn't come from the desk."

E. Booking, confirming, invites

"Coordinate meetings by booking and confirming rooms and attendees, managing meeting requests from Portfolio Managers and Analysts, and sending calendar invites for Corporate Access meetings and events in EMEA." [S40]
E1. Ten minutes before a management meeting, the room you booked is occupiedEasy
The trap
Arguing with the people in the room, or panicking in front of the visiting company.
Say
"Find another room first, argue later. I'd grab the nearest free room or ask reception, update the attendees with one message, and meet the company at reception myself so they never see the scramble. Afterwards I'd find out why the clash happened and confirm rooms the morning of every meeting from then on."
Why it works
The company's experience is the firm's reputation with that IR team; Millennium's Tokyo posting describes the team as "a bridge, creating direct dialogue between companies and our investment teams" [S49]. Solve, communicate, protect the guest, then prevent.
The twist
"There are no free rooms." "Then a video link from the PM's desk, and I'd tell the PM before the company arrives so he chooses, not me."
E2. The CFO has arrived. The PM who requested the meeting is not answeringMedium
The trap
Leaving the CFO alone while you hunt, or letting the meeting collapse.
Say
"Host first: coffee, small talk, a colleague from the desk if I have to leave. In parallel, message and call the PM and his analysts; an analyst can start the meeting. If after five minutes nobody is coming, I'd ask my associate whether another team that had requested the company could take the slot, and I'd be honest with the CFO that we're rearranging attendees. What I wouldn't do is leave a CFO in a room with no one."
Why it works
You protect the relationship with the company, use the team hierarchy (analysts can attend), and only reallocate through the associate, because giving a slot to another team is an allocation decision, not a logistics one.
The twist
"The PM turns up 20 minutes late and is angry the meeting started without him." "I'd take it privately, explain the choice and ask how he'd want it handled next time. I wouldn't apologise for keeping the CFO's time respected."
E3. You double-booked a CEO: two teams both think they have the 10:00 one-on-oneHard
The trap
Choosing between the teams yourself. And hiding it until 9:55.
Say
"The moment I see it, I tell my associate: my error, two teams, one slot, here are the options. Options: ask the company for a second slot or a small-group format; offer one team a call later; or have the associate decide who keeps it under whatever rule the desk uses. I'd deliver the bad news to the team that loses myself, with the alternative already arranged, and I'd fix the checking step that let it happen."
Why it works
Disclose fast, generate options, escalate the allocation, own the message, prevent. The allocation is a level-2 decision and an intern taking it alone would be the real error. This scenario tests whether you hide mistakes; every platform's culture punishes hiding more than erring.
The twist
"Both PMs say they were first." "Then the record decides, not the argument. I'd pull the request timestamps from the portal and give them to my associate. That's what the log is for."

F. Conference requests and allocations

"Proactively support conference attendance strategy by helping facilitate a high volume of company meeting requests and allocations through efficient and reliable processes." [S40]
F1. A broker conference: 400 requests from 12 teams, and the broker gives the firm 60 one-on-one slotsEasy
The trap
Describing it as an email problem. It is a data and rules problem.
Say
"One sheet, one row per request: team, requester, company, their priority rank, reason, last time they met the company, and whether a group meeting would do. Then rank at firm level using the desk's criteria, submit one prioritised list to the broker, and when the 60 come back allocate them and offer group slots, calls or the next conference to the rest. Every step in the portal so it's the record."
Why it works
Brokers allocate to firms that send clean, ranked lists; requests carry "portfolio team, AUM relevance, and prior coverage" so allocators have context [S28]. You have described the desk's core process without pretending to know its private criteria.
The twist
"What criteria would you rank on?" "I don't know the desk's; I'd expect relevance of coverage, existing position or research interest, whether they've met the company recently, and the PM's own ranking. I'd want to learn the actual rule before touching a real allocation."
F2. Ten teams want the same CFO. The broker gives us four slots.Medium
The trap
First-come-first-served, or giving it to whoever complains. Both are rules you could not defend to the losers.
Say
"Apply the desk's ranking and stretch the supply: ask the broker for a small-group slot as well as one-on-ones, so three or four teams share one; ask the company's IR for a follow-up call for the rest; and tell every team that missed out what they're getting instead, before they hear it from someone else. Nobody is told who got the one-on-ones."
Why it works
Multi-manager desks stretch access with "multiple levels (IR, CFOs, division heads)" and group formats [S10]. Fairness is a rule you could explain to the loser; confidentiality is not telling the loser who won. This is the medium version of the Part 3 centrepiece.
The twist
"One team says its position is ten times bigger than anyone else's." "That may well be a factor in the ranking, and I'd pass it to whoever ranks. It isn't something I'd act on alone, and I wouldn't confirm or deny anyone else's position."

G. Relationships with brokers

"Build and maintain relationships with brokers to support smooth and effective communication." [S40]
G1. Your first call with a broker's corporate access desk. What do you want from it?Easy
The trap
Treating them as a supplier to be squeezed, or as a friend to be pleased.
Say
"Three things. How they want requests: format, deadlines, one list or many. What's coming: their conference calendar for the next quarter so I can market early. And how they see us: what makes Millennium easy or hard to work with. I'd come out of it with their process written down and a standing time to catch up."
Why it works
Broker desks handle hundreds of clients; the firm that is easiest to serve gets served first [S28][S30]. You are building a working relationship, not a social one, and you are gathering the calendar that drives everything else.
The twist
"They ask what Millennium's total budget with them is." "Not something I'd know or share; I'd say the broker vote is where that's decided and I'd pass any commercial question to my associate."
G2. A broker asks which of your teams wants the meeting, "so we can prioritise the right people"Medium
The trap
Naming the team or the PM. The desk exists so that the firm speaks with one voice.
Say
"I'd give them what helps them without exposing the team: the number of teams interested, the sector, the format we'd accept, and that this is a firm priority. That's what they need to allocate. Which PM it is stays inside. If they push, I'd say the desk submits on behalf of the firm and that's how every request comes."
Why it works
The desk "centralize[s] the access provided by sell-side brokers on behalf of our Portfolio Managers" [S42]. One voice out is the point; leaking which pod wants a company tells the market something about that pod.
The twist
"The broker says other firms tell them." "That's their choice. Ours is to aggregate. I'd keep it friendly and move on to what they can do for us."
G3. A broker offers you two tickets to a sold-out event "as a thank you for being so responsive"Hard
The trap
Accepting because it is small, or refusing rudely and damaging the relationship.
Say
"Thank them warmly and say I'd need to check it with compliance first, because gifts from brokers are exactly the thing the rules are strict about. Then actually check, and follow whatever the policy says. I'd tell my associate it was offered regardless of the outcome. The relationship survives a polite 'I have to clear that'; it doesn't survive a policy breach."
Why it works
Inducements from brokers are regulated in the UK [S15]; the firm's compliance runs due diligence on "sell-side experts" and a gifts perimeter is standard [S389]. Warm plus firm is the frame. Disclosing the offer even if declined shows you understand why the rule exists.
The twist
"It's only £60 of value." "Then compliance will probably say yes in ten seconds. It's still their call, not mine."

H. Consumption reports for PM strategy sessions

"Assist with preparing Corporate Access consumption reports for strategy sessions with Portfolio Managers and Analysts." [S40]
H1. What is a consumption report and what goes in it?Easy
The trap
"A list of meetings." It is a decision tool.
Say
"A record of what a team used: meetings attended by company, sector, format and broker; requests made and not filled and why; and how that compares with the team's coverage. It's used two ways: with the PM, to plan next quarter and fix gaps; and by the desk, to score brokers in the semi-annual vote and to show the firm what access it paid for."
Why it works
Industry-wide, one-on-one company meetings are the most tracked item in broker votes [S39]; Millennium's desk runs a "semi-annual Corporate Access Broker Vote" [S43]. You have shown the report is both client service and compliance record.
The twist
"Why does the firm need to prove what access it paid for?" "Because since 2014 UK managers can't pay for access with dealing commission, and under MiFID II it's a separately paid service, so there has to be a record of what was received and how it was paid for" [S2][S13].
H2. The report says a team attended 40 meetings; the broker's records say 55Medium
The trap
Picking one number. Both are probably partly right.
Say
"Reconcile line by line before the session. The usual causes: meetings the broker arranged directly with the PM that never hit our log; group meetings counted per attendee by one side and per meeting by the other; cancellations. I'd list the 15 differences, classify them, fix the log where we missed something, and tell the associate which broker relationship has a leak in it. Then the process fix: brokers copy the desk on every confirmation."
Why it works
You treat the discrepancy as information about the process, not as an embarrassment. Every unlogged meeting is a compliance gap and a broker-vote error at once.
The twist
"You have 30 minutes." "Then I reconcile the largest differences, mark the rest as unreconciled on the report, and finish after the session. Marked uncertainty is fine; hidden uncertainty is not."
H3. In the strategy session the PM says your report is wrong and his team met far more companiesHard
The trap
Defending the report in the room, or caving and agreeing it is wrong without evidence.
Say
"'You may well be right; the report only shows what reached the desk. Can you give me two examples and I'll trace them today?' Then I'd trace them. If they were meetings arranged around the desk, the conversation becomes how we capture them, which helps him in the broker vote too. If the report was wrong, I'd correct it and say so to everyone who saw it."
Why it works
Neither defend nor cave: ask for the specific, commit to a time, and turn the dispute into a process improvement. The PM keeps face, the record gets better, and you have shown you can be challenged by a senior person without wobbling.
The twist
"He says 'this is why corporate access is useless'." Back to A3. Don't defend the desk; get the data and come back one-to-one.

Part 3

Under fire: the composure set

Five scenarios designed to break you. In each, the pressure is the point and the answer is the same four steps at a slower tempo. Two decision trees you can draw from memory.

P1. Two PMs are shouting at you over the same one-on-one slot, five minutes before it startsHard
What they test
Tempo, hierarchy, confidentiality, and whether you decide above your level under pressure.
Say
"I'd slow down, not speed up. Same volume, lower pitch. 'I hear you both. Give me two minutes and I'll come back with an answer.' Then three things at once: check the record for who was confirmed; ask the company's IR or the broker if the slot can take two attendees or a second slot after; and get my associate on the phone, because who keeps a contested slot is the desk's decision, not mine. I'd come back with the answer and an alternative for whoever doesn't get it, delivered to each PM separately. I would not say to either of them what the other one wanted or why."
Why it works
You control tempo, you buy time honestly, you use the record (the portal exists for this [S40]), you stretch the supply (small groups are how desks stretch access [S10]), you escalate the allocation, and you protect the wall between teams [S313]. Two shouting PMs are a normal Tuesday on a platform where teams compete for everything; the desk is the calm part.
The twists, in order
"Your associate isn't answering." Then the record decides: "I'd go with the confirmed booking and offer the other PM the first alternative I've secured, and tell the associate what I did the moment they're back."
"The PM who loses says he'll make sure you don't get a return offer." Do not react to it. "I'd say I understand he's frustrated, and that I'll get him the best alternative I can. I'd mention the comment to my associate afterwards, factually, and leave it there."
"The winner asks why the other guy wanted it." Same answer as B3: warm, no.

Figure 5. Two PMs, one slot. Record first, escalate the contested call, stretch the supply, protect the wall.
P2. A PM tells you to send a request straight to a company's IR team "and don't bother compliance, it's just a call"Hard
What they test
Whether level 1 of your priority order survives a direct instruction from a senior person.
Say
"I'd do the request, because setting up a call with IR is normal, and I'd log it the way every request is logged, because that's not optional and it protects him as much as the firm. I wouldn't announce that I was 'going to compliance'; the log is the process. If he explicitly told me not to record it, I'd stop and tell my associate the same hour. I'd be polite about it and I wouldn't argue with him on the floor."
Why it works
You separate the legitimate request (a call with IR) from the illegitimate instruction (skip the record). The record is why the FCA and the firm can show access was handled properly [S2][S22]; a control room runs the restricted list and wall-crossings and the log is how it knows what happened [S389]. Refusing to be told to skip it, while still serving the PM, is the frame.
The twist
"He says everyone does it." "Then it'll be easy for my associate to confirm that. I'd still log it."
P3. You are in the room taking notes. The CFO says "we'll be announcing something on the debt side next week that the market will like"Hard
What they test
Whether you recognise possible inside information and know that it is not your call to judge it.
Say
"I wouldn't react in the room; that's not my meeting to run. Straight after, I'd tell my associate exactly what was said, word for word, who was present, and the time, and let compliance decide whether it's inside information and whether the name needs restricting. I wouldn't discuss it with anyone else, and I wouldn't decide myself that it was fine because it was vague."
Why it works
The Greenlight case: a CEO on a broker-arranged call said the company was at an advanced stage toward an equity raise; the fund traded and was fined over £7m, and "conceptual" vagueness was no defence [S19]. The FCA's good practice is an initial contact point "independent of the fund managers" [S20]. The desk's meeting log is how the firm proves who heard what and when [S22]. Escalate fast, precisely, and only upward.
The twist
"The PM says it's fine, it's public knowledge." "It may be. Compliance can confirm that in minutes, and then nobody has a problem. If I stay quiet and it wasn't, everyone in the room has one."

P4. An interviewer says: "Honestly, this is an admin job. Why would someone with your background want it?"Hard
What they test
Whether you are defensive about the seat, and whether you understand what it actually is.
Say
"Part of it is admin, and I'd want to be very good at that part, because the record is the compliance evidence and the broker-vote evidence. But the seat is the one place where the demand of hundreds of independent teams for the same companies becomes visible, and someone has to turn that into one voice to the market and a fair allocation back. That's a judgement job wearing an admin job's clothes. I'd rather learn the firm from the desk that touches every team than from one team."
Why it works
You agree with the true part, reframe the rest with the mechanism, and end on why it suits you. No defensiveness, no grandiosity. The reframe is drawn from the earlier guides: the desk centralises access for the firm [S42] and its record feeds compliance and the vote [S43][S22].
The twist
"So you think you're too good for the admin?" "No. I think the admin is where the trust is earned. Nobody lets an intern near the allocation until the rooms, the invites and the log are perfect."
P5. "It's 18:30, a conference deadline is 19:00, your associate has gone home, and a PM's request has a field you don't understand."Hard
What they test
Judgement with incomplete information, and whether you know the difference between reversible and irreversible.
Say
"Submit the request with everything I'm sure of, flag the uncertain field to the broker as 'to confirm', and message the PM and my associate saying exactly what I submitted and what I wasn't sure of. Missing the deadline is irreversible; a field I can correct tomorrow is not. What I wouldn't do is guess the field silently or let the deadline pass waiting for an answer."
Why it works
Take the reversible step, flag the assumption, tell everyone what you did. Booking windows fill fast and are not reopened [S30]. "I'd wait for full information" is the answer that fails.
The twist
"The field was 'confidential: do not submit'." "Then the flag saved it: the broker would have seen 'to confirm', not the content. And I'd learn what that field means the next morning. If I had submitted content I shouldn't have, I'd tell the associate first thing, not wait to be asked."

Part 4

The CV grill

They will pick at your CV to see whether you know your own story and whether you inflate it. Same rule: the frame is honesty plus structure.

4.1 How a CV grill works

They choose one line and go three levels deep: what did you do, what exactly was your part, what would you do differently. Then they look for a gap between the bullet and the answers. A candidate who wrote "led a team" and then describes attending meetings has broken their own frame. The defence is to know, for every line, the concrete thing you did, one number, one difficulty, and one honest limitation.

The attackThe frame-holding answer
"Walk me through this line. What did you do, not the team?"Name your specific task in one sentence, then one number, then one thing that went wrong and what you did. Never say "we" when the question was "you".
"You have no finance experience. Why should we believe you understand this?"Agree, then show the work: "I don't have it. So I read the firm's postings, the FCA's 2014 rules on corporate access and how the platform model works, and I can tell you what the desk does and why it sits where it does." Then one sentence of it.
"Why this and not investment banking or consulting?"A reason that is specific to the seat: touching every team, the allocation judgement, being at the centre of a platform. Never "I couldn't get IB" and never criticise the alternative.
"This society role looks inflated.""You're right that the title sounds bigger than the job. What I actually did was X, which was about Y hours a week. The useful part for this role was Z." Deflating your own CV calmly is a strong signal.
"What's your biggest weakness?" then "That's not a real weakness."Give a real one that is not disqualifying (over-preparing, slow to delegate, uncomfortable with silence), one example, one thing you are doing about it. If they push, hold it; do not swap to a fake one.
"Tell me something you failed at." then "Whose fault was it?"Own the part that was yours, even if not all of it was. "Mostly mine: I assumed X and didn't check." Blaming is a frame break.
"Why did you leave / stop / switch?"One neutral sentence, forward-looking. Never a story about a bad boss.
"What do you actually know about Millennium?"Three mechanisms, not adjectives: 350-plus independent teams and why they are kept apart; a centre bigger than the investment side; capital investors cannot pull quickly. Then stop. (See the Why Millennium guide.)
Prepare this tonight, for every line on your CV

Did: the one concrete thing you personally did. Number: one figure (people, hours, money, count). Hard part: one difficulty and how you handled it. Honest limit: one thing you would do better. Four lines per bullet, on one page. If a bullet cannot produce four lines, expect it to be the one they attack, and soften it in your telling before they do.

Don't

Do not upgrade any claim in the room. Do not guess dates or numbers you are not sure of; say "roughly" and give the range. Do not fill silence with a second, weaker version of your answer. Do not badmouth a previous employer, society, university or colleague, however invited.
