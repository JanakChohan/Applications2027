The whole report in ten sentences

  1. Alopecia areata is your own immune system mistakenly attacking your hair follicles — the follicles are switched off, not destroyed, which is why regrowth stays possible even after years.
  2. The attack runs on a self-reinforcing loop between two chemical messengers, interferon-gamma and interleukin-15.
  3. Ritlecitinib cuts that loop in two places at once: it permanently disables an enzyme called JAK3, and a second family of enzymes called TEC kinases.
  4. In the main trial, 23% of people reached "20% or less scalp hair loss" by week 24 — and 43% by week 48. The drug keeps working the longer you take it.
  5. By three years, around 65% of people still on treatment had reached that target, and about 31% had a completely full head of hair.
  6. Roughly one in ten responders only responded after a full year — so the biggest mistake you can make is quitting early.
  7. The most common side effects are headache, diarrhoea, acne and a mild rash. Serious events are rare: about 1 shingles case per 100 people per year of treatment.
  8. The scary boxed warning on the label comes from a different drug in older, sicker patients; your personal cardiovascular risk profile is close to the floor.
  9. Oral minoxidil works by a completely separate route — it grows hair, it does not calm immunity — so it complements rather than duplicates ritlecitinib, and there is no reason found in the evidence to stop it unless your dermatologist advises otherwise.
  10. Your NHS decision point is week 36: that is when your dermatologist formally reviews whether it is working, so plan the whole first nine months around getting a clean, well-documented answer.
Section 1

How hair actually grows

Before you can understand what has gone wrong, you need one picture in your head: a hair follicle is not a static tube that pushes hair out forever. It is a small organ that runs on a repeating three-phase cycle, and it is the cycle — not the follicle — that alopecia areata attacks.

You have roughly 100,000 hair follicles on your scalp. Each one behaves independently, which is why you do not moult all at once. At any given moment about 85–90% of them are actively growing, around 1% are shutting down, and the rest are resting. A follicle that has finished a cycle does not die; it waits, then starts again. That single fact is the reason people who have had alopecia areata for a decade can still regrow a full head of hair.

In plain English

Think of each follicle as a factory on a long production run. It builds hair for years, then shuts down for a service, rests, and reopens. Alopecia areata does not burn the factory down — it repeatedly forces an emergency shutdown mid-run. Stop the thing forcing the shutdowns and the factory reopens on its own.

Two words you will hear constantly

Vellus hair

The fine, short, pale, almost invisible hair — "peach fuzz". When regrowth starts, this is what appears first. It is a very good sign and it is easy to miss unless you look in bright light at a low angle.

Terminal hair

Thick, long, pigmented, normal scalp hair. Vellus hair matures into terminal hair over weeks to months as the follicle regains full size. The SALT score your dermatologist records only really moves once this happens.

This vellus-then-terminal sequence is why months 2–4 can feel like nothing is happening while a great deal is happening. Regrowth may also come back white or grey at first, because the pigment-making cells take longer to switch back on than the hair-making cells. It usually re-pigments over subsequent cycles.

Section 2

What alopecia areata is doing to you

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune disease: your immune system has misidentified part of your own hair follicle as foreign and is attacking it. Understanding how that attack sustains itself is the key to understanding why ritlecitinib is aimed where it is aimed.

Step one: your follicles have a security system, and it fails

The growing part of a hair follicle is one of a handful of places in the human body with immune privilege — a protected zone the immune system is deliberately kept out of. (The others include the eye, the brain and the testes.) The follicle maintains this in two ways: it hides its identity badges, and it puts out chemical "stand down" signals.

How a healthy follicle keeps the immune system out
MechanismWhat it actually does
Very low MHC class I and IIMHC molecules are the "identity badges" every cell wears so immune cells can inspect them. The anagen bulb wears almost none — so patrolling T-cells have nothing to inspect and walk past.
Immunosuppressive signalsThe follicle actively secretes calming molecules — MIF, TGF-β1 and β2, and α-MSH (from POMC) — that switch off nearby immune cells.
CD200 "do not attack" flagA surface protein that directly tells immune cells to stand down.

Something — and in most people we genuinely do not know what: a viral illness, a period of severe stress, another autoimmune flare, or simply a genetically pre-loaded system tipping over — causes this privilege to collapse. The follicle starts displaying MHC badges. Immune cells that had been ignoring it for 21 years suddenly see a target.

Step two: the loop that keeps it going

This is the single most important diagram in the report, because it is exactly where your new medicine intervenes. The attack is not a one-off event — it is a self-feeding loop between two chemical messengers (cytokines):

  • Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) — released by the attacking T-cells. It is the signal that forces the follicle to display MHC badges in the first place.
  • Interleukin-15 (IL-15) — released by the follicle cells in response to IFN-γ. IL-15 is food and fuel for the very T-cells attacking it.

So: T-cells make IFN-γ → the follicle responds by making IL-15 → IL-15 feeds the T-cells → they make more IFN-γ. Once this wheel is turning it sustains itself indefinitely. The follicle is, in effect, being tricked into paying for its own attackers.

In plain English

Imagine a fire that keeps itself alight because the burning building keeps producing its own fuel. Removing the original spark (whatever triggered your first patch four years ago) achieves nothing now — the fire has moved on from needing it. You have to interrupt the fuel supply. That is what your medicine does.

Why yours comes back: the relapsing–remitting pattern

You have described a pattern that will be immediately familiar to any hair specialist: patches appear, regrow, and then a larger episode follows. This is the natural history of the disease, and it happens for a reason worth understanding.

Alopecia areata has a tissue memory. Some of the T-cells recruited during a flare do not disappear when the flare settles; they become resident memory T-cells that stay parked in the scalp skin. They are quiet, but they are primed. When conditions shift — an infection, a stress load, a change in your other immune activity — those resident cells can reactivate quickly and locally, which is why relapses often begin near where previous patches were and can escalate faster than the first episode did.

This has a direct practical implication that you should carry into every conversation with your dermatologist:

The single most important consequence

Ritlecitinib suppresses the attack; it does not delete the memory cells. If you stop the drug, the loop can restart. In the published data, regrown hair is typically lost after discontinuation, and UK guidance explicitly advises counselling patients before they start that this is likely to be a long-term, possibly life-long treatment. That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to plan for it, and to not treat month 12 as a finish line.

Where your family history fits

You mentioned a family history of autoimmune conditions. That is entirely consistent and worth being explicit about: alopecia areata is strongly heritable, and the genes involved are shared with other autoimmune diseases — in particular those governing how T-cells recognise targets (HLA region), how they are regulated (CTLA4, IL2/IL21), and the NKG2D ligand system that sits at the heart of Figure 2.2.

Practically, this means two things:

  • It is not your fault and it is not something you caused. Diet, stress and lifestyle can modulate an autoimmune disease; they do not create the underlying genetic predisposition.
  • It is worth screening for the conditions that travel with it, because they are more common in people with alopecia areata and their families. Ask specifically about thyroid function (autoimmune thyroid disease is the classic companion), coeliac serology, vitamin B12, and a note in your record about vitiligo and type 1 diabetes. These are cheap tests and Section 10 explains why they matter for regrowth as well as general health.

Other things this disease does that nobody warns you about

Nails

Fine pitting, ridging or roughness of the nail plate affects a meaningful minority. It reflects the same inflammation and — usefully — it tends to improve alongside the scalp on treatment.

Eyebrows & lashes

Often affected, often the most distressing part, and often the slowest to recover. Trials measure these separately for good reason. See §5.

Body hair

Beard, chest and limb hair can be involved. Beard involvement in men is common and sometimes precedes scalp change.

There is also a well-documented psychological load that men in particular tend to under-report. In a UK mixed-methods survey, over half of men with alopecia described depleted confidence, and wellbeing scores sat below matched population norms — yet help-seeking was strikingly low. If that resonates, treat it as a clinical finding rather than a character flaw; §10 and §11 both address it, and under UK guidance the psychological impact is a formally recognised part of how severity is defined.

Section 3

How ritlecitinib works

Ritlecitinib is a "kinase inhibitor". To understand what that means you need one more piece of biology — how a cell hears a message from outside — and then the drug's design becomes genuinely simple and rather elegant.

How a cell hears a message: the JAK-STAT relay

Cytokines like IL-15 are messages. But a message is useless unless it gets from the outside of a cell to the DNA in its nucleus, where the cell decides what to do. That journey is a four-step relay:

In plain English

A JAK is a relay operator sitting just inside the cell wall. Ritlecitinib walks up to one specific operator — JAK3 — and superglues its hands together. The phone still rings; nobody can answer it.

Why "JAK3 only" is the clever part

There are four JAK enzymes in humans: JAK1, JAK2, JAK3 and TYK2. They work in pairs, and different pairs carry different messages. This matters enormously for side effects:

EnzymeMessages it carriesWhat happens if you block it
JAK1Very broad — inflammation, interferons, many interleukinsPowerful anti-inflammatory effect, but broad immune suppression
JAK2Erythropoietin (red blood cells), thrombopoietin (platelets), growth hormoneAnaemia, low platelets, raised cholesterol — the classic JAK-inhibitor blood problems
JAK3Only the six "common gamma chain" cytokines: IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-9, IL-15, IL-21 — all of which are lymphocyte (T, B, NK cell) signalsTargeted effect on the exact cells driving alopecia areata. No effect on red cells or platelets via this route.
TYK2IL-12, IL-23, type I interferonsRelevant in psoriasis; not a main driver here

JAK3 is the narrowest of the four. It appears essentially only in immune cells, and it only ever partners with JAK1 on that one family of receptors. Blocking it is therefore a scalpel rather than a sledgehammer — and, critically, IL-15 is on that list. IL-15 is the fuel line in Figure 2.2.

The second target: TEC kinases

This is what makes ritlecitinib genuinely unusual. As well as JAK3, it irreversibly blocks a second family of five enzymes — BTK, BMX, ITK, RLK/TXK and TEC. These sit on a completely different signalling pathway.

The most relevant of these is ITK, which sits directly downstream of the T-cell receptor — the part of the T-cell that physically recognises a target and decides to fire. Blocking ITK dampens T-cell activation itself. The TEC family is also involved in the cytotoxic (killing) machinery of natural killer cells.

Cut #1 — JAK3

Starves the attack. IL-15 and IL-2 can no longer reach the T-cells that are attacking your follicles. Fewer are recruited, fewer survive, fewer multiply.

Analogy: cutting the enemy's supply lines.

Cut #2 — TEC / ITK

Disarms the attack. The T-cells that are already in your scalp — including those resident memory cells from §2 — have their activation and killing machinery turned down.

Analogy: jamming their triggers.

"Irreversible" and "covalent" — why those words are good news

Most drugs bind their target loosely and let go; keeping the effect going means keeping a high drug level in the blood at all times. Ritlecitinib does something different. It forms a permanent chemical bond with a single sulphur atom — a cysteine residue numbered 909 — in JAK3's structure. That specific cysteine is not present in JAK1, JAK2 or TYK2, which is precisely why the drug can be so selective.

Two practical consequences:

  • The effect outlasts the drug. Ritlecitinib is cleared from your bloodstream quite quickly, but the JAK3 enzymes it has disabled stay disabled until the cell manufactures fresh ones. This is why once-daily dosing works despite a short half-life, and why a single missed dose is not a catastrophe.
  • It is not a "level in the blood" drug. You do not need to obsess about taking it at exactly the same minute each day. Consistency matters for habit, not pharmacology.

What this looks like in your scalp, week by week

A note on the loading dose

You may read about a "200 mg loading dose for 4 weeks" in the trial literature. Several ALLEGRO studies used that, but the licensed dose in the UK and US is simply 50 mg once daily with no loading dose, because the trials showed the loading dose did not add meaningful benefit. If you have been prescribed plain 50 mg daily, that is correct and current.

Section 4

Measuring hair loss: the SALT score

Every trial result, every NHS funding decision and every review appointment revolves around one number. If you learn nothing else administrative, learn this one — it is the number that decides whether your treatment continues.

SALT stands for Severity of Alopecia Tool. It is simply the percentage of your scalp with hair missing. SALT 0 means no loss at all. SALT 100 means a completely bare scalp. It is calculated by dividing the scalp into four regions, scoring the percentage loss in each, and weighting them by how much of your total scalp surface they represent.

The thresholds that matter

ThresholdMeansWhy it exists
SALT ≤ 2080% or more of your scalp hair is presentThe primary endpoint of every major trial and the UK continuation target. It is roughly the point at which hair loss stops being obvious to other people.
SALT ≤ 1090% or more presentA stricter secondary endpoint. Close to cosmetically normal.
SALT = 0Complete regrowthThe best possible outcome, and achieved by a meaningful minority on long-term treatment.
SALT ≥ 50Half or more of the scalp bareThe definition of severe alopecia areata, and the standard NHS funding threshold (see §8).

Two related terms

Alopecia totalis (AT)

Complete loss of scalp hair — SALT 100.

Alopecia universalis (AU)

Complete loss of scalp and all body hair, including eyebrows and lashes.

These matter because response rates are meaningfully lower in AT/AU than in patchy disease. From what you have described — patches that enlarge and regrow — you are not in the AT/AU category, and that is genuinely good news for your odds. See §6.

What the evidence does not tell us

  • Whether combining ritlecitinib with oral minoxidil is better than ritlecitinib alone. No randomised trial has tested it. §9 covers what we do know.
  • How to safely come off it. There is no established tapering protocol. Real-world data on the related drug baricitinib found that everyone who stopped relapsed, at an average of about 15 weeks, and that halving the dose led to relapse in half of patients at an average of 24 weeks.
  • Very long-term safety beyond about five to six years. Data now extend to roughly 5–6 years with no new signals, but this is a genuinely new drug.
  • Whether it works better started early in a flare. Strongly suggested by the severity and duration findings, but never tested head-to-head.
  • Much about men specifically. Nearly two-thirds of the pivotal trial were women, and male sex was associated with poorer response — but no trial was designed to answer why, or by how much. See §6.
Section 6

Your personal odds

Researchers have repeatedly analysed which characteristics predict who responds. Here is every published predictor, mapped honestly against what you have told me about yourself — including the ones that do not go your way.

Before you read the scorecard

Predictors describe averages across large groups. They cannot tell you what will happen to you. People with every unfavourable factor regrow completely, and people with every favourable factor do not respond at all. Use this to calibrate patience and expectations — not to predict your future.

So what should you actually expect?

Nobody can give you a personal percentage, and anyone who does is guessing. But here is a defensible way to frame it.

You share the profile of the group the trials called early-to-middle responders: young, JAK-naïve, patchy rather than total loss, and a relatively short current episode. Those characteristics track with the upper part of the response distribution. Working against that, you are male and an adult rather than an adolescent, and adult real-world 24-week figures are more modest than the headline.

A reasonable, honest expectation:

  • By week 12: quite possibly no visible change, or fine vellus hair in the patches. Any active shedding should have stopped. This is a normal, on-track result.
  • By week 24: somewhere between "clearly filling in" and "modest but definite regrowth". Roughly a quarter of trial participants had already hit the SALT ≤20 target here; your profile suggests you sit at or above that average.
  • By week 36 — the NHS review point: the great majority of people who will eventually respond show some terminal regrowth by now. This is the number that matters administratively.
  • By month 12–24: this is where most of the gains land. Across the pooled data roughly two-thirds of people still on treatment reached the target, and around a third eventually reached a fully covered scalp.
  • Eyebrows and lashes: expect these to lag the scalp by weeks to months if they are involved.

The three things that would most change these odds — all of them yours to control

  1. Not stopping. More than one in ten responders crossed the line only after a year. Every month of continuous treatment measurably increases your cumulative chance. This is by far the biggest lever you hold.
  2. Not letting a gap open up. Breaks under six weeks are unlikely to cost you hair; beyond that the risk of losing regrowth rises with the length of the gap. Repeat prescriptions ordered early, not on the last tablet.
  3. Getting an accurate, documented SALT score at every visit. Your continuation past week 36 depends on demonstrable regrowth. Undocumented improvement is, administratively, no improvement. §10 explains exactly how to photograph this.

If it does not work

You should know the ladder ahead of time, because knowing there is one takes a lot of pressure off the next nine months:

  • Extend before abandoning. UK guidance explicitly allows a further three months beyond week 36 if there is terminal regrowth but the SALT ≤20 target has not been met.
  • Switch JAK inhibitor. Baricitinib (JAK1/2) works through an overlapping but different mechanism, and published series show switching between JAK inhibitors can succeed after failure on the first.
  • Adjunct therapies. Intralesional or topical corticosteroids for stubborn individual patches, and continued oral minoxidil, are commonly used alongside.
  • Newer agents. Deuruxolitinib and others are progressing; the pipeline in this field is unusually active right now.

Level 3: that alarming boxed warning

Your patient information leaflet carries a black-bordered warning listing serious infections, mortality, malignancy, major adverse cardiovascular events and thrombosis. It is genuinely frightening to read. Here is what it actually is.

In 2021 a large safety trial called ORAL Surveillance compared a different JAK inhibitor (tofacitinib) against older drugs in people with rheumatoid arthritis who were aged 50 or over and had at least one cardiovascular risk factor. It found higher rates of cardiac events, cancers and death in the JAK inhibitor group. Regulators responded by applying a class-wide warning to every drug in the family — including ones not yet studied in those populations, in different diseases, at different doses.

What this means for you specifically

The warning on your leaflet is extrapolated, not observed. It describes a population you could hardly be less similar to. You are 21, not 50+. You do not smoke. You do not drink. You are lean, active and have no cardiovascular risk factors. You have alopecia areata, not rheumatoid arthritis — a disease that does not itself carry the inflammatory cardiovascular burden RA does. And ritlecitinib's own observed rate of major cardiac events across 2,092 patient-years was 0.14 per 100 patient-years.

This is not a reason to dismiss the warning. It is a reason to understand that it is a ceiling estimate for the worst-case population, and that your position is close to the floor.

Reassuring

Section 8

Monitoring, tests and the UK pathway

Because you are in the UK, your treatment runs on a defined national framework — NICE Technology Appraisal TA958, plus supplementary professional guidance from the British Association of Dermatologists and the British Hair and Nail Society. Knowing this framework is genuinely useful: it tells you what has to be documented and when.

How you qualify

NICE recommends ritlecitinib for severe alopecia areata in people aged 12 and over. The Scottish Medicines Consortium has accepted it for NHS Scotland. It must be started by a consultant dermatologist, and approval runs through the Blueteq system.

"Severe" is defined in one of two ways:

Route 1 — extent

An absolute SALT score of 50 or more — at least half the scalp affected.

Route 2 — extent plus impact

SALT 21–49 plus at least one additional factor:

  • negative impact on psychological functioning
  • noticeable eyebrow or eyelash involvement
  • inadequate response after at least 6 months of other treatment
  • a diffuse positive hair pull test indicating rapidly progressive disease
A point in your favour

UK guidance explicitly states that disease duration should not be used to restrict access to ritlecitinib — the committee noted that people excluded from trials on duration grounds have nonetheless regrown hair in practice. Your four-year history is not a barrier.

Section 9

Oral minoxidil: keep it or stop it?

You asked whether the two work together, and whether you should stop the minoxidil. This is a genuinely good question, and the short answer is: they do two completely different jobs, the theoretical case for combining them is strong, the trial evidence is thin, and there is no pharmacological reason found in the literature to stop — but the decision is your dermatologist's to confirm.

They are not the same kind of drug at all

This is the crux of it. Ritlecitinib is an immune drug: it stops something bad from happening. Minoxidil is a growth drug: it makes something good happen faster. Neither substitutes for the other.

In plain English

Your follicles are like a car with someone standing on the brake. Ritlecitinib gets that person off the brake. Minoxidil presses the accelerator. Doing both moves you faster than doing either — but pressing the accelerator alone, while someone is still on the brake, achieves very little. That last point is probably why minoxidil alone has never been enough for you.

What the evidence actually shows about minoxidil in alopecia areata

  • As a solo treatment for alopecia areata, it is weak. Systematic reviews and scoping reviews find case series and observational benefit, but randomised trial data in alopecia areata specifically remain limited. This is entirely expected given the mechanism — it does nothing about the autoimmune attack.
  • Expert consensus does list alopecia areata among the conditions where low-dose oral minoxidil may be beneficial, alongside androgenetic alopecia, telogen effluvium, lichen planopilaris and chemotherapy-induced alopecia.
  • Typical dosing for men in international Delphi consensus is 2.5 mg/day as a starting point, with a usual range of 1.25–5 mg/day.

What the evidence shows about combining it with a JAK inhibitor

A 2026 systematic review in the International Journal of Dermatology looked specifically at combining low-dose oral minoxidil with immunomodulators — including JAK inhibitors — in alopecia areata. The literature's working hypothesis is that low-dose oral minoxidil may have an additive effect alongside a JAK inhibitor and may reduce the need to escalate the JAK inhibitor dose. Supporting this, a published series combining tofacitinib with oral minoxidil in 12 people with severe alopecia areata reported that 8 achieved at least 75% scalp regrowth.

Be honest about the strength of this evidence

There is no randomised trial of ritlecitinib plus oral minoxidil versus ritlecitinib alone. The combination evidence is small, observational, and mostly involves different JAK inhibitors. Anyone who tells you the combination is definitely better is going beyond what has been shown. What can be said fairly is: the mechanisms are complementary, the observational signal is favourable, and no safety interaction has been identified.

So — continue or stop?

Here is the case each way, laid out honestly. The decision is your dermatologist's, but you should walk into that conversation with the arguments.

Reasons to continue

  • Different mechanism — no duplication, no redundancy.
  • No documented drug interaction with ritlecitinib. Minoxidil is not a significant player in the CYP enzyme interactions listed for ritlecitinib.
  • Plausible additive benefit, supported by systematic review and case series.
  • Stopping risks a shed. Minoxidil-dependent hair can be lost when it is withdrawn, typically over 2–3 months. Losing hair right at the point you are trying to demonstrate regrowth before week 36 would be a genuinely bad outcome.
  • It may accelerate the visible result by helping vellus hair mature into terminal hair faster — which is exactly what the week 36 assessment measures.
  • You are already established on it and tolerating it.

Reasons a clinician might stop it

  • Attribution. With two drugs running, you cannot tell which one produced the result. Some clinicians prefer a clean read on the new drug.
  • Overlapping side effects. Both can cause headache and dizziness; if either becomes a problem, it is unclear which to blame.
  • Unwanted hair. Minoxidil causes hypertrichosis in roughly 15–19% of people — hair growth on the face, arms and back. If ritlecitinib works well on your scalp, you may find you no longer want that trade-off.
  • Fewer drugs is simpler, and if ritlecitinib alone achieves SALT 0, minoxidil is arguably redundant.
  • Any individual cardiovascular concern, fluid retention or palpitations.
A reasonable position to take into the conversation

On the balance of what is published, there is a stronger case for continuing the oral minoxidil through at least the week 36 assessment than for stopping it now. The mechanisms are complementary, the safety signal for the combination is unremarkable, and the downside of a withdrawal shed lands at precisely the wrong moment in your NHS pathway. If you and your dermatologist later want a clean read on the ritlecitinib alone, the logical time to consider reducing or stopping minoxidil is after you have a stable, documented response — not before it.

The rule that matters more than the decision itself: change one thing at a time. If you stop the minoxidil in the same month you start the ritlecitinib and something changes, you will never know which caused it.

Section 10

Maximising your chances

Everything below is within your control. They are ranked by how much difference they actually make — which is not the same as how much attention they usually get. The first three are worth more than everything else on this list combined.

Tier 1  The things that genuinely change outcomes

  1. Take it every single day, and do not stop early. This is the whole game. Over 27% of trial responders reached target only after week 24, and 10.5% only after a full year. Every additional month of continuous treatment adds to your cumulative chance. The most common way people fail this drug is quitting during the silent months. Set a daily phone alarm and pair the tablet with something you already do without fail — brushing your teeth, or your morning protein shake.
  2. Never let the prescription run out. Breaks under six weeks are unlikely to cost you regrowth; longer gaps increasingly are. Order repeats when you have two weeks left, not two days. Keep a small buffer supply if your clinic allows it. If you travel, take more than you need and keep it in hand luggage.
  3. Build the evidence for your week 36 review from day one. Continuation depends on documented regrowth. Do the photography protocol below, every four weeks, without fail, and ask for an absolute SALT score to be recorded at every clinic visit. Bring your photos to the review. This costs you ten minutes a month and protects your access to a treatment that may need a year to work.

Tier 2  Removing the obstacles

  1. Get the deficiency screen done and correct anything abnormal. None of these cure alopecia areata — be clear about that. But a follicle attempting to rebuild itself is doing an unusually demanding manufacturing job, and running it short of raw materials will limit what your regrowth can achieve. Ask for: ferritin (iron stores — aim well clear of the bottom of the range), vitamin D, thyroid function, vitamin B12 and folate, zinc, and coeliac serology. Vitamin D deficiency is documented in around half of people with alopecia areata, and low zinc is one of the most consistent findings across studies.
  2. Vitamin D deserves a specific mention for you. Deficiency is very common among South Asian people living in the UK — darker skin synthesises less vitamin D from limited British sunlight. The UK guidance for everyone is 10 micrograms (400 IU) daily through autumn and winter; people at higher risk are often advised to take it year-round. Get the level measured rather than guessing, and let your GP set the dose.
  3. Sort the vaccinations — Shingrix in particular (§8). This is the single most effective thing you can do about the most likely serious risk of this drug.
  4. Protect the CPK reading. No heavy or unaccustomed training in the 48–72 hours before a blood test, and tell the phlebotomist you train seriously (§7).
  5. Protect an exposed scalp from the sun. Skin that has not seen daylight in years burns easily, periodic skin checks are recommended on this drug, and non-melanoma skin cancer is on the monitoring list. A hat or a daily SPF on bare patches is cheap insurance.
  6. Tell every prescriber you see — GP, dentist, pharmacist, walk-in centre — that you take ritlecitinib. It interacts with CYP3A-cleared drugs and it changes how infections should be assessed.

Tier 3  Genuinely useful, but not magic

  1. Eat enough protein. Hair is almost entirely keratin — a protein. At 68 kg and training hard, a reasonable target is roughly 1.2–1.6 g of protein per kg per day, so about 82–110 g. If you follow a vegetarian Gujarati diet, this is very achievable — dal, chickpeas, paneer, yoghurt, soya, eggs if you eat them — but it does need attention rather than assumption, and vegetarian diets also warrant a closer eye on iron, B12 and zinc, all three of which matter for hair.
  2. Do not crash-diet or run a large calorie deficit while trying to regrow hair. Energy restriction pushes follicles into the resting phase — this is the mechanism behind telogen effluvium. Given you are lean and active already, eat at maintenance or above for the next year.
  3. Sleep 7–9 hours. Sleep is when immune regulation is recalibrated. The evidence linking sleep specifically to alopecia areata outcomes is thin, but the general immune evidence is not, and there is no downside.
  4. Take the stress side seriously. Stress is a recognised trigger and aggravator in genetically predisposed people, and the relationship runs both ways — hair loss drives distress, distress may drive hair loss. Whatever form it takes for you — training, prayer, therapy, time away from mirrors — treat it as part of the treatment plan rather than a soft extra.
  5. Handle new hair gently. New vellus and early terminal hair is fragile. Avoid harsh brushing, tight styling, hot tools and chemical treatments on regrowing areas for the first several months.
  6. Keep training. Nothing about ritlecitinib requires you to stop exercising. Stay hydrated, keep salt intake sensible (relevant given minoxidil's fluid effects), and remember the pre-blood-test rule.

Tier 4  What not to do

  1. Do not take high-dose biotin supplements. This is a genuinely important and widely unknown point: high-dose biotin — present in most "hair, skin and nails" supplements — interferes with many laboratory immunoassays, including thyroid tests and cardiac troponin. It can produce falsely abnormal results that trigger unnecessary investigation, and in the case of troponin it can mask a real problem. There is also no good evidence it helps hair in people who are not biotin-deficient, which is almost everyone. If you take it, stop, or at minimum tell any lab before a blood test.
  2. Be sceptical of "immune-boosting" supplements. You are deliberately taking a drug to calm one arm of your immune system. Products marketed as immune stimulants are, at best, unproven and at worst working against your treatment. Tell your dermatologist about anything you take, including Ayurvedic and herbal preparations — some have genuine pharmacological activity and CYP interactions.
  3. Do not change two things at once — especially the minoxidil (§9). And do not stop the ritlecitinib because month three looks like nothing. Month three looking like nothing is the expected result.

Glossary

Every term used in this report, in alphabetical order. If a doctor uses a word you do not know, it is very likely here — and if it is not, ask them to define it. That is a reasonable request, not an imposition.

Alopecia areata
Autoimmune hair loss in which the immune system attacks hair follicles, typically causing round patches. The follicles survive.
Alopecia totalis (AT)
Complete loss of scalp hair.
Alopecia universalis (AU)
Complete loss of scalp and all body hair including brows and lashes.
ALT / AST
Liver enzymes measured on a blood test. Raised levels can indicate liver irritation.
Anagen
The active growing phase of a hair follicle, lasting 2–7 years.
Autoimmune
The immune system mistakenly attacking part of your own body.
Blueteq
The NHS system through which approval for high-cost drugs like ritlecitinib is registered.
Boxed warning
The most serious warning a regulator can place on a drug label, shown in a black box.
Catagen
The brief transitional phase where a follicle shuts down, lasting 2–3 weeks.
CD8+ T-cell
A type of white blood cell that kills target cells. The main aggressor in alopecia areata.
Covalent
A permanent chemical bond. Ritlecitinib bonds covalently to JAK3, which is why its effect outlasts its presence in the blood.

A muscle enzyme measured on blood tests. Rises after exercise and, mildly, on JAK inhibitors.

Liver enzymes that break down drugs. Ritlecitinib inhibits both, which is why it raises caffeine levels.
Cytokine
A chemical messenger used by immune cells to communicate. IFN-γ and IL-15 are cytokines.

The cluster of cells at the base of a follicle that directs hair growth. Minoxidil's main target.

Excess hair growth in unwanted places. The commonest minoxidil side effect.

Interferon-gamma release assay — the blood test for latent tuberculosis.

A protected zone the immune system is normally kept out of. Its collapse causes alopecia areata.

The cytokine released by attacking T-cells that strips the follicle's protection.
Interleukin-15 (IL-15)
The cytokine the follicle produces under attack, which feeds its own attackers. Ritlecitinib blocks its signal.
JAK (Janus kinase)
A family of four enzymes (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, TYK2) that relay cytokine messages into a cell.
Kinase
An enzyme that switches other proteins on or off. A "kinase inhibitor" blocks one.

Dormant tuberculosis infection with no symptoms. Must be treated before starting a JAK inhibitor.

Low-dose oral minoxidil.

"Last observation carried forward" — a conservative statistical method that counts people who dropped out, giving a pessimistic estimate.
Lymphocyte
A white blood cell (T, B or NK cell). Monitored on this drug.

MHC
Molecules that display a cell's internal "identity badges" for immune inspection. Healthy follicles hide theirs.
NKG2D
A receptor on aggressive T-cells that recognises stressed cells. Central to the attack in alopecia areata.
NMSC
Non-melanoma skin cancer — basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma.

Results counting only people still in the study — an optimistic estimate. The opposite of LOCF.
Ophiasis
A band-like pattern of loss around the sides and back of the scalp. Harder to treat.
Patient-year
One person treated for one year. 100 people for 6 months = 50 patient-years. Used to express risk fairly.
PGI-C
Patient Global Impression of Change — the patient's own rating of whether they are better.
Prodrug
An inactive drug converted into its active form inside the body. Minoxidil is one.
SALT score
Severity of Alopecia Tool — the percentage of scalp with hair missing, 0 to 100.
SmPC
Summary of Product Characteristics — the official UK prescribing document.
STAT
The messenger protein that carries a signal from a JAK into the cell nucleus.
TEC family kinases
BTK, BMX, ITK, RLK and TEC — a second enzyme family ritlecitinib blocks, controlling T-cell activation.
Telogen
The resting phase of the hair cycle, lasting 2–4 months.
Telogen effluvium
Diffuse shedding when many follicles enter rest at once — from illness, stress or under-eating. Different from alopecia areata.
Terminal hair
Thick, pigmented, normal scalp hair.
Trichoscopy
Examining the scalp with a magnifying dermatoscope. Detects regrowth long before you can see it.
Vellus hair
Fine, pale "peach fuzz". The first sign of regrowth.
VTE
Venous thromboembolism — a blood clot in a vein.
