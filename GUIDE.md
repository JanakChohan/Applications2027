# The Aon scales playbook — everything you need, and the exact thought process

This is the complete field guide for the five modules in the trainer: **scales numerical**, **scales verbal**, **scales ix** (odd-one-out), **scales lst** (shape-sudoku), and **scales cls** (grid categorisation). It tells you what the test is, how it's scored, every trap, and — most importantly — the **step-by-step mental process** to run on each question: where to look, what to read, what to think, and in what order.

Everything here is sourced from the Phase 1 research (`research/FINDINGS.md`, `research/FINDINGS_verbal_logical.md`, and Aon's official practice PDFs). Preparation only — it trains the skill, never how to cheat.

---

## The five rules that apply to every module

1. **Negative marking.** Correct **+1**, wrong **−1**, blank **0**. A wrong answer is *two* points worse than a blank (a missed +1 and a −1).
2. **Blind guessing loses points.** A random 1-in-3 guess averages a loss. **Skip beats guess** unless you can eliminate at least one option. If you can't narrow it, leave it blank.
3. **You are not expected to finish.** These tests are deliberately over-length. A strong score is a high *correct* count with few wrong answers — **accuracy beats coverage.** Never rush an answer to reach the end.
4. **It's norm-referenced.** No fixed pass mark. Your raw score becomes a percentile + Stanine (1–9) versus other candidates, and each employer sets its own cut-off (competitive schemes often want ~70th percentile / Stanine 6+).
5. **Integrity: only the skill transfers.** Items are randomised (nothing to memorise) and you may be **re-tested under supervision at interview** — a score you didn't earn collapses there. The full note is at the end. Everything below builds the actual skill.

> The one-line strategy for all of them: **maximise correct answers, skip rather than guess, read the exact thing being asked, and never commit to True/False when the honest answer is "not enough information".**

# MODULE 1 — scales numerical

**What it is:** ~6 tabbed data displays (tables and charts about one company), a statement, and three buttons: **True / False / Cannot Say**. It measures whether you can pull the right figure from the right display, do a quick *correct* calculation, and know when you *can't* answer.

**Format:** 37 tasks / 12 min (long) or 18 / 6 min (short) — about **20 seconds per item**. On-screen calculator provided; pen and paper allowed.

## The 7-step drill — run this on EVERY numerical item

**Step 0 — Orient (first ~20 seconds of the test, once).** Click each of the six tabs a single time. For each, note only three things: (a) its **name**, (b) whether it's a **table / doughnut / bar / line**, (c) its **unit** (thousand? million? %? index?). Don't read numbers yet. You're building a map so that later, the instant a statement says "margin", you know which tab and what unit — without hunting.

**Step 1 — Read the statement twice, literally.** First read for gist; second read to pull out **five things**: the **entity** (which region/segment), the **period** (which year), the **quantity type** (revenue? units? margin?), the **number + comparator** ("more than $7m", "exactly", "rose 10%"), and any **trap word** (`%`, `percentage points`, `all/every/only`, `exactly`, `approximately`, `more than`). Name them to yourself before doing anything.

**Step 2 — Decide if it's even answerable (the Cannot-Say gate).** Before any maths, ask: is this exact **quantity type**, for this exact **entity**, in this exact **period**, shown on *any* tab?
- Quantity type not displayed (asks "units sold" but no tab shows units) → **Cannot Say. Stop.**
- Period outside the years shown → **Cannot Say. Stop.**
- Entity not listed → **Cannot Say. Stop.**
This single check decides roughly a quarter of items and costs nothing when you're right. Most people skip it and over-infer — that's the #1 score-killer.

**Step 3 — Open the one tab; read the UNIT first, then the figure.** Go to the tab that holds it. Read the **footnote / axis title before any number** — thousands, millions, %, index? Then find the exact cell/bar/segment for your entity + period and read the value. If the statement's unit differs, **convert now** (a table "in $ thousand" showing 7,256 = **$7.256 million**).

**Step 4 — Do the minimal operation, matched to the wording:**
- "was / exceeded / less than X" → just **read and compare**, no maths.
- "rose / fell by X%" → **(new − old) ÷ old × 100** (divide by the *original*).
- "rose by X percentage points" → **subtract the two rates** (don't divide).
- "accounted for X% of total" → **part ÷ total × 100**.
- "profit / net / per head" → **combine two tabs** (subtract or divide).
- "highest / lowest" → **scan all entities** for that period.
- "every year / throughout" → **check each step**; one exception kills it.
Where you can, **compare before you compute** ("is A clearly bigger than B?" often needs no division).

**Step 5 — Apply the decision rule.**
- Confirmed **exactly** by the data → **True.**
- **Contradicted** — including "close but not the exact number asked" → **False.**
- A figure you needed was **missing** → **Cannot Say.**

**Step 6 — Two-second sanity gate before you click:** (a) Is the **correct tab still on screen**? (answering with the wrong tab up can cost points). (b) Did I answer the **quantity actually asked**, not a look-alike (revenue vs units, points vs %)? (c) Am I about to say True on something the data only *suggests*? If so → Cannot Say. Now answer.

**Step 7 — Time gate.** Past ~30 seconds and not cracked? **Blank it** (0, no penalty) and move on. Come back if time remains.

## How to read each chart type — where your eyes go, in order

- **Data table** (Aon's most common form): title → **units footnote** → the **column** (period) → the **row** (exact entity label) → the cell. Beware the bold **Total** row — never grab it when a line item was asked.
- **Doughnut / pie** (market/revenue share): read the **% on the slice** you need → then the **absolute-total caption underneath** ("Total revenue: $86m"). If the question wants an absolute amount, it's **share% × total** — that's a two-part combination, not a lookup.
- **Stacked bar** (splits, e.g. headcount by region): read the **axis unit title** ("… in thousands") → find the **period column** → read the segment's **printed value** (or its height). The **full stack height** is the total.
- **Grouped horizontal bar** (Aon's FORECAST form): read the **value-axis unit at the bottom** → find the **category row** (the year) → read the **value labelled at the end** of the bar whose **legend colour** matches your series.
- **Line chart** (a metric over time): read the **axis unit** → follow **your entity's colour** → read the point for the period. Check whether the axis **starts at zero or is rebased/indexed** (an index of 130 is not "130 units").

## Finance terminology (for firms that use accounting statements)

Some employers frame the data as **income statements, balance sheets, and cash flow statements**, with segment breakdowns and ratios. The trainer includes these themes. Here's what each shows and how questions use them.

**Income statement (P&L) — a flow over a period.** The chain:
`Revenue − Cost of Sales = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses = Operating Profit − interest & tax = Net Profit.`
- **Gross margin** = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue. **Operating margin** = Operating Profit ÷ Revenue. **Net margin** = Net Profit ÷ Revenue.
- **EPS** (earnings per share) = Net Profit ÷ shares.
- Question shapes: "Segment X's **gross profit** exceeded $Ym" → Revenue − Cost of Sales (two tabs). "**Gross margin** was above 40%" → Gross Profit ÷ Revenue. Watch **% (a margin) vs percentage points** and **$ thousand vs $ million**.

**Balance sheet — a snapshot at a point in time.** The identity:
`Assets = Liabilities + Equity`, so `Net Assets (Equity) = Assets − Liabilities.`
- **Return on Assets (ROA)** = Profit ÷ Assets. **Gearing** = Debt ÷ Equity. **Current ratio** = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities.
- Question shapes: "**Net assets** for the Division exceeded £Xm" → Assets − Liabilities (two tabs). "**Return on assets** rose more than 2 percentage points" → subtract the two ROA rates (not a % change).

**Cash flow statement — cash in/out over a period.**
`Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure = Free Cash Flow.`
- **Cash conversion** = Operating Cash Flow ÷ Operating Profit.
- Question shapes: "**Free cash flow** was positive / above $Xm" → OCF − Capex (two tabs). Beware "cash flow" ≠ "profit" — a data-type mismatch trap: a profit figure can't answer a cash-flow question.

**Reading order for any financial-statement table:** units footnote → the **exact line label** (don't confuse Gross Profit with Net Profit, or Assets with Net Assets) → the period column → cross-check any subtotal/total row. The classic finance traps are (1) grabbing the wrong line, (2) grabbing a Total, (3) confusing a margin **%** with a **percentage-point** move, and (4) mixing thousands and millions.

## The traps, each with a worked example and the fix

1. **Units — thousands vs millions.** Table "in $ thousand" shows Research = 7,256. Statement: "research costs exceeded $7 million." 7,256 thousand = **$7.256m > $7m → True.** *Fix: read the unit footnote before the number; convert into the statement's unit.*
2. **Close but not exact = False.** Data gives 1,550,000; statement says "1,500,000 more". They're close, but the statement didn't say "about". **False.** *Fix: unless it says "approximately", any mismatch is False.*
3. **Missing period/entity/quantity = Cannot Say.** Tabs show FY5–FY8; statement asks about FY3 → **Cannot Say.** *Fix: run Step 2 before any maths.*
4. **Percent vs percentage points.** Margin 10% → 15% is **+5 percentage points** but **+50% relative**. "Grew 5%" is False; "rose 5 points" is True. *Fix: "points" → subtract rates; "%" → divide the change by the original.*
5. **Data-type mismatch = Cannot Say.** Asks "units sold" but the tab shows "revenue" (or a doughnut of revenue **share**). Revenue ≠ units. **Cannot Say**, even though numbers are on screen. *Fix: match the quantity type exactly.*
6. **Combine two displays.** "Segment revenue" from a **share %** doughnut × the **total** caption; or profit = revenue tab − costs tab. *Fix: identify every figure first; note the two tabs may use different units.*
7. **Cumulative / Total-row distractor.** A "YTD" or bold **Total** row is not a single period/line. *Fix: match the statement's granularity; read the exact row label.*
8. **Index / base-100.** An index 100 → 130 is **+30%** (because base 100) but is **not** "130 units". Absolute-value claims about an index are usually **Cannot Say.** *Fix: check the axis for "index / base 100 / rebased".*
9. **Compound % isn't additive.** +12% then −8% is **not** +4%; it's ×1.12 ×0.92 = **−0.96%.** *Fix: chain multiplicatively.*
10. **Biggest absolute ≠ biggest %.** The largest raw move often comes off the largest base. *Fix: divide each change by its own base before ranking.*
11. **Axis not at zero.** A bar axis starting above zero exaggerates differences. *Fix: read the numbers, not the bar heights.*

## Per-item-type quick thought process

- **Direct lookup** — Step 2 gate → open tab → check unit → read cell → compare. The trap is the unit and the exact row.
- **Single-step arithmetic** — identify the two cells → subtract/add → compare to the stated amount. Keep both in the same unit.
- **Percentage change** — find old and new → (new−old)/**old**×100. The trap is dividing by the new value.
- **Percentage points vs %** — decide which the wording means *first*, then either subtract (points) or divide (%).
- **Ratio / share** — part ÷ total × 100. The total may be a caption or a Total row.
- **Multi-tab combination** — write down each figure and its tab before combining; mind differing units.
- **Trend / direction** — check every consecutive step; "every period" fails on one exception.
- **Rank / comparison** — scan all entities for the named period; for "largest growth" compare **%**, not raw size.
- **Insufficient data** — the answer is Cannot Say; identify exactly which figure is missing (period/entity/quantity) so you can say it with confidence.

## A full worked example

*Tabs: Revenue (table, $ thousand), Cost of Sales (table, $ thousand), Revenue Mix (doughnut, %), Headcount, Gross Margin (line, %), EPS Index.* Statement: **"Online's gross profit in FY8 exceeded $2 million."**
- Step 1: entity = Online, period = FY8, quantity = **gross profit** (= revenue − cost of sales), comparator = "> $2m".
- Step 2: revenue and cost of sales are both shown for Online in FY8 → answerable.
- Step 3: Revenue tab (in $ thousand) Online FY8 = 5,400 → $5.4m. Cost of Sales tab Online FY8 = 3,100 → $3.1m.
- Step 4: gross profit = 5.4 − 3.1 = **$2.3m**.
- Step 5: 2.3 > 2.0 → **True.**
- Step 6: correct tabs used (both revenue and costs), quantity matches ("gross profit", not "revenue"), not over-inferring. Answer **True.**

# MODULE 2 — scales verbal

**What it is:** ~49 statements / 12 min (~15s each) over ~5–6 **tabs of short passages**, all about one fictional company. Judge each **True / False / Cannot Say** against the *one* tab it relates to — which you must find yourself.

## The thought process — run this on every verbal item

**Step 0 — Orient.** Skim the tab labels once (Structure, Strategy, Values, Locations, Products, People…). You'll know within ~10 items which tab holds what; that's most of your speed.

**Step 1 — Read the statement and extract its claim.** Identify the **subject** (who/what), the **attribute** (what about it), and any **scope word** (`all / always / only / never / some / may`). Notice absolutes especially.

**Step 2 — Find the one relevant tab.** Match the statement's subject to a tab label and open it. If you answer with the wrong passage showing, you can lose the point even when right.

**Step 3 — Hunt for the specific sentence.** Find the sentence that addresses this exact attribute. If **no sentence addresses it at all → Cannot Say. Stop.** (Don't reason from what's "probably" true — that's the outside-knowledge trap.)

**Step 4 — Compare word-for-word.** Put the statement beside the sentence:
- Every key word supported (a genuine synonym counts) → **True.**
- Any key word contradicted, or a detail swapped (e.g. "online" where the text says "on-premises") → **False** — *one* wrong element makes the whole thing False.
- The sentence is about a **weaker** claim than the statement (text says "several markets", statement says "all markets") → **not supported → Cannot Say.**

**Step 5 — Guard against the two killers before you answer.** (a) Am I using anything **not in the passage**? If yes → it's Cannot Say. (b) Did an **absolute word** ("all/every/only") sneak past a passage that only supports a general claim? If yes → Cannot Say (or False if contradicted).

## The decision rule (from Aon's own worked examples)
- **True** — the relevant tab entails it, *including via a true synonym/paraphrase* ("imported" ⇒ "sourced from abroad").
- **False** — the tab contradicts it, even partly. One swapped detail ⇒ False.
- **Cannot Say** — the tab neither confirms nor denies it (not stated, or needs an unstated inference).

## Traps + worked example
- **Outside knowledge** (the biggest): judge only from the passage. Plausible ≠ stated.
- **Absolute words**: "all/always/only" rarely match a hedged passage → usually Cannot Say/False.
- **Paraphrase flip vs synonym**: a genuine synonym is True; a swapped detail is False.
- **Quantifier swap**: "some" in the text does not support "all" in the statement.

*Worked example.* Passage (Strategy tab): *"The company plans to expand into several Asian markets over the next three years."* Statement: **"The company will open stores in every Asian country."** The text supports *several* markets, not *every country*, and "open stores" isn't stated. Nothing confirms or denies "every country" → **Cannot Say** (the absolute + the unstated detail both point there).

# MODULE 3 — scales ix (odd-one-out / discovering rules)

**What it is:** 20 items / 5 min (~15s each). A row/grid of **9 objects**; eight share a hidden rule and **one breaks it** — click the breaker.

## The thought process
**Step 1 — Inventory the attributes.** For the nine objects, run through each attribute in turn: **shape**, **number of sides**, **fill** (solid vs outline), **rotation/orientation**, **inner shape** (present/absent), **count**. Don't lock onto the first thing you notice.
**Step 2 — Find the shared property.** The rule is the property **eight objects share**. Ask of each attribute: "do 8 of 9 match on this?" The one that doesn't match on that attribute is your breaker.
**Step 3 — Confirm it's unique.** Check that only **one** object violates the rule — if two seem odd, you've picked the wrong attribute as the rule; try another.
**Step 4 — Click it; if not found in ~15s, skip** (a wrong click costs a point).

**Rules to cycle through:** even vs odd number of sides · all the same shape · all solid vs all outline · all the same orientation · all contain (or all lack) an inner shape · a count that increases/repeats.

*Worked example.* Nine shapes, all **solid**, all **upright**, but they're a mix of squares, hexagons and one **triangle**. Fill and orientation are constant (not the rule). The shared property is **even number of sides** (squares = 4, hexagons = 6); the **triangle** (3 sides) is the only violator → click the triangle.

# MODULE 4 — scales lst (shape-sudoku / gapChallenge)

**What it is:** a grid where **each shape appears once per row and once per column** (a Latin square), 4×4 growing to 5×5. One cell is a **"?"** — pick the shape that belongs there. You can pre-fill blank cells to reason.

## The thought process
**Step 1 — Work the "?" cell's row.** List the shapes already present in its row. The full shape set minus those = the candidates.
**Step 2 — Cross-check with its column.** Of those candidates, eliminate any already in the "?" cell's column. Usually one shape survives — that's the answer.
**Step 3 — If more than one survives (harder 5×5),** use the pre-fill cells: fill a nearby forced cell first, which then forces the "?".
**Step 4 — Confirm with both** row and column before committing. Using only the row (or only the column) is the usual slip.

*Worked example.* 4×4 with shapes {circle, square, triangle, star}. The "?" cell's row already has circle, square, triangle → only **star** is missing from the row. Its column doesn't contain a star → **star** is confirmed.

# MODULE 5 — scales cls (grid categorisation)

**What it is:** six 3×3 grids of letters/numbers are **colour-sorted into two groups** by a hidden rule; classify a new grid. 12 tasks / 12 min.

## The thought process
**Step 1 — Form a hypothesis from the clearest contrast.** Compare an obvious Group A grid with an obvious Group B grid. What single property differs? Candidates: **centre cell is a number/letter**, **contains a specific character**, **count of a character ≥ k**, **number of distinct symbols is odd/even**, **more letters than digits**.
**Step 2 — Test it against ALL six examples.** The correct rule separates every example perfectly. If one example breaks your rule, discard it and try the next hypothesis.
**Step 3 — State the rule as one testable sentence** ("Group A = the centre cell is a number").
**Step 4 — Apply it to the target grid**, counting carefully (miscounting is the main error), and classify.

*Worked example.* Every Group A grid has a **digit in the centre cell**; every Group B grid has a **letter** there. That rule fits all six examples. The target grid's centre is "7" → **Group A**.

# The integrity reality — why practising the skill is the only thing that works

Stated plainly (sources in `research/FINDINGS_verbal_logical.md §C`):
- **You get a unique, randomised set of items** from a large bank — there is no fixed answer key to memorise, and every candidate's test differs.
- **Scores are norm-referenced and negatively marked** — the only durable way to raise your percentile/Stanine is faster, more accurate reasoning.
- **You are re-tested under supervision.** Aon's own guidance: *"If you are invited to an interview after the online assessment, you will be expected to complete a repeat module… If you cheated in the online assessment, it will come to light during the retest, at the very latest,"* and dishonest candidates are excluded. Some assessments are webcam-proctored.

So a score you didn't earn collapses at the supervised retest. **The only thing that transfers to the real, proctored, re-tested assessment is the trained skill.** Legitimate practice — the drills and thought processes above — raises both the unsupervised and the supervised score identically. That is exactly what this trainer is for. It contains no answer lookup and nothing to help during a live test.
