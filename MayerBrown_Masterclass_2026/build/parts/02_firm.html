The firm as a business, from the inside

Where Mayer Brown came from, how the money flows, what each group gives and gets, how risk is controlled, what has grown, what leadership says comes next, and the skeletons, told straight.
Don't say this: four common errors 1. "Mayer Brown merged with Brown & Wood." False. Brown & Wood merged with Sidley in 2001 [S92][S339]; Mayer Brown's own history doesn't mention it [S70].
2. "Jon Van Gorp is managing partner." He is Chair. The firm's leadership page lists Jeremy Clay as Managing Partner [S74].
3. "Mayer Brown is the biggest international firm in Hong Kong." Out of date. Johnson Stokes & Master became independent again in 2024; Mayer Brown Hong Kong had 21 lawyers in the 2026 data [S70][S220].
4. "It's a one-firm global partnership." The firm calls itself "a global legal services provider comprising associated legal practices that are separate entities" [S75]. Say "integrated transatlantic platform", the firm's own phrase [S327].

A. The founding insight

In 1881 "Adolf Kraus and Levy Mayer form a partnership in Chicago" [S70] Confirmed. The firm became Mayer, Meyer, Austrian & Platt in 1909 and Mayer, Brown & Platt in 1970. Separately, in 1895, Rowe & Maw was founded in London [S70]. In 2002 the two combined as Mayer, Brown, Rowe & Maw, shortened to Mayer Brown in 2007 [S70].

The insight for you: London is not a US outpost bolted on in 2002. It is a 130-year-old English practice that a Chicago firm merged into. The firm marked "its 130th anniversary in the City of London" in 2025 [S222]. The London head in April 2026 framed it the same way: Legal Business headlined his interview "We are not an outpost" [S303] (paywalled; headline read only).

The firm's own description of its purpose: "The world's most sophisticated major corporations, funds, and financial institutions rely on Mayer Brown ... to represent them in their most important and complex transactions and disputes" [S79]. It publishes client-service principles rather than a mission statement, including "Collaborating across offices to deliver the best of our knowledge, wherever in the world it's needed" [S79].

Growth in 2025 came from price and mix, not volume. Billed hours fell about 4%, revenue rose about 10%, billing rates rose more than 10%, and the number of clients billed fell 17% [S220] Confirmed (percentages inferred). Chair Jon Van Gorp: "We're sustaining demand, but also repositioning the platform" [S220].

Context: the 2026 Am Law 100 average PEP was reported at $3.59m [S118], so Mayer Brown's $3.196m is below the US average but well above the Magic Circle (Part 4). Bloomberg Law described the firm as likely among the 30 largest US firms [S86] Reported.

Key term: WIP and lock-upWork in progress (WIP) is time recorded but not yet billed. Lock-up is the time between doing the work and getting paid. The London LLP credited its 2025 turnover rise partly to "an increased focus on WIP management and lock-up" [S78]. For a trainee, that means recording time accurately and promptly.

C. The contracts the business is made of

{{FIG:contracts}}

Partners. About 239 equity partners in 2025 [S220]. Pay is not pure lockstep at the top: Van Gorp said "The compensation for equity partners was expanded with more room at the top to pay our top performers" [S72]. In the English LLP, "Allocation of variable profits to members is made on a calendar year basis, taking into account the performance of the business and the individual", and "Capital is treated as debt and is repaid in full on retirement" [S78] Confirmed. No source names the system; say "banded, with a performance element" and no more.

Clients. Banks, funds, insurers, corporates. Bloomberg Law names JPMorgan Chase and Citibank as major clients [S86]; Legal 500 lists ISDA, Barclays, BNP Paribas and others for London derivatives [S316].

Lateral partners. 26 joined in 2024 and 56 in 2025, aimed at leveraged finance and private credit, insurance, energy, infrastructure, capital markets and white collar work. Van Gorp: "We just became very deliberate this year about wanting to expand the firm through lateral hiring" [S84] Reported.

D. The risk and control architecture

{{FIG:risk_arch}}

Why this produces the firm's characteristic result: in a law firm the product is trust. A firm that acts in a conflict, leaks information or files the wrong document can lose a client and its insurance, and can face regulatory sanctions. The controls are slow by design. A trainee is the person most likely to see the detail that goes wrong, which is why the controls lean on trainees doing checklists properly.

Firm-wide growth areas named by leadership: in 2024, "insurance transactions, energy, data privacy and cybersecurity" [S72]; in 2025, finance, energy, infrastructure and insurance, with Asia "a very big bright spot for us" [S220]. F. Private partnership: what that means for research

Mayer Brown has no shareholders, annual report or earnings calls. There are three windows: figures self-reported to the American Lawyer (unaudited; the firm reprints them), the English LLP's audited accounts at Companies House, and press releases (positive by design) Inferred. The Companies House accounts are the single best primary source, and almost no applicant reads them.

Say this in the interview"I looked at the LLP's accounts at Companies House. Turnover went from £163m to £186m in the year to April 2025, and the members' report credits better WIP management and lock-up. I'd like to understand how that shows up for a trainee: time recording, billing narratives?"

G. Leadership's view of the future, and what hiring suggests

  • Stated: fewer, deeper client relationships [S220]; deliberate lateral growth in areas "we thought we were underweight" [S84]; London "central to our international platform" [S222]; AI that amplifies "lawyer capabilities and judgement, not replacing them" [S230].
  • Inferred from hiring: London is building a sponsor-side (private equity) finance capability to sit alongside its bank-side strength; Asia has shifted from Hong Kong to Tokyo and Singapore; headcount is held roughly flat while revenue per lawyer rises Inferred.
  • Not doing: merging. While rivals combined, the chair said "Right now, we're pretty focused on executing our strategy" [S220].

I. The skeletons, told straight

{{FIG:skeletons}}

Refco (the serious one). Refco, a commodities broker, collapsed in October 2005, two months after its stock market listing, when it emerged that the chief executive had hidden a receivable of more than $1bn owed by a company he controlled. Joseph Collins, a Mayer Brown partner, was Refco's "primary outside counsel" [S94]. Prosecutors said lawyers under his direction drafted documents for loans that hid the debt. His 2009 conviction was overturned in 2012 because the judge had spoken privately with a juror; he was convicted again at retrial in November 2012 and sentenced in July 2013 to one year and one day [S94][S95][S96] Confirmed. The firm settled the Refco trustee's claims for an undisclosed sum in 2013 [S99]. One investor case against the firm, Pacific Investment Management v Mayer Brown (2nd Cir. 2010), is now a leading US case limiting advisers' liability for statements not attributed to them [S98] Reported.

GM (the trainee lesson). In 2008, closing papers that Mayer Brown prepared for General Motors, to pay off one loan, also terminated the security filing for a separate $1.5bn term loan. JPMorgan and its lawyers approved them. In 2015 the Second Circuit held the security really was terminated [S103]. The lenders then sued Mayer Brown, and lost: in 2017 the Seventh Circuit held "Mayer Brown did not owe a duty to plaintiffs" [S102] Confirmed. No settlement was found.

Hong Kong, 2021. Acting for the University of Hong Kong, the firm wrote to the custodians of the "Pillar of Shame" Tiananmen memorial about its removal; after criticism, it stopped acting on the matter [S111] Reported.

August 2026. The firm said "one of its personnel mistakenly sent a small number of documents to an unauthorised third party" who had misrepresented their identity, and that the third party "did not access our systems" [S105]. Do not call it a hack.

No SRA or Solicitors Disciplinary Tribunal sanction against the London LLP was found, but the register itself could not be checked Gap.
