Why the firm is built this way

Four questions, answered with evidence: why this structure; how the teams actually work together; what clients are really buying; and what the non-fee-earning side looks like and how it is wired to the lawyers.

1. Why is the business structured this way?

The structure. A group of "associated legal practices that are separate entities" [S75]: a US LLP, the English LLP (which also runs Tokyo), Hong Kong, a Singapore joint venture and an association in Brazil. In London, the practice mix is weighted to finance products sold to institutions (asset-based lending, derivatives, trade finance, securitisation), plus real estate and construction disputes [S139][S140].

{{FIG:model_compare}}

The theory: what the structure buys

  1. Depth in asset-focused finance. Top tier in London for asset-based lending (13th consecutive year), derivatives and structured products (new this year) and trade finance [S139]. These are repeat products for repeat bank clients, which fits "go deeper with fewer clients" [S220].
  2. Products that need several teams at once. A pension buy-in needs pensions, insurance, derivatives and finance lawyers together (figure below). A firm with only one of those can't run it alone.
  3. Credible transatlantic execution. English-law and New York-law finance from one platform: "a fully integrated platform for New York and English law debt and equity solutions" [S328]; 96 London-New York deals in 2025 [S302].
  4. A small cohort. About 15 trainees a year. The training principal: "One of the advantages of a training contract at Mayer Brown is that it is a small cohort ... we can develop a bespoke programme" [S16].

What the structure costs

  1. Less brand in private equity and M&A. Legal 500 ranks London premium M&A in tier 6 and mid-market PE in tier 3 [S146]. The firm is importing sponsor-side talent rather than having grown it [S327].
  2. Fewer NQ jobs per practice because the intake is small.
  3. A smaller Asia platform since 2024 [S70][S220].
  4. Lower London profitability than the global headline. Average profit per LLP member was £861,333 in the year to April 2025 [S78], a different measure from the $3.196m global PEP; treat the gap as directional only.

2. How do the teams work together, and where does collaboration actually live?

Read the firm's own words precisely. Its principles say "Collaborating across offices to deliver the best of our knowledge, wherever in the world it's needed" and "Building diverse teams of lawyers from our market-leading practices to help achieve our clients' goals" [S79]. Those are about teams on a client's matter, not about a shared pool of ideas. Collaboration lives in three concrete places:

  • Cross-practice products. The Pensions Finance practice combines pensions with "finance, financial services, insurance, and derivatives lawyers" as a "one-stop shop" [S314].
  • Cross-office deal teams. The Zankore AI financing was led from Singapore with London on hedging and Washington and Dubai on sanctions and export controls [S231]. Former London head Dominic Griffiths: "We set out to build something distinctive - a platform where the best lawyers collaborate across borders" [S301].
  • Industry groups that cut across practices: Financial Services, Insurance, Global Energy, Projects & Infrastructure, Funds, Technology, Data Centers, Chemicals [S79].
{{FIG:pensions_finance}}

The Ford deal shows the mechanism. The £4.6bn buy-in, the largest UK pension risk transfer announced in 2025, was led by Tom MacAulay of the Global Insurance Industry Group, with Andrew Block of the Pensions Group as relationship partner [S310]. MacAulay had been hired from Legal & General in 2024 to "bring cross-office and practice expertise across corporate, finance and pensions" [S313] Confirmed. A pensions relationship opened the door; an insurance specialist ran the deal.

3. What do clients actually buy, and why do they pay?

{{FIG:client_view}}

What they buy. Precise, repeatable execution on complex finance products: ISDA netting opinions, significant risk transfer notes, cross-border asset-based lending, securitisation programmes, buy-ins [S316][S317][S318]. And, increasingly, judgement: Thomson Reuters finds UK buyers treat expertise as "the price of entry" [S167].

Why they pay. Firm-curated directory comments on its derivatives team: "has its finger on the pulse with ISDA and transatlantic regulatory work"; "military-style turnaround, good value and a deep bench" [S323] Reported, undated and selected by the firm. The current Legal 500 pages carry no client testimonials, only editorial text [S315]-[S319].

{{FIG:london_growth}}

The counter-case, with numbers

  • Many named clients (Barclays, BNP Paribas, JP Morgan, Deutsche Bank) also use the Magic Circle and US rivals; these are not exclusive relationships Inferred.
  • Clients billed fell 17% (7,600 to 6,316). That is either deliberate focus or attrition; the firm says focus [S220].
  • In London securitisation and pensions the firm is tier 2, not tier 1 [S141][S145].
  • Acting for both trustees (Ford) and insurers (Royal London) shows breadth, but it limits who the firm can act for on any one deal Inferred.

How it is organised. The firm's careers page lists business services groups: "Accounting & Analysis, Business Development & Marketing, Business Intake & Conflicts, Facilities, Human Resources, Information Technology, Knowledge Management, Paralegal, and Secretarial" [S331] Confirmed. London names a Director of Administration and a Senior Director of Commercial Strategy & Growth [S49]. No offshore shared-services centre was found.

AI and innovation. Global CIO Evette Pastoriza Clift joined from Fried Frank in September 2024; training is compulsory before anyone gets Harvey or Copilot; the firm takes a hybrid "buy v build" approach. "The AI literacy piece is really fundamental to everything" [S330] Reported. The GenAI programme was Highly Commended at the FT Innovative Lawyers Europe 2025 awards [S325].
