Part 11

The scenario playbook

How to answer any hypothetical without losing your footing, then 39 practice cards, five composure scenarios with decision trees, and a CV grill. This is method, so most of it carries the Inferred tag; where it leans on a rule, the rule is cited.

Why scenarios matter at this firm and this event

At the masterclass itself nobody will "interview" you. But three things on the agenda behave like an interview: the trainee panel (where your question is judged by everyone in the room), the practice deep dive (where a partner may throw a hypothetical at the audience), and networking (where every conversation is a two-minute assessment). Then, if the event goes well, the training contract process follows. Law firm assessment centres commonly use a case study, a written exercise and a partner interview, and partners like to push a candidate with "what would you do if" questions to see whether they stay calm and sensible (see Part 9 for what is known about Mayer Brown's own process).

The aim of this part is not to memorise answers. It is to build one stable way of thinking that you can apply to anything: a set order of priorities, and a four-step routine that turns that order into a spoken answer.

The four-step protocol: steady, sort, act, close

{{FIG:protocol}}

1. Steady (about 5 seconds)

Take one breath. Then restate the question in one line. This buys time and proves you heard the actual question, not the one you prepared for.

"So I've got a signing tonight, the associate is unreachable, and the other side wants the documents now."

2. Sort (about 15 seconds)

Name the two or three things in tension, then rank them using the priority order below. Saying the ranking out loud is what interviewers mark.

"Two things matter: getting the deal signed on time, and not releasing anything I'm not authorised to release. The second comes first."

3. Act (25 to 40 seconds)

Give the concrete next step: who you call, what you send, by when. Trainees are judged on initiative within limits, so show both halves: you move, and you escalate.

"I'd try the associate on phone and Teams, then the supervising partner. Meanwhile I'd get everything else ready so we lose no time once someone says yes."

4. Close (5 to 10 seconds)

One sentence of principle. Then stop. Silence after a good answer is fine (see tactic 3).

"So: authority first, then speed, and nobody is surprised."

The priority order when good things collide

{{FIG:priority}}

Every hard trainee scenario is a clash between good things: helping the client, pleasing a senior colleague, hitting a deadline, following the process. The order above settles almost all of them. It mirrors the way the solicitors' regulator, the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA), frames a solicitor's duties: the seven SRA Principles require you to act with honesty and integrity, in a way that upholds public trust, and in the best interests of each client, and where Principles conflict, those that safeguard the wider public interest take precedence over an individual client's interests [S341] Confirmed. Speed and convenience are not Principles at all.

Say this in the interview"I'd protect the rules and the client first, keep the record straight, and then go as fast as that allows."

Eight ways interviewers break a frame, and the counters

{{FIG:tactics}}

Partners test whether a view survives pressure. A good candidate changes their mind when given a new fact, and holds it when given only pressure. Say which one is happening: "If that's the case, I'd change my answer to..." versus "I understand the pressure, but I'd still..."

Staying calm physically

  • Breathe out longer than you breathe in before you speak (four in, six out). It slows the heart rate and stops the rushed first sentence.
  • Feet flat, hands still and visible. At networking, hold your drink in your left hand so your right is free to shake.
  • Pause before the answer, not during it. A two-second pause reads as thought. Fillers ("um, like") read as panic.
  • Have a written fallback line for a blank: "Can I take ten seconds to think about that?" Then use all ten.
  • Lower your pitch at the end of the sentence. Rising endings sound like questions.

The scenario ladder

The "job description" for this pack is two documents read together: the SEO London event page, which sets out what happens on 2 November, and Mayer Brown's own description of what trainees do (Part 1). Each line becomes three practice scenarios.

{{FIG:ladder}}

A. The event lines

A1.1 · Application: "Why this masterclass?"Easy

Setup: The SEO application asks why you want to attend the Mayer Brown masterclass.

Trap: Writing about "a global firm with a diverse client base". That is the event page's own sentence. The reader wrote it.

Say: One mechanism about the firm (for example, its depth in finance work for banks and its structured finance heritage; Part 4), one thing you want to learn on the day (a specific agenda item), and one honest reason it fits you.

Why it works: SEO's event is for 60 candidates [S340]; readers sort hundreds of forms. Specifics are the only thing that separates.

Twist: Word limit is tight. Hold: cut adjectives first, never the mechanism.

A1.2 · Application: grades below the lineMedium

Setup: You had a weak first-year module or a 2:2 in one year.

Trap: Hiding it, or writing a paragraph of apology.

Say: State the facts in one line, any mitigating circumstance in one line (the event page says mitigating circumstances will be considered [S340]), and the evidence of recovery.

Why it works: Eligibility is "predicted or achieved 2:1"; the form is checking honesty and trajectory as much as the number.

Twist: "Why should we pick you over someone with a first?" Hold: "Grades are one signal. Here's another one that matters for a trainee: [specific example of sustained, careful work]."

A1.3 · Application: "Tell us about a commercial issue affecting the firm"Hard

Setup: A commercial awareness question with a short word count.

Trap: Summarising a headline. That shows reading, not thinking.

Say: Headline in one sentence, then the chain: what changes for Mayer Brown's clients, what work that creates or removes, and which practice does it. Use one story from Part 7.

Why it works: Commercial awareness at a law firm means seeing how a market event becomes legal work.

Twist: Asked about it again at the event. Hold: give the same view and add one thing you've learned since.

A2.1 · Firm overview: the speaker asks the room a questionEasy

Setup: "Does anyone know when Mayer Brown came to London?"

Trap: Silence, or guessing a year.

Say: If you know it from Part 2, say it in one line with the mechanism (the merger and what it brought). If not: "I know it came through a merger with a UK firm, but not the year."

Why it works: Being right matters less than being precise about what you know.

Twist: Follow-up: "And what did that bring?" Hold with one practice name, not a list.

A2.2 · Firm overview: a number you've read differs from the slideMedium

Setup: The slide says one revenue or headcount figure; your pack says another.

Trap: Correcting the presenter in public.

Say: Nothing in the session. Later, if natural: "I'd seen a different figure in the legal press. Is that a different year or a different basis?"

Why it works: Firm figures vary by financial year and by what is counted (lawyers versus total staff). Asking about basis shows numeracy without point-scoring.

Twist: "Where did you read that?" Hold: name the outlet and date.

A2.3 · Firm overview: "What would you change about our strategy?"Hard

Setup: A partner asks this in a Q&A.

Trap: Either flattery ("nothing") or a lecture.

Say: "I'm not in a position to change it, but the question I'd be watching is..." then one tension from Part 8 (for example: how a firm strong in bank finance positions itself as private credit grows).

Why it works: It shows a view and humility in one move.

Twist: "And your answer?" Hold: give a tentative view and the fact that would change it.

A3.1 · Trainee panel: asking a good questionEasy

Setup: The chair asks for questions from the floor.

Trap: "What's the culture like?" Everyone asks it; every answer is "friendly".

Say: A question only research could produce, from the list in Part 9. Example: "In your finance seat, how much of the work was on repeat programmes for the same clients, and how did that change what you were trusted with?"

Why it works: Trainees remember the good questions and often feed back to graduate recruitment.

Twist: The trainee gives a short answer. Hold: one follow-up, then let someone else in.

A3.2 · Trainee panel: a trainee says something negativeMedium

Setup: A trainee says the hours in one seat were tough.

Trap: Pouncing on it, or pretending you didn't hear.

Say: Later, in networking: "You mentioned the hours in that seat. What helped you get through the busiest weeks?"

Why it works: It treats the honesty as useful and asks for the coping skill, which is what you need to learn.

Twist: "Would that put you off?" Hold: "No. I'd want to know it's coming and how the team shares it."

A3.3 · Trainee panel: "So why do you want to be a lawyer?"Hard

Setup: A trainee turns the question on you in front of others.

Trap: A speech. Or "I like problem solving" (so does everyone).

Say: Thirty seconds, from your own evidence: one experience, what it showed you about the work (precision, advising someone with money at stake, being trusted with detail), why commercial law and not another job.

Why it works: It is the same structure you will use at interview, tested early.

Twist: "Why not the Bar?" Hold: "I want to be part of a team on a transaction or a case from start to finish, and to work for clients over years, not one hearing at a time."

A4.1 · Practice deep dive: you don't understand a termEasy

Setup: The partner says "true sale opinion" or "buy-in" without explaining.

Trap: Nodding.

Say: Check the glossary (Part 14) in your notes. If still lost, ask at the end: "You mentioned X. Could you say what the lawyer is actually checking there?"

Why it works: "What is the lawyer actually doing" is the question partners like, because it is the job.

Twist: None. This one is a free point.

A4.2 · Practice deep dive: a hypothetical for the roomMedium

Setup: "A bank client wants to sell a pool of car loans to investors. What could go wrong?"

Trap: Saying "I don't know law". Nobody expects law.

Say: First principles: "Investors need to know the loans really belong to the new vehicle if the bank goes bust, that the borrowers keep paying, and that someone collects and passes on the money." That is, in plain words, true sale, credit risk and servicing (Part 6).

Why it works: Structured finance is a core Mayer Brown practice (Part 4). Thinking in risks is how lawyers start.

Twist: "And the regulator?" Hold: "There are rules on securitisation in the UK. I know the regime was overhauled in 2024 but not the detail." (Part 6 has the detail.)

A4.3 · Practice deep dive: the partner challenges your answerHard

Setup: You answered A4.2. The partner says: "But the bank still services the loans. So has it really sold them?"

Trap: Folding ("oh, then no") or digging in.

Say: "That's the tension. Legally it can sell the loans and still act as collector for a fee, as long as the money it collects is held for the buyer and not mixed with its own. So the documents and the cash flow have to show that."

Why it works: You updated on a fact and kept the structure. That is the whole frame method.

Twist: "How would you check?" Hold: "Read the sale agreement and the servicing agreement, and follow where the cash goes."

A5.1 · Applications overview: the deadline questionEasy

Setup: Graduate recruitment explains the vacation scheme and training contract process.

Trap: Asking something that's on the website.

Say: Take notes. Ask only what the site doesn't say, for example how the masterclass connects to the vacation scheme process (Part 9 lists the known facts).

Why it works: Shows you've read the site.

Twist: None.

A5.2 · Applications overview: law vs non-lawMedium

Setup: You are non-law. A recruiter asks how you'd handle the SQE.

Trap: Over-apologising for your degree.

Say: "The SQE route means law and non-law graduates sit the same exams. I'd do the firm's sponsored preparation course first." (Facts on the route: Part 6.)

Why it works: It shows you know the route since September 2021 is the same for both.

Twist: "Why not convert first?" Hold: "The firm sponsors the course; I'd want to learn it the way the firm trains."

A5.3 · Applications overview: "Which other firms are you applying to?"Hard

Setup: A recruiter asks this casually over coffee.

Trap: Saying "only you" (not credible) or listing twenty.

Say: "A handful of firms strong in finance, mostly US and international firms in London. What links them is..." then the mechanism.

Why it works: A coherent list is evidence of a coherent reason.

Twist: "So why us first?" Hold: Part 12's thirty-second answer.

A6.1 · Networking: opening a conversationEasy

Setup: A lawyer is standing alone with a drink.

Trap: Waiting to be approached, or opening with your CV.

Say: "Hi, I'm [name], I'm at [university]. Thank you for the session. Which part of the deep dive is closest to what you actually do?"

Why it works: People like talking about their own work. You learn the thing you need.

Twist: They're a business services person, not a lawyer. Hold: just as interested: "How does your team work with the lawyers?" (Part 3 explains why that question is good.)

A6.2 · Networking: joining a crowded groupMedium

Setup: One partner, eight candidates.

Trap: Interrupting to ask your question, or standing silently.

Say: Listen. Build on someone else's question: "Following on from that..." Then step back and let someone else in.

Why it works: Generosity is visible. Partners notice who listens.

Twist: The partner asks you directly. Hold: one short answer and one question back.

A6.3 · Networking: "Ask me anything"Hard

Setup: A senior lawyer says: "Go on, what do you really want to know?"

Trap: Asking about pay, or freezing.

Say: "What separates the trainees who get kept on from the ones who don't?" or "What did you get wrong in your first year that you'd warn me about?"

Why it works: These are the questions they enjoy answering and you most need answered.

Twist: They turn it: "And what will you get wrong?" Hold: one honest weakness and the habit that manages it.

B. The trainee-role lines

These mirror what Mayer Brown says its trainees do (Part 1) and what is common across City firms. Use them for the assessment centre and partner interview, not just the masterclass.

T1.1 · Research: a quick question from an associateEasy

Setup: "Can you find out whether a company can do X under its articles? Need it by four."

Trap: Disappearing until four and returning with ten pages.

Say: Confirm the question and the format ("a short email with the answer first?"). Check in at two if it's harder than expected. Lead with the answer, then the source.

Why it works: Answer first, then reasoning, is how busy lawyers read.

Twist: You find two sources that disagree. Hold: say so; don't pick silently.

T1.2 · Research: you can't find an answerMedium

Setup: Three hours in, no clear authority.

Trap: Pretending you found one, or saying nothing.

Say: "I haven't found a direct answer. Here is what I've checked, the closest material, and my view. Shall I ask the knowledge lawyer?"

Why it works: Firms have knowledge teams (Part 3). Using them is good process.

Twist: "Just give me your best guess." Hold: give it, and label it as a guess.

T1.3 · Research: you realise your earlier note was wrongHard

Setup: Your note went to the client yesterday. Today you see a later rule change.

Trap: Hoping no one notices.

Say: Tell your supervisor now, with the correction drafted.

Why it works: The Code requires honesty with clients when things go wrong [S342]; early is cheap, late is expensive.

Twist: "Won't that embarrass us?" Hold: "Less than the client finding it."

T2.1 · Drafting: board minutesEasy

Setup: You are asked to draft board minutes approving a loan.

Trap: Starting from a blank page.

Say: Ask for the firm precedent, check names, dates and figures against the term sheet, mark anything you're unsure of in square brackets.

Why it works: Precedents and square brackets are how drafting is done safely.

Twist: The precedent is from another jurisdiction. Hold: flag it.

T2.2 · Drafting: conflicting commentsMedium

Setup: The associate and the client send conflicting changes to the same clause.

Trap: Picking one.

Say: Make neither; send the associate a short note showing both and ask which applies.

Why it works: The decision belongs to the person responsible for the advice.

Twist: The associate is on leave. Hold: ask the partner, not the client.

T2.3 · Drafting: the wrong version goes outHard

Setup: You sent the other side an earlier draft containing internal comments.

Trap: Asking them to delete it and telling no one.

Say: Tell the associate immediately. They decide how to deal with the other side. Write down what happened.

Why it works: Composure scenario 4 below is this case. Confidentiality and honesty both bite.

Twist: "How will you stop it again?" Hold: name a concrete habit (check the attachment name before sending).

T3.1 · Due diligence: reviewing contractsEasy

Setup: Review 60 leases for change of control clauses.

Trap: Reading every word of every lease with no system.

Say: Agree the template for the report, then work through in a table: document, clause, what it says, risk rating.

Why it works: Diligence reports feed the client's decision; consistency is what makes them usable.

Twist: An AI review tool is available. Hold: use it, then check its outputs on a sample.

T3.2 · Due diligence: a missing documentMedium

Setup: The data room lacks a key contract.

Trap: Assuming it doesn't exist.

Say: Note it as a gap and suggest a request to the seller's lawyers.

Why it works: Gaps are findings.

Twist: Deadline tomorrow. Hold: report with the gap flagged.

T3.3 · Due diligence: a red flagHard

Setup: You see emails suggesting a payment to an official.

Trap: Burying it in row 47 of the report.

Say: Tell your supervisor the same day, outside the report.

Why it works: Possible bribery is a legal risk for the client and may raise anti-money-laundering duties for the firm; that is for seniors and compliance to judge.

Twist: "Should we tell the client?" Hold: "That's a call for the partner and compliance."

T4.1 · Deals: running a CP checklistEasy

Setup: You own the conditions precedent (CP) checklist for a loan.

Trap: Updating it only when asked.

Say: Update daily, circulate with what's outstanding and who owes it.

Why it works: The checklist is how everyone knows when the deal can close.

Twist: Twenty parties. Hold: same method, prioritised by what blocks closing.

T4.2 · Deals: a signature page arrives unsignedMedium

Setup: Late evening, one page is missing a signature.

Trap: Marking it as received.

Say: Tell the associate; chase the signatory's lawyer.

Why it works: An unsigned page means no binding signature.

Twist: "Just release; they'll sign tomorrow." Hold: composure scenario 2.

T4.3 · Deals: two deals close the same nightHard

Setup: Two associates both need you from 6pm.

Trap: Saying yes to both without telling either.

Say: Composure scenario 1.

Why it works: Transparency lets seniors allocate.

Twist: Both say theirs is more urgent. Hold: go to whoever staffs you.

T5.1 · Disputes: preparing a bundleEasy

Setup: Prepare a hearing bundle for tomorrow.

Trap: Starting without checking the court's rules on format.

Say: Check the practice direction and the index agreed with the other side.

Why it works: Courts set bundle rules; mistakes waste the judge's time.

Twist: Late document. Hold: ask whether it needs the other side's agreement.

T5.2 · Disputes: a privileged document in a disclosure batchMedium

Setup: Reviewing documents for disclosure, you find legal advice to the client.

Trap: Leaving it in the batch.

Say: Flag as potentially privileged and ask.

Why it works: Privilege protects the client; losing it can't be undone.

Twist: It's also harmful to your client. Hold: privilege is the test, not harm.

T5.3 · Disputes: a document hurts the clientHard

Setup: A non-privileged email damages your client's case.

Trap: Suggesting it be left out.

Say: It goes in if it's within the disclosure order. Tell the associate so the team can prepare.

Why it works: Solicitors must not mislead the court [S342]; disclosure duties are owed to the court.

Twist: "The client will be furious." Hold: priority order tier 1.

T6.1 · Communication: client calls you directlyEasy

Setup: A client calls you with a question.

Trap: Answering off the cuff.

Say: "Let me check with [associate] and come back to you by [time]." Then do.

Why it works: Fast, honest, and protects the advice.

Twist: "It's a simple yes or no." Hold: same answer, shorter time.

T6.2 · Communication: an overloaded weekMedium

Setup: Your supervisor gives you a third task. You can't do all three well.

Trap: Saying yes and missing one.

Say: "Happy to. I've also got X and Y due Thursday. Which should move?"

Why it works: Capacity is information the team needs.

Twist: "All of them." Hold: ask whether someone else can help on one.

T6.3 · Communication: a cross-border call goes wrongHard

Setup: On a call with colleagues in another office, you realise the two teams are working from different versions.

Trap: Waiting until after the call.

Say: "Sorry to interrupt: can we check we're both on version 4?"

Why it works: Mayer Brown runs cross-border deals across many offices (Part 2); version control is a real risk.

Twist: A partner is talking. Hold: still say it, briefly.

T7.1 · Knowledge and BD: writing a client alertEasy

Setup: Help with a short update on a new rule.

Trap: Describing the rule without saying who it affects.

Say: What changed, who it affects, what they should do, by when.

Why it works: Firms publish insights to stay useful to clients between deals.

Twist: Word limit. Hold: cut history first.

T7.2 · Pro bono: a deadline clashMedium

Setup: A pro bono deadline clashes with fee-earning work.

Trap: Treating pro bono as optional.

Say: Tell both supervisors and agree a plan. Pro bono clients are clients.

Why it works: Same duties apply whether or not the client pays.

Twist: "Drop the pro bono." Hold: ask who will cover it.

T7.3 · BD: a friend asks about a dealHard

Setup: A friend in finance asks, "Is your firm on the X takeover?"

Trap: Hinting.

Say: "I can't talk about client work, sorry." Same answer whether it's true or not.

Why it works: Decision tree 2.

Twist: "It's public anyway." Hold: "Then you don't need me to confirm it."

The composure set: five hard scenarios with decision trees

1. Two senior people want you at the same time

Setup: Two associates on different deals each need you tonight. Both say theirs is urgent.

Path: (1) Don't promise both. (2) Tell each of them about the other, with the facts: what each task is and when it's due. (3) If they can't agree, go to the person who allocates your work (often the supervising partner or a trainee manager). (4) Do what's decided and tell both. Line: "I want to get both right, so I'd rather be honest about the clash now than miss one at midnight."

2. A senior person asks you to skip a control

{{FIG:tree_control}}

3. You hear something that might be confidential or inside information

{{FIG:tree_info}}

At the masterclass: if a speaker mentions a live deal "off the record", do not repeat it, post it or use it in your application. Use only what the firm has published.

4. A mistake you made

Path: (1) Stop the damage if you safely can (for example, recall the email). (2) Tell your supervisor within the hour, with facts and a suggested fix. (3) Write a short note of what happened. (4) Change one habit. Line: "I'd rather tell you now with a fix than have you find out later." The SRA Code requires you to be honest and open with clients if things go wrong [S342]; the firm, not the trainee, decides how to handle the client.

5. Acting on incomplete information against a deadline

Path: (1) Identify what is irreversible (filing, sending, signing) and what is not (drafting, preparing). (2) Do everything reversible now. (3) For the irreversible step, get a decision from someone with authority; keep trying different routes. (4) If the deadline passes with no decision, the cost of missing a deadline is usually smaller than the cost of acting without authority, but say so to the person in charge in writing. Line: "I'd get everything ready so the decision is the only thing left."

The CV grill

How it works: an interviewer picks one CV line and goes three levels down. Level 1: "Tell me about this." Level 2: "What exactly did you do?" Level 3: "What would you do differently, and why didn't you?" Most candidates fail at level 2 by saying "we".

AttackFrame-holding answer pattern
"What did you personally do?"Use "I" and a verb: "I built the spreadsheet, I ran the weekly call." Then credit the team in one clause.
"What was the result, in numbers?"Have one number per bullet ready. If none exists, say what changed and how you know.
"That sounds small.""It was. What it taught me was X, and that scales."
"Why did you leave / stop?"Forward reason, not backward: "I wanted to try X."
"What went wrong?"A real thing, your share of it, the fix.
"How does this relate to law?"Map to a trainee skill: detail, deadlines, explaining complex things simply, working under supervision.
"Your grade here is low."Fact, context, trajectory. One sentence each.
"Everyone says they're a team player.""Fair. Here's a time I put the team's deadline ahead of my own credit..."
Four-line drill for every CV bullet1. Situation in one sentence. 2. What I did (three verbs). 3. The result (a number or a concrete change). 4. What I'd do differently, and what it taught me that a trainee needs. Write all four lines for every bullet. Say them out loud twice.
