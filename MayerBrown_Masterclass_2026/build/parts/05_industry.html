Part 5

The industry from zero

What a commercial law firm sells, who buys it, how firms make money, how the different kinds of firm differ, and the forces changing all of it. Read this first if you have never studied law.

What a commercial law firm sells

A commercial law firm sells two things to businesses, banks, funds, insurers, pension schemes and governments. First, advice: what the law says, what the risk is, what to do. Second, execution: getting a deal or a case done. That means drafting and negotiating documents, running the process, and making sure nothing is missed. Typical work is buying and selling companies (M&A), lending and borrowing (finance), turning loans into bonds (securitisation), contracts that move financial risk (derivatives), disputes (litigation and arbitration), and regulation.

Key term: solicitorA lawyer regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA) who advises clients directly. Barristers are mostly advocates in court and are usually instructed by solicitors. A training contract is how you become a solicitor at a firm.

The client is usually a company's in-house legal team. Thomson Reuters' 2026 UK report puts it bluntly: for UK buyers, "legal expertise is now the price of entry, not the point of differentiation". Clients choose firms for commercial judgement and practical guidance [S167] Confirmed. The same report says UK buyers are "more cautious about external legal spend than they have been at any point in the last five years" [S167].

How big the market is

TheCityUK puts UK legal services revenue at £52.3bn in 2024, up 11%. The top 100 firms took £37bn. The sector employs 364,000 people and ran an £8.9bn trade surplus. The UK is the largest legal market in Europe and second in the world after the US. More than 200 foreign firms from over 40 jurisdictions practise in London [S164] Confirmed. The Law Society uses a narrower measure and reports £34bn [S165] Reported. If you quote a number, say which one.

Say this in the interview"London is one of the two big centres for international legal work, partly because English law is chosen for so many cross-border contracts. That's why a Chicago-founded firm has had a London office for over two decades."

How a law firm makes money

{{FIG:pyramid}}

A firm bills time. Revenue is hours billed multiplied by the hourly rate, less what clients refuse to pay. Profit is what is left after salaries, rent, technology and insurance. The owners, the equity partners, share the profit. Profit per equity partner (PEP) is the profit divided by the number of equity partners. It is how the market compares firms, but it is not anyone's actual pay [S212].

Leverage is the number of lawyers per partner. One partner supervises several associates and trainees, so the firm bills many more hours than the partner could alone. This is why AI matters: it attacks the hours at the bottom of the pyramid.

Key termsBillable hour: time recorded against a client matter. Utilisation: share of available time that is billed. Realisation: share of recorded time that is actually paid. Lockstep: partners paid by seniority. Eat what you kill: partners paid by what they bring in. Non-equity partner: partner title, mainly salaried [S212][S213].

Mayer Brown's 2025 numbers show the pyramid at work: revenue $2.17bn, net income $762.5m, PEP $3.196m, revenue per lawyer $1.25m. It billed fewer hours (about 2.8m against 2.91m) and fewer clients, but raised rates by more than 10% [S220] Confirmed.

The kinds of firm, and where Mayer Brown fits

{{FIG:firm_types}}

The Magic Circle are the five London-headquartered elite firms. Clifford Chance's revenue rose 9% to £2.6bn in the year to April 2026, and Linklaters' rose to £2.5bn [S168]. US firms in London have grown faster: among London's top 50, US firms grew revenue 14.7% against 7.1% for UK firms, and 14 of the 18 London firms with revenue per lawyer above £1m are American [S168] Reported. Transatlantic mergers join a UK and a US firm (Part 7, story 8). Global firms such as Norton Rose Fulbright and Baker McKenzie have very large office networks. Mid-tier and boutique firms compete on price or a speciality.

Mayer Brown is a US firm, founded in Chicago in 1881, whose London office came from merging with the UK firm Rowe & Maw in 2002 [S138]. It pays London trainees and NQs at Magic Circle level, not at the top US level (below).

The forces changing the industry now

{{FIG:shift_map}}

1. Generative AI

On 6 May 2025 the SRA authorised Garfield.Law, the first law firm in England and Wales to deliver regulated services through AI. It helps recover small unpaid debts; named solicitors remain accountable. SRA chief executive Paul Philip called it "a landmark moment" [S159] Confirmed. Legal AI vendors have raised money at high valuations: Harvey at $11bn and Legora at $5.55bn, both reported in March 2026 [S180][S181] Reported. Clients are adopting faster than firms: 53% of UK corporate legal respondents report organisation-wide AI use, against about 35% of law firm respondents [S167]. Mayer Brown's own response is a mandatory GenAI curriculum (Part 7, story 5).

2. The pay war

{{FIG:nq_pay}}

Newly qualified (NQ) pay in the City now runs from about £140k to £189k at the firms in this chart. The Magic Circle pays £150k; Mayer Brown moved to £150k in July 2025 [S234]. Legal Cheek's September 2026 table puts the average across the firms it tracks at about £119,000 [S120] Reported.

3. Consolidation

A&O Shearman went live on 1 May 2024 with about 4,000 lawyers [S174]; Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer on about 1 June 2025 [S175]; McDermott Will & Schulte on 1 August 2025 [S176]; Hogan Lovells Cadwalader on 1 July 2026 [S240]; Ashurst Perkins Coie around mid-2026 [S178][S239] Reported. The logic: bigger platforms, US reach for UK firms, and more cross-selling.

4. Outside capital

Since the Legal Services Act 2007, non-lawyers can own UK law firms through "alternative business structures" (ABSs); 13% of firms in England and Wales are ABSs [S164]. In 2025, 19 of 62 UK law firm M&A deals (31%) involved private equity, mostly at the consumer and mid-market end [S166] Reported. In the US, investors use "management services organisation" structures because most US states bar non-lawyers from sharing legal fees; Arizona abolished that rule from 1 January 2021 [S186][S188].

5. Clients buying judgement, not hours

Thomson Reuters finds UK buyers value commercial judgement and practical guidance, and want AI to improve quality before it cuts cost [S167]. Commentators expect hybrid fees (caps, blended rates, portfolio pricing) rather than the end of the billable hour [S185] Reported.
