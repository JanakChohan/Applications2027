A. The founding insight

In 1881 "Adolf Kraus and Levy Mayer form a partnership in Chicago" [S70] Confirmed. The firm became Mayer, Meyer, Austrian & Platt in 1909 and Mayer, Brown & Platt in 1970. Separately, in 1895, Rowe & Maw was founded in London [S70]. In 2002 the two combined as Mayer, Brown, Rowe & Maw, shortened to Mayer Brown in 2007 [S70].

The insight for you: London is not a US outpost bolted on in 2002. It is a 130-year-old English practice that a Chicago firm merged into. The firm marked "its 130th anniversary in the City of London" in 2025 [S222]. The London head in April 2026 framed it the same way: Legal Business headlined his interview "We are not an outpost" [S303] (paywalled; headline read only).

The firm's own description of its purpose: "The world's most sophisticated major corporations, funds, and financial institutions rely on Mayer Brown ... to represent them in their most important and complex transactions and disputes" [S79]. It publishes client-service principles rather than a mission statement, including "Collaborating across offices to deliver the best of our knowledge, wherever in the world it's needed" [S79].

B. How the firm makes money

Growth in 2025 came from price and mix, not volume. Billed hours fell about 4%, revenue rose about 10%, billing rates rose more than 10%, and the number of clients billed fell 17% [S220] Confirmed (percentages inferred). Context: the 2026 Am Law 100 average PEP was reported at $3.59m [S118], so Mayer Brown's $3.196m is below the US average but well above the Magic Circle (Part 4). Bloomberg Law described the firm as likely among the 30 largest US firms [S86] Reported.

I. The skeletons, told straight

Refco (the serious one). Refco, a commodities broker, collapsed in October 2005, two months after its stock market listing, when it emerged that the chief executive had hidden a receivable of more than $1bn owed by a company he controlled. Joseph Collins, a Mayer Brown partner, was Refco's "primary outside counsel" [S94]. Prosecutors said lawyers under his direction drafted documents for loans that hid the debt. His 2009 conviction was overturned in 2012 because the judge had spoken privately with a juror; he was convicted again at retrial in November 2012 and sentenced in July 2013 to one year and one day [S94][S95][S96] Confirmed. The firm settled the Refco trustee's claims for an undisclosed sum in 2013 [S99]. One investor case against the firm, Pacific Investment Management v Mayer Brown (2nd Cir. 2010), is now a leading US case limiting advisers' liability for statements not attributed to them [S98] Reported.

GM (the trainee lesson). In 2008, closing papers that Mayer Brown prepared for General Motors, to pay off one loan, also terminated the security filing for a separate $1.5bn term loan. JPMorgan and its lawyers approved them. In 2015 the Second Circuit held the security really was terminated [S103]. The lenders then sued Mayer Brown, and lost: in 2017 the Seventh Circuit held "Mayer Brown did not owe a duty to plaintiffs" [S102] Confirmed. No settlement was found.

Hong Kong, 2021. Acting for the University of Hong Kong, the firm wrote to the custodians of the "Pillar of Shame" Tiananmen memorial about its removal; after criticism, it stopped acting on the matter [S111] Reported.

August 2026. The firm said "one of its personnel mistakenly sent a small number of documents to an unauthorised third party" who had misrepresented their identity, and that the third party "did not access our systems" [S105]. Do not call it a hack.

No SRA or Solicitors Disciplinary Tribunal sanction against the London LLP was found, but the register itself could not be checked Gap.

Part 3

Why the firm is built this way

Four questions, answered with evidence: why this structure; how the teams actually work together; what clients are really buying; and what the non-fee-earning side looks like and how it is wired to the lawyers.

1. Why is the business structured this way?

The structure. A group of "associated legal practices that are separate entities" [S75]: a US LLP, the English LLP (which also runs Tokyo), Hong Kong, a Singapore joint venture and an association in Brazil. In London, the practice mix is weighted to finance products sold to institutions (asset-based lending, derivatives, trade finance, securitisation), plus real estate and construction disputes [S139][S140].

The theory: what the structure buys

  1. Depth in asset-focused finance. Top tier in London for asset-based lending (13th consecutive year), derivatives and structured products (new this year) and trade finance [S139]. These are repeat products for repeat bank clients, which fits "go deeper with fewer clients" [S220].
  2. Products that need several teams at once. A pension buy-in needs pensions, insurance, derivatives and finance lawyers together (figure below). A firm with only one of those can't run it alone.
  3. Credible transatlantic execution. English-law and New York-law finance from one platform: "a fully integrated platform for New York and English law debt and equity solutions" [S328]; 96 London-New York deals in 2025 [S302].
  4. A small cohort. About 15 trainees a year. The training principal: "One of the advantages of a training contract at Mayer Brown is that it is a small cohort ... we can develop a bespoke programme" [S16].

What the structure costs

  1. Less brand in private equity and M&A. Legal 500 ranks London premium M&A in tier 6 and mid-market PE in tier 3 [S146]. The firm is importing sponsor-side talent rather than having grown it [S327].
  2. Fewer NQ jobs per practice because the intake is small.
  3. A smaller Asia platform since 2024 [S70][S220].
  4. Lower London profitability than the global headline. Average profit per LLP member was £861,333 in the year to April 2025 [S78], a different measure from the $3.196m global PEP; treat the gap as directional only.

2. How do the teams work together, and where does collaboration actually live?

Read the firm's own words precisely. Its principles say "Collaborating across offices to deliver the best of our knowledge, wherever in the world it's needed" and "Building diverse teams of lawyers from our market-leading practices to help achieve our clients' goals" [S79]. Those are about teams on a client's matter, not about a shared pool of ideas. Collaboration lives in three concrete places:

  • Cross-practice products. The Pensions Finance practice combines pensions with "finance, financial services, insurance, and derivatives lawyers" as a "one-stop shop" [S314].
  • Cross-office deal teams. The Zankore AI financing was led from Singapore with London on hedging and Washington and Dubai on sanctions and export controls [S231]. Former London head Dominic Griffiths: "We set out to build something distinctive - a platform where the best lawyers collaborate across borders" [S301].

What a commercial law firm sells

A commercial law firm sells two things to businesses, banks, funds, insurers, pension schemes and governments. First, advice: what the law says, what the risk is, what to do. Second, execution: getting a deal or a case done. That means drafting and negotiating documents, running the process, and making sure nothing is missed. Typical work is buying and selling companies (M&A), lending and borrowing (finance), turning loans into bonds (securitisation), contracts that move financial risk (derivatives), disputes (litigation and arbitration), and regulation.

Key term: solicitorA lawyer regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA) who advises clients directly. Barristers are mostly advocates in court and are usually instructed by solicitors. A training contract is how you become a solicitor at a firm.

The client is usually a company's in-house legal team. Thomson Reuters' 2026 UK report puts it bluntly: for UK buyers, "legal expertise is now the price of entry, not the point of differentiation". Clients choose firms for commercial judgement and practical guidance [S167] Confirmed. The same report says UK buyers are "more cautious about external legal spend than they have been at any point in the last five years" [S167].

How big the market is

TheCityUK puts UK legal services revenue at £52.3bn in 2024, up 11%. The top 100 firms took £37bn. The sector employs 364,000 people and ran an £8.9bn trade surplus. How a law firm makes money

A firm bills time. Revenue is hours billed multiplied by the hourly rate, less what clients refuse to pay. Profit is what is left after salaries, rent, technology and insurance. The owners, the equity partners, share the profit. Profit per equity partner (PEP) is the profit divided by the number of equity partners. It is how the market compares firms, but it is not anyone's actual pay [S212].

Leverage is the number of lawyers per partner. One partner supervises several associates and trainees, so the firm bills many more hours than the partner could alone. This is why AI matters: it attacks the hours at the bottom of the pyramid.

Key termsBillable hour: time recorded against a client matter. Utilisation: share of available time that is billed. Realisation: share of recorded time that is actually paid. Lockstep: partners paid by seniority. Eat what you kill: partners paid by what they bring in. Non-equity partner: partner title, mainly salaried [S212][S213].

The scenario playbook

How to answer any hypothetical without losing your footing, then 39 practice cards, five composure scenarios with decision trees, and a CV grill. This is method, so most of it carries the Inferred tag; where it leans on a rule, the rule is cited.

Why scenarios matter at this firm and this event

At the masterclass itself nobody will "interview" you. But three things on the agenda behave like an interview: the trainee panel (where your question is judged by everyone in the room), the practice deep dive (where a partner may throw a hypothetical at the audience), and networking (where every conversation is a two-minute assessment). Then, if the event goes well, the training contract process follows. Law firm assessment centres commonly use a case study, a written exercise and a partner interview, and partners like to push a candidate with "what would you do if" questions to see whether they stay calm and sensible (see Part 9 for what is known about Mayer Brown's own process).

The aim of this part is not to memorise answers. It is to build one stable way of thinking that you can apply to anything: a set order of priorities, and a four-step routine that turns that order into a spoken answer.

The four-step protocol: steady, sort, act, close

1. Steady (about 5 seconds)

Take one breath. Then restate the question in one line. This buys time and proves you heard the actual question, not the one you prepared for.

"So I've got a signing tonight, the associate is unreachable, and the other side wants the documents now."

2. Sort (about 15 seconds)

Name the two or three things in tension, then rank them using the priority order below. Saying the ranking out loud is what interviewers mark.

"Two things matter: getting the deal signed on time, and not releasing anything I'm not authorised to release. The second comes first."

3. Act (25 to 40 seconds)

Give the concrete next step: who you call, what you send, by when. Trainees are judged on initiative within limits, so show both halves: you move, and you escalate.

"I'd try the associate on phone and Teams, then the supervising partner. Meanwhile I'd get everything else ready so we lose no time once someone says yes."

4. Close (5 to 10 seconds)

One sentence of principle. Then stop. Silence after a good answer is fine (see tactic 3).

"So: authority first, then speed, and nobody is surprised."

The priority order when good things collide

Every hard trainee scenario is a clash between good things: helping the client, pleasing a senior colleague, hitting a deadline, following the process. The order above settles almost all of them. It mirrors the way the solicitors' regulator, the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA), frames a solicitor's duties: the seven SRA Principles require you to act with honesty and integrity, in a way that upholds public trust, and in the best interests of each client, and where Principles conflict, those that safeguard the wider public interest take precedence over an individual client's interests [S341] Confirmed. Speed and convenience are not Principles at all.

Say this in the interview"I'd protect the rules and the client first, keep the record straight, and then go as fast as that allows."

Eight ways interviewers break a frame, and the counters

Partners test whether a view survives pressure. A good candidate changes their mind when given a new fact, and holds it when given only pressure. Say which one is happening: "If that's the case, I'd change my answer to..." versus "I understand the pressure, but I'd still..."

Staying calm physically

  • Breathe out longer than you breathe in before you speak (four in, six out). It slows the heart rate and stops the rushed first sentence.
  • Feet flat, hands still and visible. At networking, hold your drink in your left hand so your right is free to shake.
  • Pause before the answer, not during it. A two-second pause reads as thought. Fillers ("um, like") read as panic.
  • Have a written fallback line for a blank: "Can I take ten seconds to think about that?" Then use all ten.
  • Lower your pitch at the end of the sentence. Rising endings sound like questions.

(3) If they can't agree, go to the person who allocates your work (often the supervising partner or a trainee manager). (4) Do what's decided and tell both. Line: "I want to get both right, so I'd rather be honest about the clash now than miss one at midnight."

2. A senior person asks you to skip a control

3. You hear something that might be confidential or inside information

At the masterclass: if a speaker mentions a live deal "off the record", do not repeat it, post it or use it in your application. Use only what the firm has published.

4. A mistake you made

Path: (1) Stop the damage if you safely can (for example, recall the email). (2) Tell your supervisor within the hour, with facts and a suggested fix. (3) Write a short note of what happened. (4) Change one habit. Line: "I'd rather tell you now with a fix than have you find out later." 5. Acting on incomplete information against a deadline

Path: (1) Identify what is irreversible (filing, sending, signing) and what is not (drafting, preparing). (2) Do everything reversible now. (3) For the irreversible step, get a decision from someone with authority; keep trying different routes. (4) If the deadline passes with no decision, the cost of missing a deadline is usually smaller than the cost of acting without authority, but say so to the person in charge in writing. Line: "I'd get everything ready so the decision is the only thing left."
