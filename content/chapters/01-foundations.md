# Foundations from first principles

This chapter teaches the minimum you need before any other chapter makes sense. It assumes no finance background. Each idea is taught once: a plain-English version first, then the mechanics a professional would use. Later chapters build on these ideas without repeating them.

Every number in an example is invented to show the arithmetic and is tagged {{IL}}. Nothing in this chapter describes Goldman Sachs's actual fees, clients or contracts.

For each important idea the chapter answers the same seven questions: what is it, why does it exist, who uses it, what actually happens, how does money move, what can go wrong, and why does it matter to a client coverage role.

## 1. Assets, liabilities and why anyone invests

### Assets and liabilities

:::l1
An **asset** is something you own that is worth money or will pay you money: cash, a share in a company, a loan you have made to someone, a building.

A **liability** is something you owe: a mortgage, a bill, a promise to pay someone a pension for the rest of their life.

Almost every investor that an asset manager serves has both. The assets exist *because of* the liabilities. A pension scheme does not hold £1 billion for its own sake. It holds £1 billion because it has promised to pay pensions that will cost roughly that much.

This is the single most useful habit in this field: when you meet an investor, ask what they owe, to whom, and when. The answer explains most of what they buy.
:::

:::l2
Professionals compare the two sides directly.

- **Funding level** = value of assets ÷ value of liabilities. A scheme with £950m of assets and £1,000m of liabilities is 95% funded {{IL}}.
- Liabilities are future payments, so they must be converted into a value today. This is called **discounting**. The further away a payment is, and the higher the interest rate used (the **discount rate**), the less it is worth today.
- That creates a non-obvious result: when long-term interest rates fall, the present value of long-dated liabilities rises, even though nobody has promised to pay a penny more. An investor can lose ground while its assets go up, if its liabilities go up faster.
- **Asset–liability management (ALM)** is the discipline of choosing assets with the liabilities in mind rather than in isolation.
:::

:::example Why a "good year" can be a bad year
A scheme's assets rise 6% in a year, from £1,000m to £1,060m. Over the same year long-term interest rates fall and the value placed on its pension promises rises 12%, from £1,000m to £1,120m. The funding level falls from 100% to about 95% (1,060 ÷ 1,120). The trustees are worse off against their real goal, despite a positive return. {{IL}}
:::

### Who has money to invest, and why

:::l1
Six kinds of owner appear again and again. What separates them is not how much money they have but what the money is *for*.
:::

| Owner | Why the money exists | What it must pay, and when | What that usually demands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual savers | Retirement, a home, a safety net | Their own future spending; timing is personal | Simplicity, low cost, access |
| Pension schemes | To pay members' pensions | Monthly pensions for decades | Either matching promises (defined benefit) or growing pots (defined contribution) |
| Insurers | Premiums collected before claims are paid | Claims and annuity payments, under strict capital rules | Predictable income, high credit quality, capital efficiency |
| Endowments, foundations and charities | A gift meant to fund a mission indefinitely | Annual spending on the mission, ideally forever | Growth above inflation plus steady spending |
| Sovereign wealth funds | A state's surplus, often from oil, gas or trade | Varies: future generations, budget stabilisation, national development | Very long horizons, large size, access to private deals |
| Companies with cash | Money waiting to pay wages, suppliers, tax, dividends | Days to months | Safety and same-day access before return |

:::l2
The same grid tells you how each owner is governed, which matters as much as what it needs.

- A pension scheme is run by **trustees**, who have a legal duty to act for members and who usually meet only a few times a year.
- An insurer is run by executives under a prudential regulator, with a chief investment officer and an investment committee.
- A sovereign wealth fund (**SWF**) is run by professional staff under a state mandate.
- A corporate treasury is run by a treasurer under a board-approved investment policy that typically lists what the cash may be placed in.

Who decides, how often they meet, and what they are personally accountable for shape the sales and service process far more than the product does. Chapter 3 returns to this client by client.
:::

## 2. The asset manager and the people around it

### What an asset manager is

:::l1
An **asset manager** invests other people's money on their behalf, under instructions, for a fee.

Three points are easy to miss.

1. **The money stays the client's.** The manager decides what to buy and sell. It does not own the assets, and they are held separately by a **custodian**, a bank whose job is safekeeping.
2. **The manager is an agent.** It acts for the client within limits the client sets. Gains and losses belong to the client.
3. **The fee is usually a small percentage of the money managed.** So the manager earns more when it manages more money and when markets rise, and less when clients leave or markets fall.

An analogy: you own a car and hire a professional driver. The car is still yours, kept in your garage (the custodian). You tell the driver where you need to go and which roads to avoid (the guidelines). Where the analogy stops working: a driver is paid by the hour, but an asset manager is paid as a share of the car's value, which gives it a commercial interest in the pot being large. That interest is usually aligned with yours, but not always, and Chapter 7 deals with the cases where it is not.
:::

:::l2
**Why it exists.** Investing well needs scale, specialist skill, systems, market access and regulatory permissions that most asset owners cannot justify building. Pooling many clients spreads those fixed costs.

**How value moves.** The client pays a **management fee**, normally an annual percentage of the assets, accrued daily and billed monthly or quarterly. Some strategies add a **performance fee**. Trading costs, custody and administration are separate and are borne by the client's portfolio.

**What "assets under management" means.** **Assets under management (AUM)** is the total client money a firm manages. Some firms report a wider figure, **assets under supervision (AUS)**, which also counts assets where the firm earns a fee for advice or oversight without full discretion. Chapter 5 uses the exact definitions from Goldman Sachs's own filings.

**What can go wrong.** Underperformance against the agreed objective; breaching a guideline; operational errors in trading or reporting; a key investor leaving; or the manager growing a strategy beyond the size at which it can still be run well.

**Why it matters to this role.** Client coverage is the part of the asset manager that finds out what the client needs, brings the right internal specialists to the table, and remains accountable to the client afterwards. Coverage staff do not pick the investments. They are responsible for the client understanding what they have bought and for the firm understanding what the client actually needs.
:::

### How an asset manager differs from its neighbours

Beginners often blur five different businesses. The cleanest test is to ask: *whose money is at risk, and what is the firm paid for?*

| Business | What it does | Whose money is at risk | How it is paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset manager | Invests clients' money under a mandate | The client's | A percentage of assets, sometimes plus a performance fee |
| Bank (lending and investment banking) | Takes deposits, lends, advises companies on raising money and on deals | Partly its own balance sheet | Interest margin, advisory and underwriting fees |
| Broker or dealer (markets, "sales and trading") | Executes trades for investors and quotes prices | Its own capital when it takes the other side of a trade | Commissions and the gap between buying and selling prices |
| Investment consultant | Advises asset owners on strategy and on which managers to hire | Nobody's: it gives advice | Fees for advice, by retainer or project |
| Wealth adviser or wealth manager | Advises individuals and families and builds portfolios for them | The individual client's | Advice fees and a percentage of assets |

:::l2
The boundaries matter for this role in three practical ways.

- An asset manager is a **buy-side** firm: it buys investments for clients. Banks' markets divisions are the **sell side**: they sell trading and financing services *to* asset managers. A client coverage person at an asset manager is not a markets salesperson, even inside the same parent company.
- The **investment consultant** is neither client nor competitor in the simple sense. It advises the client on whether to hire you, and often rates your strategies. That is why asset managers have a dedicated function called **consultant relations**.
- A wealth manager can be a *client* of an asset manager. When a private bank selects a fund for its customers' portfolios, the asset manager is selling to a professional intermediary, not to the end saver. This is the business usually called third-party or wholesale distribution.
:::

### The chain of people between a saver and an investment

:::l1
The person whose retirement depends on the money almost never talks to the person investing it. In a UK pension scheme the chain typically looks like this:

**Member (beneficiary) → trustees → investment consultant → asset manager → markets**

Each link hires the next one to act on its behalf, and each link knows more about its own job than the one before it. Economists call this a **principal–agent chain**: a principal (who wants something done) relies on an agent (who does it) and cannot fully observe how well the agent is doing it.
:::

:::l2
Each link has its own incentives and its own constraints.

- **Members** want the pension they were promised. They have almost no say in investment.
- **Trustees** carry the legal responsibility. Many are not investment specialists. They must take proper advice and they are judged, in hindsight, on process as well as outcome.
- **Consultants** advise on strategy and recommend managers. Their research ratings act as a gate: an unrated or poorly rated strategy may never reach the shortlist.
- **Asset managers** want to win and keep assets, and are judged on performance, service and cost.

Because nobody in the chain can fully check the next link, the system relies on substitutes for direct observation: contracts, benchmarks, reporting, due diligence questionnaires, regulation, and reputation built over repeated dealings. Client coverage sits exactly at the joint between two links. Much of the job is reducing the information gap honestly, so that the principal can make a sound decision. Chapter 7 develops this at length.
:::

## 3. How money is handed over: funds, mandates and the contract

### Pooled funds and segregated mandates

:::l1
There are two basic ways to give an asset manager money.

- A **pooled fund** is a shared pot. Many investors buy units or shares in one fund, the manager runs one portfolio, and everyone gets the same strategy and the same rules. The value of one unit is the **net asset value (NAV)** per unit.
- A **segregated mandate** (also called a separate account) is a private pot. One client's assets are held in that client's own custody account and managed to rules written for that client alone.

A pooled fund is like taking a scheduled coach: cheaper, runs on a fixed route, leaves whether or not you are on it. A segregated mandate is like chartering your own coach: you set the route, but you need enough passengers to justify it. The analogy fails in one respect: in a pooled fund, other investors' behaviour can affect you, because large withdrawals by others can force the fund to sell assets.
:::

:::l2
| Feature | Pooled fund | Segregated mandate |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Units in a fund vehicle | The underlying securities directly |
| Customisation | None beyond choosing a share class | Benchmark, exclusions, risk limits, reporting all negotiable |
| Typical minimum | Low | High, often tens or hundreds of millions, because of fixed set-up cost |
| Documents | Prospectus and application form | A negotiated investment management agreement |
| Set-up effort | Days | Weeks to months: legal negotiation, custody account openings market by market, operational testing |
| Fee | Published by share class; large investors may get a cheaper class or a rebate | Individually negotiated, often tiered |

In Europe and the UK the most common regulated pooled fund for general distribution is the **UCITS** (Undertakings for Collective Investment in Transferable Securities), a fund built to a common rulebook on diversification, liquidity and disclosure. A fund's annual running cost to the investor is summarised as the **ongoing charges figure (OCF)**.

**What can go wrong.** In pooled funds: dealing restrictions or suspension if the assets cannot be sold as fast as investors want out. In segregated mandates: slow onboarding, a guideline written ambiguously, or a market the custodian has not opened in time for the first trade.

**Why it matters to this role.** The vehicle changes the work. Selling a fund to a wealth intermediary is about getting an existing product approved and then used. Winning a segregated institutional mandate is a negotiated project with legal, operational and investment workstreams that client coverage must coordinate.
:::

### The investment management agreement and guidelines

:::l1
A segregated mandate runs on a contract called the **investment management agreement (IMA)**. Attached to it are the **investment guidelines**: the client's written instructions about what the manager is trying to achieve and what it may not do.

The guidelines are the practical answer to the principal–agent problem. The client cannot watch every trade, so it writes down the boundaries in advance and asks for regular proof that they were respected.
:::

:::l2
A typical IMA and its schedules cover:

- **Objective and benchmark**: for example, outperform a named index by 1% a year over rolling three-year periods, before fees {{IL}}.
- **Permitted and prohibited investments**: asset types, credit quality floors, country or sector limits, exclusions.
- **Risk limits**: maximum position sizes, tracking error range, use of derivatives.
- **Fees**: the rate, any tiers, any performance fee and how it is calculated.
- **Reporting and meetings**: what the client receives, how often.
- **Termination**: notice period, often short. Many institutional mandates can be ended on roughly a month's notice or less.
- **Standard of care and liability**: what the manager is responsible for if it breaches.

Two consequences follow. First, a mandate is recurring revenue but not guaranteed revenue: the client can usually leave quickly. Second, a breach of guidelines, even one that made money, is a serious event that must be reported to the client, because the client did not consent to that risk.
:::

:::example A guideline in practice
A mandate says: "No more than 5% of the portfolio in any one issuer." A bond the portfolio holds is upgraded and rallies; a second bond from the same issuer is already held. Together they drift to 5.3% of the portfolio without a single trade. That is a *passive* breach. The portfolio team must bring it back within the limit in an agreed time, and the client is told. The coverage person does not fix the breach; they make sure the client hears about it promptly, in plain terms, with the remedy and the date. {{IL}}
:::

## 4. Measuring the job: benchmarks, risk, liquidity and time

### Benchmarks, and active versus passive

:::l1
A **benchmark** is the yardstick a portfolio is measured against, usually a market **index**: a published list of securities and their weights, such as the largest listed companies in a country.

- A **passive** (or index) manager tries to *match* the benchmark as closely and cheaply as possible.
- An **active** manager tries to *beat* it by choosing differently, and charges more for trying.

The extra return above the benchmark is called **alpha**. The return you get simply from being exposed to the market is called **beta**. Beta is cheap to buy. Alpha is uncertain and is what active fees pay for.
:::

:::l2
- Active returns across all investors sum to roughly the market return before costs, so the *average* active manager, after fees, lags the index. That arithmetic is the source of the long-running shift towards passive and of pressure on active fees.
- Active management is easier to defend where indexes are hard to replicate or where the benchmark itself is flawed: much of fixed income, smaller companies, and private markets where no investable index exists.
- A benchmark can be the client's own liabilities instead of a market index. Then "doing well" means keeping pace with what the client owes, not beating other managers.
- **Choosing the benchmark is a client decision with consequences.** A manager measured against the wrong yardstick can look brilliant or terrible for reasons that have nothing to do with skill. Coverage staff are often the people who notice the benchmark no longer fits the client's situation.
:::

### Risk, four ways

:::l1
"Risk" means different things to different clients. Four measures cover most conversations.

1. **Volatility**: how much returns bounce around from period to period.
2. **Drawdown**: the fall from a peak to the next low. This is what loss feels like.
3. **Tracking error**: how differently the portfolio behaves from its benchmark. Low tracking error means it hugs the index.
4. **Liability-relative risk**: how much the gap between assets and liabilities can move. For a pension scheme this is the risk that matters most.
:::

:::l2
- Volatility is usually quoted as an annualised standard deviation. A fund with 10% volatility would be expected to land within about ±10% of its average return in roughly two years out of three, if returns behaved like a bell curve, which they do not reliably do {{IL}}.
- Tracking error is the volatility of the *difference* between portfolio and benchmark returns. A mandate targeting 1% outperformance with 2% tracking error is asking for an **information ratio** of 0.5 {{IL}}.
- A "safe" asset on one measure can be risky on another. Cash has almost no volatility, but for a pension scheme with 20-year promises, holding cash is a large liability-relative risk, because cash does not rise in value when the liabilities do.
- Other risks sit alongside these: **credit risk** (a borrower fails to pay), **counterparty risk** (the other side of a contract fails), **concentration**, **currency**, and **operational risk**.
:::

:::example The same portfolio, two verdicts
A portfolio of long-dated government bonds falls 20% in a year when interest rates rise sharply. For a saver who needs the money next year, that is a disaster. For a pension scheme whose liabilities also fell about 20% for the same reason, the funding level barely moved, which is exactly what the portfolio was hired to do. {{IL}}
:::

### Liquidity

:::l1
**Liquidity** is how quickly you can turn an investment into cash without accepting a poor price. Shares in large companies: seconds. A commercial building: months. A commitment to a private equity fund: usually years, and not at a time of your choosing.

Investors are normally paid extra for accepting illiquidity. The danger is needing cash at the moment the asset cannot be sold.
:::

:::l2
- **Liquidity mismatch** is the classic failure: a fund promises daily dealing but owns assets that take weeks to sell. If many investors ask to leave at once, the fund may have to suspend dealing.
- Institutions run a **liquidity budget**: how much of the portfolio can be locked up, given the cash they must pay out and the collateral they may be called on to post.
- Liquidity needs change. A pension scheme preparing to transfer its liabilities to an insurer suddenly cares far more about being able to sell, which can make previously sensible illiquid holdings a problem.
:::

### Investment horizons

:::l1
The **investment horizon** is how long the money can stay invested before it is needed. A long horizon lets an investor ride out falls and hold illiquid assets. A short horizon does not.

Horizon comes from the liabilities, not from preference. An endowment may think in decades; a corporate treasurer in days.
:::

:::l2
The stated horizon and the *governance* horizon often differ. Trustees with a 30-year liability may still review a manager every quarter and lose patience after three poor years. A good coverage professional keeps the client's attention on the horizon the strategy was designed for, and also says so plainly when underperformance has gone beyond what the strategy's design can explain.
:::

### Public and private markets

:::l1
**Public markets** are where securities trade openly with visible prices: listed shares and most bonds. **Private markets** are investments negotiated directly and not traded on an exchange: private equity (buying companies), private credit (lending directly), infrastructure, real estate.

Private assets can offer returns or income that public markets do not, in exchange for illiquidity, less frequent valuation and higher fees.
:::

:::l2
Private market funds work differently from ordinary funds, and the difference changes the fee arithmetic in Chapter 5.

- The investor makes a **commitment** (a promise of, say, £50m {{IL}}). The manager **calls** the money in stages over several years as it finds deals, and returns it through **distributions** as investments are sold.
- Management fees are often charged on **committed capital** during the investment period and on invested capital afterwards, not on NAV.
- The manager typically also earns **carried interest**: a share of profits, often around a fifth, payable only after investors have received their money back plus a minimum return called the **hurdle** {{IL}}.
- Valuations are estimates made quarterly, so reported volatility understates the real economic risk.
:::

## 5. How the manager gets paid: basis points and fees

### Basis points

:::l1
Fees in asset management are small percentages, so the industry counts in **basis points (bps)**. One basis point is one hundredth of one percent.

- 1 bp = 0.01%
- 100 bps = 1%
- 25 bps = 0.25%

The arithmetic you will use constantly:

**Annual fee = assets × fee rate**

£500 million at 20 bps = £500,000,000 × 0.0020 = £1,000,000 a year. {{IL}}
:::

:::l2
Four refinements turn the simple sum into what actually gets billed.

1. **Fees accrue on average assets over time, not on the opening balance.** If the £500m arrives halfway through the year, the first-year fee is about half. If markets fall 10% the day after funding, the fee base falls with them.
2. **Tiered fees (breakpoints).** Larger mandates pay a lower rate on each additional slice. Example: 25 bps on the first £200m and 15 bps on the rest. On £500m that is £500,000 + £450,000 = £950,000, an *effective* rate of 19 bps {{IL}}.
3. **Performance fees.** A share of the return above a benchmark or hurdle, often with a **high-water mark** so the manager is not paid twice for recovering an earlier loss.
4. **Most-favoured-nation (MFN) clauses.** A promise that the client's fee will be no worse than that of any comparable client. One discount can therefore reprice several relationships.

In pooled funds the investor sees an all-in ongoing charge deducted inside the fund. Different **share classes** of the same fund carry different charges for different types of investor. Where an intermediary or large investor negotiates a lower price, it is often delivered through a cheaper share class or a **rebate**.
:::

:::warn Revenue is not profit, and recurring is not guaranteed
A management fee is the manager's *gross* revenue. Out of it come portfolio management, trading, technology, operations, compliance, distribution and overheads. And because most mandates can be ended at short notice and the fee base moves with markets, a fee that recurs is not a fee that is guaranteed. Chapter 5 works this through.
:::

**Why it matters to this role.** Every commercial judgement in client coverage, such as whether a mandate is worth a custom build, what a fee concession really costs, or what a client's partial withdrawal means, starts with this arithmetic. You are expected to do it quickly and to state your assumptions.

## 6. Who the clients are, and why coverage exists

### Institutional, intermediary and retail

:::l1
An asset manager's clients fall into three broad groups, and each is reached in a different way.

- **Institutional clients** invest large sums on behalf of an organisation and its beneficiaries: pension schemes, insurers, endowments, sovereign funds, central banks, corporates.
- **Intermediaries** are professional firms that choose investments for *their* clients: private banks, wealth managers, financial advisers, fund platforms, funds of funds. The asset manager's customer is the intermediary; the money belongs to the intermediary's clients.
- **Retail investors** are individuals investing for themselves, usually through an adviser or an online platform.
:::

:::l2
| | Institutional | Intermediary (wholesale) | Retail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides | Trustees, investment committee, CIO, often on a consultant's advice | A fund selection team (the **gatekeeper**), then individual advisers or portfolio managers | The individual, or their adviser |
| Typical vehicle | Segregated mandate or institutional fund | Pooled fund, model portfolio, ETF | Pooled fund via a platform |
| Size per decision | Large and lumpy | Starts small, builds as advisers adopt | Small |
| Selection process | Formal: request for proposal, due diligence, presentations | Fund research, approved list (**buy list**), then internal promotion | Brand, ratings, platform availability |
| What "winning" means | Appointment, then funding | Approval, then *usage*: being on the list is permission to be bought, not a purchase | Net sales |

Two terms you will meet immediately:

- A **request for proposal (RFP)** is a formal invitation to bid for a mandate, with a long list of questions about the firm, team, process, performance, risk controls and fees.
- A **due diligence questionnaire (DDQ)** is a detailed questionnaire used to verify a manager before or after appointment. Answering RFPs and DDQs accurately and consistently is a core, unglamorous work product of any client business.
:::

### What "distribution" or client coverage is

:::l1
**Distribution** is the industry's word for everything involved in getting an asset manager's capabilities to clients and keeping those clients: finding prospects, understanding needs, proposing solutions, winning mandates, onboarding, reporting, and maintaining the relationship. Firms use different names for the department: client coverage, client solutions, client business, sales and client service.

Why does it need to exist at all? Why can't good investment performance sell itself?
:::

:::l2
Four reasons, each tied to something already taught.

1. **Search costs.** There are thousands of strategies. An asset owner cannot evaluate them all, and a portfolio manager cannot find and visit every potential client. Someone has to match need to capability.
2. **Information asymmetry.** The client cannot see inside the investment process. Coverage translates it, answers the hard questions, and brings the right specialist. The client's own protection against being misled is formal due diligence and, often, a consultant.
3. **The need is rarely "a product".** A scheme's real problem might be a funding gap, a cash-flow shortfall or a governance burden. Working out what is being asked for, and whether the firm has an honest answer, is skilled work.
4. **Mandates are terminable and needs change.** Someone must own the relationship after the sale: reviews, problems, changes in the client's situation, and the decision about what to propose next, or whether to propose anything.

**How money moves.** Coverage produces no investment return. Its commercial contribution is to the *quantity and durability* of fee-paying assets: new assets won, existing assets retained, and relationships widened. Those outcomes are produced jointly with investment teams, product specialists, legal and operations, so attributing a win to one person or function is always partly arbitrary.

**What can go wrong.** Selling something unsuitable; over-promising what a strategy can do; slow or inaccurate answers; a relationship that depends on one individual; or winning business the firm cannot serve well.

**The usual split.** Many firms divide coverage into **sales** (new clients and new assets) and **relationship management** (existing clients), with **consultant relations**, **product or investment specialists** and **RFP teams** alongside. How strict that split is varies by firm and team. Chapter 6 examines it without assuming a universal rule.
:::

## 7. Four client problems you will meet constantly

### Defined benefit and defined contribution pensions

:::l1
A workplace pension is one of two types.

- **Defined benefit (DB)**: the *pension* is promised. "You will receive a fraction of your salary for each year worked, for life." The employer bears the risk. If the investments fall short, the employer must pay in more.
- **Defined contribution (DC)**: the *payments in* are defined. You and your employer contribute to a pot in your name, and what you retire on depends on how that pot grows. The member bears the risk.

DB is a promise and therefore a liability to be matched. DC is a savings pot to be grown. They are different investment problems and are sold to and served differently.
:::

:::l2
**UK DB today.** The Pension Protection Fund's *Purple Book 2025* records 4,840 eligible private-sector DB schemes with about 8.6 million members at 31 March 2025, of whom only around 0.7 million are still building up benefits. Schemes held 71% of assets in bonds and bond proxies, and in aggregate showed a surplus of £214 billion on the PPF's own measure, a funding ratio of 125% {{V}} [@W1-S01]. In short: UK DB is large, mostly closed, mature, heavily in bonds, and in aggregate well funded on that measure. The PPF measure values a reduced level of benefits, so it flatters funding compared with the cost of insuring the full promises.

That maturity sets the agenda. A well-funded closed scheme is asking about its **endgame**: transfer the liabilities to an insurer through a **buy-in** (the scheme buys an insurance policy as an asset) or a **buyout** (the insurer takes over the promises and the scheme winds up), or **run on** and manage the scheme for the long term.

**Who governs.** Trustees run the scheme. **The Pensions Regulator (TPR)** describes itself as "the public body that protects workplace pensions in the UK" {{V}} [@W1-S02]. The strength of the employer's ability and willingness to support the scheme is called the **covenant**.

**UK DC today.** Employers must automatically enrol eligible workers, with a legal minimum total contribution of 8% of qualifying earnings, at least 3% of it from the employer {{SI}} [@W1-S07]. Most members never make an investment choice and sit in the scheme's **default fund**, so the commercial decision is made by whoever designs the default: a master trust, an insurer or the employer's trustees. DC is growing, is very price-sensitive, and is where the policy debate about investing in private markets is concentrated. Chapter 9 covers the current reforms.

**Public-sector DB.** The **Local Government Pension Scheme (LGPS)** in England and Wales is funded DB, run through 86 local administering authorities that invest through shared asset pools, which government policy is consolidating from eight to six {{SI}} [@W1-S08]. For an external manager the buyer is increasingly the pool, not the individual fund.

**Why it matters to this role.** "A UK pension client" is at least four different clients: a closed corporate DB scheme heading for insurance, a DB scheme choosing to run on, a DC default designer, and an LGPS pool. A proposal that suits one can be irrelevant to another.
:::

### Liability-driven investment (LDI) in plain English

:::l1
A DB scheme's biggest risk is not a stock market fall. It is that the value of its promises rises because long-term interest rates fall or expected inflation rises.

**Liability-driven investment (LDI)** means holding assets that move in step with the liabilities, so that when the liabilities go up, the assets go up too. The natural matching assets are long-dated government bonds (in the UK, **gilts**), including **index-linked gilts** whose payments rise with inflation.

Think of a fixed-rate mortgage. You fix the rate not because you predict rates will rise but because you do not want your finances to depend on it. LDI is the same idea in reverse for a scheme: removing a risk it is not paid to take. The analogy stops being accurate once leverage is involved, as explained below.
:::

:::l2
**The complication: leverage.** A scheme that is not fully funded cannot afford to put everything in gilts; it also needs growth assets to close the gap. So many schemes obtained gilt exposure *with borrowed money*, through gilt repurchase agreements (**repo**) or interest rate swaps. £1 of capital might support £2 to £4 of hedging {{IL}}. That freed capital for growth assets.

**How money moves.** Leveraged positions require **collateral**. When gilt yields rise (prices fall), the LDI portfolio must post more collateral, quickly. The scheme is not worse off overall, because its liabilities have fallen as well, but it needs *cash now*.

**What went wrong in 2022.** After the UK government's fiscal statement in late September 2022, long gilt yields rose faster than LDI funds could raise collateral. The Bank of England describes funds having to deleverage by selling gilts into a falling market, "and an adverse feedback loop took hold". The Bank bought £19.3 billion of gilts between 28 September and 14 October 2022 to restore order {{V}} [@W1-S05]. The lesson the industry took was about liquidity and leverage, not about hedging itself: LDI portfolios now run with larger collateral buffers and clearer plans for where emergency cash comes from.

**Why it matters to this role.** LDI is central to UK DB relationships. Even a junior person must be able to explain why a scheme's assets falling 25% was not necessarily bad news, why the *speed* of a collateral call matters, and why a client's other holdings are judged partly on how quickly they could be sold to support the hedge.
:::

### Money market funds in plain English

:::l1
A **money market fund (MMF)** is a pooled fund that invests in very short-term, high-quality debt, such as government bills and bank deposits maturing within days or weeks. Its job is to keep cash safe, available the same day, and earning roughly the going short-term interest rate.

Who uses them? Anyone holding large amounts of cash that would be unwise to leave with a single bank: company treasurers, pension schemes holding collateral, insurers, other asset managers.

It behaves like a shared current account spread across many borrowers. The analogy stops being accurate in one important respect: an MMF is an investment, not a deposit. No deposit guarantee stands behind it and its value is not promised.
:::

:::l2
**Why it exists.** A treasurer with £300m cannot sensibly leave it with one bank: if that bank fails, the money is an unsecured claim. An MMF spreads the cash across dozens of issuers, with professional credit analysis and daily access.

**How money moves.** The investor earns the fund's **yield** minus a fee, typically a few to a couple of dozen basis points. The fee is small, so the business depends on very large balances, operational reliability and treasury-system connectivity, not on outperformance.

**The three types.** UK and EU rules recognise three structures: public debt constant NAV (**CNAV**), low volatility NAV (**LVNAV**) and variable NAV (**VNAV**) {{SI}} [@W1-S09]. An LVNAV fund may deal at a stable price of 1.00 per share only while the true market value of its assets stays close: if the gap exceeds 20 basis points, dealing must switch to the market-based price {{V}} [@W1-S06].

**What can go wrong.** A run: many investors redeem at once, as in March 2020, and the fund must sell assets to pay them. Regulation therefore sets minimum holdings of assets maturing within a day and a week.

**Why it matters to this role.** Liquidity clients are a distinct business. The client's questions are about safety, access, cut-off times, concentration and their own investment policy limits. Balances move with the client's business cycle and with interest rates, so "flows" here mean something different from winning a long-term mandate.
:::

### OCIO and fiduciary management in plain English

:::l1
Running an institutional portfolio involves dozens of decisions: how much in each asset class, which managers, when to rebalance, how to hedge, how to report. Many governing bodies meet quarterly and are not full-time investors.

An **outsourced chief investment officer (OCIO)** arrangement hands those day-to-day decisions to a specialist provider. The governing body keeps the big decisions (objectives, risk appetite, the journey plan) and delegates implementation. In the UK pensions market the usual name is **fiduciary management (FM)**. TPR's guidance describes the term as generally used for "a governance model through which trustees delegate" investment decisions to a provider {{V}} [@W1-S04].

It is like hiring a general contractor to build a house instead of hiring and coordinating each tradesperson yourself. The analogy stops being accurate at one point that matters: a fiduciary manager may place some of the money in its *own* funds, which creates a conflict of interest that a general contractor does not normally have.
:::

:::l2
**How it differs from an ordinary mandate.** An ordinary mandate is one slice of the portfolio run to a benchmark. An OCIO mandate covers the whole portfolio, or a large part of it, and is measured against the client's own objective, typically the liabilities. The provider usually selects *other* managers as well as, or instead of, using its own strategies.

**How money moves.** The client pays a fee for the fiduciary service, plus the fees of the underlying managers and funds. Scale lets the provider negotiate lower underlying fees, which is part of the case for the model. The fee rate on the whole portfolio is low compared with an active mandate, but the asset base is very large and the relationship is deep.

**Safeguards.** Because delegation is broad, UK regulation requires process. Following a Competition and Markets Authority investigation and its 2019 Order {{SI}} [@W1-S03], trustees must run a qualifying competitive tender, inviting bids from at least three unconnected providers, before fiduciary management first covers 20% or more of scheme assets {{V}} [@W1-S04]. Many schemes also hire an independent third-party evaluator to help select and then monitor the fiduciary manager.

**What can go wrong.** Unclear division of responsibility between trustees and provider; conflicts over use of in-house products; difficulty comparing performance across providers; and a costly, slow exit if the relationship fails.

**Variants.** Mandates range from a small scheme using a provider's standard pooled solution to a very large scheme with a bespoke arrangement. In some large cases the provider takes on the client's existing in-house investment staff. That is one model, not the definition of OCIO.

**Why it matters to this role.** OCIO changes the nature of the sale and the relationship. The sale is a governance decision as much as an investment decision, often run as a formal tender with an evaluator. The relationship afterwards is closer to a partnership with the trustee board than to reporting on a single strategy.
:::

## 8. Putting it together

One short story uses almost everything in this chapter. All figures are invented. {{IL}}

A closed UK defined benefit scheme has £800m of assets and liabilities valued at £780m. It is advised by an investment consultant. Its trustees meet quarterly.

- **The need (section 1).** The scheme is slightly over-funded and wants to keep it that way while it decides between insuring and running on. Its problem is liability-relative risk and liquidity, not maximum return.
- **The chain (section 2).** The trustees decide. The consultant frames the options and shortlists managers. Members never appear in the room.
- **The vehicle and contract (section 3).** The trustees want a £200m segregated credit mandate designed to produce the cash the scheme needs to pay pensions each year. The guidelines set a minimum credit quality and limit how much can be in any one borrower.
- **The yardstick (section 4).** Success is defined as delivering the scheduled cash flows with few defaults, not as beating a bond index. The portfolio must be saleable to an insurer later, so illiquid holdings are capped.
- **The fee (section 5).** At 18 bps the mandate would generate about £360,000 a year of gross revenue for the manager if fully funded for a full year, before costs.
- **The coverage work (section 6).** A salesperson and the consultant relations team make sure the consultant understands the strategy. An RFP is answered. A portfolio manager presents. Legal negotiates the IMA. After funding, a relationship manager owns quarterly reporting and is the first call when something changes.
- **What changes (section 7).** Two years later the trustees agree a buy-in with an insurer. The mandate is transferred or wound down. The manager's revenue from this client falls, possibly to zero, and that is the right outcome for the members.

That last line is deliberate. Understanding this business includes understanding that the best result for the client sometimes reduces the manager's assets. The chapters that follow examine what client coverage does about that, honestly.
