# Start here: the whole thing from zero

This part assumes you know nothing about the industry. It uses short sentences and plain words. Each idea gets one picture where a picture helps.

## 1. What you are applying to, in six sentences

1. **Goldman Sachs** is a large financial firm.
2. One part of it manages other people's money. That part is called **Goldman Sachs Asset Management**.
3. Managing money needs two kinds of people. Some decide what to buy. Others find the clients and look after them.
4. The **Client Solutions Group** is the second kind. Goldman describes it as the team that develops client relationships and delivers investment solutions for institutional and third-party wealth clients. {{V}} [@R1-S10]
5. The **Summer Analyst programme** is a nine to ten week internship in that group, in London, in summer 2027. {{V}} [@R1-S22]
6. If it goes well, it can lead to a full-time job as an **Analyst**, the first rung of the career.

That is the whole thing. The rest of this part explains each sentence.

:::warn One honest gap
The public London listing for this programme carries only general wording. It does not describe the duties, the team or the pay. {{V}} [@R1-S02] So this guide builds the picture from Goldman's other job advertisements, its financial reports and its press releases.
:::

## 2. Start with the money: whose is it?

Forget finance for a moment. Think about a nurse who is 30.

Every month, part of her pay goes into a pension. She will need that money in about 35 years. Until then, somebody has to look after it and make it grow.

She does not do that herself. Her **pension scheme** does. A pension scheme is an organisation that holds the savings of many workers and pays them an income when they retire.

The pension scheme usually does not pick investments itself either. It hires a firm to do that. That firm is an **asset manager**.

<!-- DATA:diagram:money-chain -->

Four things to notice in the picture.

- **The money never belongs to the manager.** It belongs to the nurse and people like her. The manager is hired help.
- **The client is the organisation in the middle.** In this job, "the client" usually means the pension scheme, the insurer or the fund. It does not mean the nurse.
- **An adviser often stands beside the client.** An **investment consultant** is a firm that advises the client on which manager to hire. Consultants do not hold the money. Their opinion carries great weight.
- **The manager is paid a small yearly percentage.** More on that in section 4.

Pension schemes are one kind of client. There are others. They all share one feature: they hold a large amount of money that must do a job in the future.

| Who holds money | The job the money must do |
|---|---|
| A pension scheme | Pay pensions for decades |
| An insurance company | Pay claims when they fall due |
| A charity or university fund | Pay for good work, for ever |
| A state investment fund | Save a country's wealth for later generations |
| A company with spare cash | Stay safe and be available tomorrow |

These large organisations are called **institutional investors**, or simply **institutions**.

## 4. How the manager is paid

The idea is simple. The client pays a small percentage of its money each year.

The percentage is so small that the industry uses a special unit. One **basis point** is one hundredth of one per cent. So 20 basis points is 0.20%.

<!-- DATA:diagram:fee-arithmetic -->

Three things follow from this picture, and they explain most of the job.

1. **Big clients matter enormously.** One mandate can be worth a million pounds a year.
2. **The fee comes back every year, but only while the client stays.** A client who leaves takes the fee with them.
3. **The fee moves without anyone doing anything.** If markets fall 10%, the fee falls about 10%.

A **mandate** is the name for one job a client gives a manager: "invest this £500 million in bonds, under these rules".

For a sense of real levels: across everything it manages, Goldman's average management fee in 2025 was 31 basis points. It was 17 for bonds and 58 for private investments. {{V}} [@R2-S01] These are averages across the whole firm. They are not the price of any one mandate.

## 5. What the Client Solutions Group does all day

An asset manager can be excellent at investing and still fail. The right clients have to know it exists, understand what it offers, check it thoroughly and then stay.

That is the work of the Client Solutions Group. It has two halves.

- **Sales** means finding clients and winning new mandates.
- **Relationship management** means looking after clients once they have appointed the firm.

<!-- DATA:diagram:sales-rm -->

What do the names mean at Goldman?

- Goldman's own job titles pair "Client Solutions Group" with the word "Sales". {{V}} [@R1-S04]
- Goldman also uses the name "Client Coverage Group" for people who retain, expand and support existing client relationships. {{V}} [@R1-S11]
- How the two groups fit together in London is not published. {{NPE}} At least one London advertisement gives a Client Solutions Group role both kinds of work. {{V}} [@R1-S01]

So the safe way to say it is this. "The group covers both winning clients and keeping them. Teams split that work in different ways."

### What a junior person does

This is published. A Goldman advertisement for a junior London role in this group lists the tasks. {{V}} [@R1-S01]

- Prepare for client and prospect meetings, and follow up afterwards.
- Answer day-to-day client questions.
- Work with product specialists, and with the marketing, compliance and operations teams.
- Support the head of the team with market information and presentations.

In plain terms, a junior person makes sure the senior person walks into every meeting with the right facts, and that every promise made in the meeting is kept.

:::example A Tuesday for a first-year analyst {{IL}}
- 08:30. Check that last night's performance figures for three clients match the system.
- 10:00. Build the pack for Thursday's review with a pension scheme. Twelve slides. Every number traced to a source.
- 13:00. A client asks why their report shows a different return from last quarter's. Find out. It is a change in the benchmark. Draft a clear reply for your manager to send.
- 15:00. Sit in on a call with a prospect. Take notes. Write up the four things the firm promised to send.
- 16:30. Chase the portfolio manager for the answer to one of those four things.

Nothing here is glamorous. All of it is what trust is made of. If the pack has a wrong number in it, the meeting goes badly, and nobody in the room will remember who drew the nice chart.
:::

## 6. Two routes to the people whose money it is

The group reaches clients by two main routes. The former intern you spoke to described these as two of the UK teams, and Goldman's own reports use the same split. {{V}} [@R2-S02]

<!-- DATA:diagram:two-routes -->

**Route 1 is institutional.** The team talks to the organisation that owns the money. A pension scheme is run by **trustees**, people with a legal duty to act for its members. One decision can move hundreds of millions of pounds. It can take a year or more to make.

**Route 2 is third party wealth.** "Third party" means another firm sits in the middle. Goldman publicly uses the name "Third Party Wealth" for this business. {{V}} [@R4-S01] The team persuades a bank or wealth manager to approve a Goldman fund. That firm's advisers then decide, one by one, whether to use it for their own customers.

A key point about Route 2: being approved is permission to be bought. It is not a purchase. A fund can be on the approved list and attract nothing.

Goldman's long-term client assets at 30 September 2026 split like this. {{V}} [@R2-S02]

| Route | Assets |
|---|---|
| Institutional | $1,245 billion |
| Wealth management (Goldman's own private clients) | $1,087 billion |
| Third-party distributed | $714 billion |

### Three special businesses

You will also hear three more names. Each serves a client with an unusual need.

- **OCIO** stands for outsourced chief investment officer. The client hands over the running of its whole portfolio, not one slice. Goldman reports more than $540 billion of these assets worldwide. {{V}} [@R8-S22] The best-known UK example is the BAE Systems pension schemes, about £23 billion, announced by Goldman in September 2023. {{V}} [@R3-S01]
- **Liquidity Solutions** looks after cash that a company or fund needs to keep safe and reach quickly. Goldman says the team oversees more than $1.2 trillion. {{V}} [@R5-S01]
- **Insurance** serves insurance companies, which must follow strict rules on what they can hold. Goldman says it manages $651 billion for them. {{V}} [@R5-S02]

## 7. How one relationship is won and kept

Here is the whole life of one client, start to finish.

<!-- DATA:diagram:lifecycle -->

A few things in this picture surprise people.

- **Winning is slow.** Steps 1 to 5 often take many months. A large mandate goes through written questionnaires, meetings with the consultant, and a final presentation to the trustees.
- **Most attempts fail.** Several managers compete. Only one is appointed. A good sales person hears "no" far more often than "yes".
- **The win is the start.** After step 5 the firm has made a set of promises. Steps 6 to 8 are about keeping them, year after year.
- **Step 8 has three honest outcomes.** The client may give more money, take some back, or leave. Sometimes leaving is the right decision for the client, and a good relationship manager helps them do it well.

## 8. How trust really grows

People say this job is about "relationships" and "trust". Those words mean little until you can see them.

Start with a definition. To **trust** someone is to rely on them when you cannot check everything they do, and when you would be worse off if they let you down.

A trustee cannot watch a portfolio manager all day. So the trustee looks for evidence that relying on the firm is safe. The evidence is behaviour, repeated over time.

<!-- DATA:diagram:trust-steps -->

Take the steps one at a time.

**Step 1. You do what you said, on time.** You promised the document by Friday. It arrives on Thursday. This sounds small. It is the foundation. A client decides very early whether your word means anything.

**Step 2. Your numbers are always right.** One wrong figure in a report makes the client doubt every other figure. This is why a junior person's care with detail matters so much.

**Step 3. You explain a bad result plainly.** Every strategy has bad periods. The test is what you say then.

:::dialogue {{IL}}
**Trustee:** You are behind the benchmark by 2% this year. Why?

**Weak answer:** Markets were very difficult, and we remain confident in our process.

**Better answer:** Two decisions cost us. We held more company bonds than the benchmark, and they fell. We also held less in government bonds, which rose. Here is each effect in numbers. We still hold the first position, for these reasons. We have closed the second. If it would help, the portfolio manager can walk you through it next week.
:::

The second answer admits what happened, gives numbers, says what changed, and offers more. The client learns that this firm tells the truth when the truth is unwelcome.

**Step 4. You say "this is not right for you" when it is not.** Suppose a client asks about a product that would earn your firm a higher fee, and it does not fit their needs. Saying so costs a sale today. It earns something that lasts longer: the client now knows your advice is not just selling.

**Step 5. They trust the firm, not only you.** People change jobs. Trustees retire. A relationship that lives only in one person's head leaves with that person. So good teams write things down, share what they know, and introduce colleagues early. After ten years the client should feel safe whoever picks up the phone.

**The slide back down.** Trust builds slowly and breaks fast. A hidden problem is far worse than the problem itself. Clients forgive mistakes that are reported quickly. They rarely forgive finding out later.

:::warn Two things trust does not do
- **It does not replace checking.** A client who likes you must still do full due diligence. Their own legal duty requires it.
- **It does not mean keeping every client.** If a pension scheme becomes fully funded and passes its money to an insurer, the manager loses the mandate. That can be the best possible outcome for the nurse. A good relationship manager helps it happen smoothly.
:::

## 9. The long view: what would you be choosing?

You asked what you would be working in for forty years or more. Nobody can honestly map forty years. What this guide can do is show the shape of the path, what changes along it, and what stays the same.

<!-- DATA:diagram:career-path -->

Three facts about titles at Goldman.

- Outside the United States, "Executive Director" is the working title for the Vice President level. {{V}} [@R1-S10]
- Partner is not an automatic step. People are invited, in a named class. The class of 2026 has 109 people across the whole firm. {{V}} [@R1-S27]
- The timings in the picture are a general industry pattern. Goldman does not publish them. {{SI}}

### What changes over the years {{SI}}

| Stage | What you are trusted with | What makes you good at it |
|---|---|---|
| First 3 years | Getting things right | Care, speed, curiosity, asking good questions |
| Years 3 to 8 | Running client work | Organisation, clear writing, knowing the products |
| Years 8 to 15 | Owning relationships and targets | Judgement, honesty under pressure, commercial sense |
| After that | Owning a business | Leading people, choosing where to compete |

### What stays the same

Three things are true on day one and in year thirty.

- You stand between people who have money and people who invest it. You have to understand both.
- You are responsible for outcomes you do not fully control. You do not pick the investments. You do have to explain them.
- Your reputation is your main asset. It is built from many small kept promises.

### The honest downsides

- **Repetition.** Reports, packs and questionnaires come round every quarter.
- **Rejection.** Most pitches do not win.
- **Dependence.** You rely on portfolio managers, lawyers and operations teams to keep your promises.
- **Targets.** Senior people are measured on money won and money kept.
- **Narrowing.** Years spent on one client type in one region make you expert in it. They can also make it harder to move.

### What is shifting under the industry right now

A forty-year career will see the industry change several times. These are the shifts visible today.

- **UK pensions are being reshaped.** The Pension Schemes Act 2026 became law on 29 April 2026. {{V}} [@R3-S20] It pushes schemes to merge into fewer, larger ones.
- **Many UK pension schemes now have more than enough money.** The typical funding level was 133.7% at the end of October 2026. {{V}} [@R3-S29] More schemes are passing their money to insurers, which removes it from asset managers.
- **Interest rates turned.** The Bank of England raised its rate to 4% on 5 November 2026, the first rise since 2023. {{V}} [@R3-S31]
- **Fees are under pressure.** Goldman itself warns that clients moving to cheaper products could lower its average fee further. {{V}} [@R2-S01]
- **AI is taking over routine drafting.** A 2026 survey found 81% of institutional sales teams use AI to draft answers to client questionnaires. {{V}} [@R8-S41] The checking, the judgement and the conversation still fall to people.

The work changes with all of these. The need for someone to stand between the client and the investors does not go away.

## 10. From understanding to "why I want this"

An interviewer will ask why you want this job. A strong answer cannot be borrowed. It has to come from you.

Here is a way to build one that is honest.

<!-- DATA:diagram:why-funnel -->

**Step 1. Know what the job is.** You now do. It is helping large organisations choose and keep the right investments, by understanding their problem, bringing the right people to it, and keeping every promise afterwards.

**Step 2. Pick out what might suit you.** Read this list slowly. Notice which lines you react to. A reaction either way is useful.

- Working out what someone needs, when it differs from what they first asked for.
- Explaining something complicated so that a non-expert can decide.
- Being the person who makes sure nothing is dropped.
- Learning how pensions, insurers and markets work, and keeping on learning.
- Building something slowly, over years, with the same people.
- Being measured, eventually, on money won and kept.
- Hearing "no" often and carrying on.

**Step 3. Test each one against your own life.** For each line that drew you, find one real time you did something like it. Then ask the harder question. Did you enjoy the doing, or only the result?

**Step 4. Keep what survives.** A reason that has passed step 3 is one you can defend when an interviewer pushes.

Two sentence frames may help. Fill them only with things that are true.

> "The part of this work that draws me is [ ... ]. I know that because [ ... ]. What I have not tested yet is [ ... ]."

> "I understand the job is mostly [ ... ] in the first years. That suits me because [ ... ]."

If you have no evidence for something yet, say so. "I want to find out whether I enjoy this, and here is how the internship would test it" is a stronger answer than a confident story with nothing behind it.

The Why-trees section of the app pushes each reason through six or seven harder questions. The blank personal ledger in chapter 8 is where your own evidence goes.

## 11. What happens next in the process

Goldman publishes the general shape of its hiring process. {{V}} [@R1-S23]

- First, a recorded video interview of about 30 minutes.
- Then a final round, which Goldman calls a "Superday": two to five interviews with people you could work with.

You may apply to at most four business and location combinations in one year. {{V}} [@R1-S02]

What is not published: how candidates are matched to teams, whether this team's final round is in person, and what it pays. Treat anything you hear on those as one person's account.
