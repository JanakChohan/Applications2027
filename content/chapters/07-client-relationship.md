# What a client relationship actually means

"Relationships build trust, and trust creates loyalty" is a sentence that sounds like an answer and explains nothing. This chapter replaces it with things you can observe: who is in the room, what each of them is responsible for, what a coverage professional says in a hard conversation, what the client can and cannot check, and when the right outcome is for the client to leave.

:::note How to read this chapter
- The mechanics described here are general to institutional asset management in the UK. They are not statements about Goldman Sachs's internal policies, fees or procedures, none of which are public.
- Regulatory facts carry a source and a {{V}} or {{SI}} badge. Commercial reasoning is marked {{L:B}}. Every dialogue, scenario and number in an example is invented for teaching and marked {{IL}}.
- "Coverage professional" means the sales or relationship management person responsible for a client. "Manager" means the asset management firm.
:::

## 1. Five different things people call a "relationship"

:::l1
When someone says "we have a good relationship with that client", they could mean any of five things. They are easy to confuse because they often arrive together, but each one does a different job and each one fails in a different way.

Think of a family that has used the same solicitor's firm for twenty years. They like the solicitor (rapport). They believe she is good at her job (confidence). There is an engagement letter that says what she will do and what she charges (contract). When she retires, the firm still holds the files and a colleague takes over (continuity). And the reason they keep coming back is that her advice has helped them make better decisions (decision value). The analogy stops being accurate in one important place: a solicitor is the family's adviser and owes them a duty to advise in their interest, while an asset manager's coverage professional works for a firm that is selling a service to the client. Section 5 deals with that difference directly.
:::

| Layer | What it is | How you would observe it | What it cannot do on its own |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal rapport | Two people find it easy to talk | Calls are returned; conversation is candid; bad news is raised early | Win or keep a mandate. A committee decides, and rapport with one member is not evidence of quality |
| Professional confidence | The client believes the firm and its people are competent and straight | The client asks for a view before deciding; accepts "I will check" without suspicion | Substitute for formal evidence. Confidence has to be re-earned each reporting period |
| Contractual obligations | What the firm has legally agreed to do | The investment management agreement (IMA), investment guidelines, fee schedule, reporting timetable, notice period | Make the client stay. Most segregated mandates can be ended on short notice |
| Institutional continuity | The relationship is between two organisations and outlasts individuals | A new coverage person can pick up the file and know the history; the client's new chief investment officer (CIO) inherits a working arrangement | Exist without records. Continuity is only as good as what was written down |
| Decision-making value | The client decides better because of the contact | The client changed, delayed or avoided a decision because of information the manager supplied, and was right to | Be assumed. It has to be demonstrated case by case, and some contact adds none |

:::l2
The layers interact in ways that matter commercially. {{L:B}}

- **Contract sets the floor, not the ceiling.** The IMA and guidelines define what the manager must do: the benchmark, permitted instruments, risk limits, reporting dates, fees and how either side can terminate. A manager that meets every contractual term can still lose the mandate, because nothing in the contract obliges the client to keep believing in the strategy.
- **Rapport lowers the cost of information.** A client who is comfortable with the coverage professional will say "the sponsor is nervous about the funding level" a quarter before it appears in any document. That is valuable, but it is an input to the other layers, not a layer that pays fees.
- **Continuity is what the firm owns.** The mandate is a contract with the firm. Individual credibility helps, but the firm's claim on the relationship rests on the contract, the track record, the service history and the written record, all of which stay when a person leaves.
- **Decision value is the only layer the client is paying for beyond investment returns.** If the contact does not improve the client's decisions or reduce their workload, it is hospitality.

A useful test is the **stranger test**: if the coverage professional were replaced tomorrow by a competent stranger holding the complete file, what would the client lose? If the honest answer is "nothing", the relationship is fully institutional, which is robust but means the individual adds little. If the answer is "everything", the relationship is trapped in one person, which is fragile for the firm and for the client. Healthy relationships sit between the two.
:::

## 2. Who "the client" actually is

:::l1
A retail customer is one person making one decision. An institutional client is a group of people with different jobs, different worries and a formal process for deciding. Saying "the client wants X" usually hides the fact that one person wants X, another is neutral, a third has to sign it off, and a fourth will have to make it work operationally.

Take a UK defined benefit (DB) pension scheme, which is a pool of assets held in trust to pay promised pensions. The legal decision-makers are the trustees. They are often part-time, they meet perhaps four times a year, and they are personally responsible for decisions about other people's retirement income. They are required by law to take advice before investing {{V}} [@W2-S02], so an investment consultant sits beside them. The employer that stands behind the scheme (the sponsor) cares because it has to fund any shortfall. None of these people is "the client" alone. Together they are.
:::

| Participant | Accountable for | What they want from the manager | What makes them say no |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trustee board and chair | Paying members' benefits; lawful, well-advised decisions | Clear explanations they can minute; no surprises; evidence they can defend later | Anything they cannot explain to members or the regulator |
| Member-nominated or lay trustee | Representing members | Plain language; honesty about risk | Jargon; feeling managed |
| Professional trustee | Governance quality across several schemes | Efficiency; consistency with what they see at other schemes | Poor reporting; a manager who looks worse than peers they know |
| Sponsor (finance director, treasury) | The company's cash and balance sheet | Lower funding volatility; lower cost; a route to removing the liability | Risk that shows up in the company accounts |
| In-house CIO or investment staff | Day-to-day oversight of managers | Depth, data, fast accurate answers, access to portfolio managers | Being sold to; answers that do not survive checking |
| Investment consultant | Advice to trustees; manager research ratings | Complete due diligence material; no attempts to go around them | A manager that is hard to research or inconsistent |
| Operations, custodian, administrator | Cash movements, valuations, reconciliations | Accurate files on time; a named contact when something breaks | Errors and late data |
| Legal and procurement | Contract terms; fair process | Standard terms; clean answers in the tender | Unusual liability terms; process breaches |
| Members and beneficiaries | (Not in the room) | Their pension, paid in full | They have no direct voice, which is why the duties below exist |

In wealth distribution the cast changes but the principle does not. The "client" of a third-party wealth team is typically an intermediary firm, and inside that firm sit a fund selector or research analyst (the gatekeeper), an investment committee that approves the buy list, portfolio managers or advisers who actually allocate, a platform or operations team, and compliance. The individual saver whose money it is never meets the asset manager.

:::l2
The legal structure explains much of the behaviour. {{L:A}}

- **Trustees' investment duty.** For UK occupational schemes, assets must be invested "in the best interests of members and beneficiaries" and, where there is a potential conflict of interest, in their sole interest {{V}} [@W2-S01]. This is a duty on the trustees. The asset manager does not owe this particular duty, but every trustee decision about the manager is taken under it.
- **Advice before investing.** Trustees "must obtain and consider proper advice on the question whether the investment is satisfactory", and must decide how often to refresh that advice for investments they keep {{V}} [@W2-S02]. This is why a consultant's view, or an equivalent adviser's, stands between a good meeting and an allocation.
- **Delegation with monitoring.** Trustees can delegate investment discretion to a fund manager, and are protected from responsibility for the manager's defaults only if they have taken "all such steps as are reasonable to satisfy themselves" that the manager has the right knowledge and experience and "is carrying out his work competently" {{V}} [@W2-S03]. A trustee who stopped monitoring because they trusted the manager would be giving up their own legal protection.
- **Competitive tendering for fiduciary management.** Where trustees hand 20% or more of scheme assets to fiduciary management providers, they must run a qualifying tender with bids from at least three unconnected providers, and are expected to review performance and periodically review the market afterwards {{V}} [@W2-S04]. In this part of the market, a strong relationship cannot lawfully replace a competition.
- **Wealth chains and the Consumer Duty.** For investments, the Financial Conduct Authority's (FCA) Consumer Duty applies to business with customers who are not professional clients. It also applies "across the distribution chain" to any firm that determines or materially influences retail customer outcomes, even with no direct relationship {{V}} [@W2-S06]. A fund manufacturer selling through wealth firms therefore has to think about the end investor it never meets, and its distributors will ask it for information to meet their own obligations.

{{L:B}} Three practical consequences follow.

1. **Map the decision, not the organisation chart.** For each client the coverage team needs to know who proposes, who advises, who decides, who can veto, and who implements. These are often five different people.
2. **A single champion is a risk.** If one enthusiastic investment officer is the only person who understands why the manager was hired, the mandate is exposed the day that person leaves.
3. **The consultant is a participant, not an obstacle.** Trying to win over trustees while leaving the consultant under-informed usually backfires, because the consultant's advice is part of the trustees' legal process.
:::

:::example A stakeholder map for a hypothetical scheme {{IL}}
A £1.2bn DB scheme holds a £300m investment-grade credit mandate with a manager.

- **Proposes change:** the scheme's two-person in-house investment team.
- **Advises:** an investment consultant, whose manager research team rates the strategy.
- **Decides:** an investment sub-committee of four trustees, chaired by a professional trustee; the full board ratifies.
- **Can veto in practice:** the sponsor's finance director, who is consulted on strategy changes and cares about the timetable to insurance buyout.
- **Implements:** the custodian, the scheme administrator and a transition manager if assets move.

The coverage professional has an excellent rapport with the in-house investment manager. That person does not decide. A plan that depends on that rapport alone has not yet reached three of the five functions above.
:::

## 3. What a relationship reveals that a factsheet cannot, and the reverse

:::l1
A factsheet is a short document that states a fund's objective, performance, holdings, risk figures and charges. It is the same for every reader. A relationship is the accumulated knowledge of one particular client. The two answer different questions, and neither can do the other's job.
:::

| Question | Where the answer comes from | Why |
|---|---|---|
| What has the strategy returned, after fees, against its benchmark? | Factsheet, performance report, audited or verified records | It must be the same answer for everyone and checkable |
| Is the firm operationally sound? | Operational due diligence, controls reports, the due diligence questionnaire (DDQ) | The client's own duty requires evidence, not assurance |
| What is this client actually worried about this year? | The relationship | It is specific, changes over time and is rarely written down for outsiders |
| How does this committee really decide, and how fast? | The relationship | Governance on paper and governance in practice differ |
| Which of our reports does nobody read, and which one does the chair take into the meeting? | The relationship | Only repeated contact reveals it |
| What do the contract terms say about liability and termination? | The IMA and legal review | Good feeling does not change legal terms |

What a working relationship tends to reveal {{L:B}}:

- **The real constraint.** The stated objective may be "outperform the benchmark by 1% a year". The real constraint may be "the sponsor wants to insure the scheme within five years, so nothing that cannot be sold by then".
- **Timing.** A consultant review of the asset class next spring; a trustee election; a strategy day in September. Proposals that ignore the calendar arrive when nobody can act on them.
- **Tolerance in practice.** A client may have agreed a risk budget in writing and still be unable to live with two bad quarters. The file says one thing; the reaction to the last drawdown says another.
- **Misunderstandings.** A trustee who thinks "investment grade" means "cannot lose money" needs a different conversation from one who understands credit spreads.
- **Early warning.** Dissatisfaction is usually voiced informally before it is minuted. A manager with no informal channel learns of a review when the termination notice arrives.

What formal process must establish regardless of how good the relationship is {{L:B}}:

- The performance record, calculated consistently and net of fees.
- The people, process and risk controls behind the strategy, examined by someone qualified to examine them.
- Operational and legal soundness: custody arrangements, valuation, compliance history, contract terms.
- Fees and total costs, compared with alternatives.
- For trustees, written advice that the investment is satisfactory {{V}} [@W2-S02].

:::warn The warning sign in both directions
If a manager offers warmth where evidence was requested ("you know us, you can rely on us"), a careful client should become more cautious, not less. If a client waves away due diligence because they like the team, a careful manager should still provide it, because the next person in that client's seat will ask why it is missing from the file. Soft information tells you which question to ask. Hard information has to answer it.
:::

:::workproduct Meeting preparation note (the kind of document a junior drafts) {{IL}}
**Client:** hypothetical £1.2bn DB scheme. **Meeting:** annual review, investment sub-committee. **Prepared for:** coverage lead and portfolio manager.

1. **Mandate facts (from systems, checked against the last client report):** £300m investment-grade credit, benchmark and guidelines as per IMA; 12-month return versus benchmark; current guideline headroom; fees invoiced.
2. **Open actions from the last meeting:** two items closed, one outstanding (climate metrics methodology note, promised for this meeting, attached).
3. **Who will attend and what each cares about (from call notes):** chair wants a one-page summary she can minute; the in-house manager has asked about downgrade risk in two named sectors; the consultant's researcher is re-rating the strategy next quarter.
4. **What has changed for the client (from call notes and public documents):** latest published funding position; sponsor's public statements about de-risking.
5. **Likely hard questions and who answers them:** underperformance in the second quarter (portfolio manager); fee comparison with a buy-and-maintain alternative (coverage lead).
6. **Things we do not know:** whether the buyout timetable has moved. Suggested question to ask, not assume.

The junior's contribution is accuracy and completeness: every number traced to a source, every promise from the last meeting accounted for. The junior does not decide what the firm will say about fees or performance.
:::

## 4. Trust, translated into behaviour

:::l1
To trust someone is to rely on them in a situation where you cannot fully check what they are doing and would be worse off if they let you down. A trustee cannot watch a portfolio manager all day. A fund selector cannot verify every sentence in a pitch. They rely, and they look for evidence that relying is safe.

"Trust" is too large a word to act on, so this section breaks it into eight components. Each has a definition, a behaviour someone else could observe, a conversation, a trade-off that makes it hard, and the consequences for the client and for the business. If you cannot point to a behaviour, you are describing a feeling.
:::

:::l2
A widely used academic model separates three bases of trustworthiness: ability (can they do it), benevolence (do they care about my interests) and integrity (do they keep to principles I accept) {{SI}} [@W2-S09]. The eight components below map onto it. Competence is ability. Responsiveness and willingness to challenge are how benevolence becomes visible. Honesty, reliability, confidentiality, transparency about limitations and consistency are integrity observed over time. The model is a way of organising the discussion. It does not predict which component a particular client weighs most, and the paper was cited from general knowledge, not re-read for this project.
:::

| Component | One-line definition | The behaviour a client can see |
|---|---|---|
| Competence | Knowing the subject well enough to be useful, and knowing where your knowledge ends | Correct answers; clean handovers to specialists; no bluffing |
| Reliability | Doing what you said, when you said | Promised items arrive on the promised date, or the client hears before the deadline |
| Honesty | Saying what is true, including when it is unwelcome | Bad results are reported plainly and first |
| Responsiveness | Acknowledging and progressing requests quickly | A same-day reply that states what will happen and when |
| Confidentiality | Keeping one client's information away from others | Refusing to trade in other clients' details, even when asked nicely |
| Willingness to challenge | Raising a concern about what the client wants | "Before we do that, can I test it against your own objective?" |
| Transparency about limitations | Volunteering what the product, firm or person cannot do | Capacity limits, conflicts and weak spots disclosed before the client finds them |
| Consistency | The same message to every audience and in every market | The trustee, the consultant and the operations team hear the same facts |

### 4.1 Competence

**Definition.** Enough knowledge of markets, products and the client's situation to add something, combined with an accurate sense of where that knowledge stops. For a coverage professional, competence includes knowing who inside the firm holds the answer.

**Observable behaviour.** Answers are right the first time. Technical questions outside the person's depth are routed to the portfolio manager or product specialist, with the question restated correctly. The follow-up arrives.

:::dialogue When you do not know the answer {{IL}}
**In-house investment manager (client):** Your attribution shows 30 basis points lost from "curve positioning" last quarter. Was that the 10-year versus 30-year position, or the cross-market trade against bunds?

**Analyst (on the coverage team, taking the call because the lead is travelling):** I don't know which of the two it was, and I would rather not guess on something you may repeat to your committee. I can see the total on the report, but the split sits with the portfolio team. I will ask them this afternoon and send you the breakdown in writing by midday tomorrow. If it is going to take longer than that, I will tell you before then.

**In-house investment manager:** Fine. Tomorrow works. Include the sizing of each position if you can.

**Analyst:** I will ask for it. If they cannot share position sizes for any reason, I will say so and explain why.
:::

**Difficult trade-off.** Appearing knowledgeable now versus being correct later. A fluent guess ends the awkward moment. If the guess is wrong and the client repeats it in a committee paper, the damage is to the client's credibility as well as the manager's.

**Client consequence.** The client can rely on what they are told without re-checking every statement, which is the practical meaning of professional confidence.

**Commercial consequence.** {{L:B}} Clients bring questions to people who answer them correctly. Being consulted before a decision is how a manager learns about new allocations early. A reputation for bluffing has the opposite effect, and consultants compare notes.

### 4.2 Reliability

**Definition.** Commitments are kept. When one cannot be kept, the client hears before the deadline, with a reason and a new date.

**Observable behaviour.** A tracked list of promises with owners and dates. Reports arrive on schedule. The person who said "I will come back to you" comes back.

:::dialogue When a commitment cannot be met {{IL}}
**Coverage lead:** I am calling about the liability cashflow analysis I promised for this Thursday, ahead of your sub-committee on the 14th. It will not be ready on Thursday. The team found an inconsistency in the inflation assumptions between two of the data files your administrator sent, and I am not willing to send you numbers built on inputs we know disagree.

**Pensions manager (client):** The papers go out on Monday. I told the chair it would be in the pack.

**Coverage lead:** Understood, and that is why I am calling today and not on Thursday. Two options. We can send the analysis on Monday morning using the more conservative file, clearly labelled as provisional, so it makes the pack. Or we can wait for your administrator to confirm which file is right, which probably means a late paper on Wednesday. I would lean to the first, but it is your pack and your chair.

**Pensions manager:** Send it Monday, labelled. And copy me on the query to the administrator so I can chase.

**Coverage lead:** Will do, both today.
:::

**Difficult trade-off.** Promising less so that you always deliver, versus promising what the client needs and risking a miss. Under-promising protects the record but can make the manager look slow. Over-promising wins the moment and spends credibility later.

**Client consequence.** The client can plan around the manager. Committees run on fixed timetables; a late paper can delay a decision by a quarter.

**Commercial consequence.** {{L:B}} Reliability is rarely the reason a manager is hired and often a reason one is fired. Service failures give a dissatisfied committee member a concrete, minutable complaint in a way that modest underperformance does not.

### 4.3 Honesty

**Definition.** What is said is true and complete enough not to mislead. This covers emphasis as well as facts: leading with the one good number in a bad quarter is a form of dishonesty.

**Observable behaviour.** Disappointing results are raised by the manager before the client asks. Explanations separate what was a judgement that went wrong from what was outside the strategy's control, and do not relabel the first as the second.

:::dialogue When performance disappoints {{IL}}
**Trustee chair:** We are 1.8% behind the benchmark over twelve months. Our consultant has put the mandate on watch. I would like to hear it from you.

**Portfolio manager:** About two-thirds of the shortfall came from one decision. We held more lower-rated financials than the benchmark, we were early, and spreads moved against us. That was our judgement and it has cost you money. The remaining third came from being underweight a sector that rallied, which is consistent with the cautious positioning you asked for in the guidelines, but it is still a shortfall.

**Coverage lead:** I want to add two things the report does not show well. First, we have not changed the process, and we would rather you judged that for yourselves, so we have sent your consultant the full position history. Second, there is a version of events where we recover this, and a version where we do not. We think the position is right, but you should not keep the mandate on the strength of our optimism. If the committee wants to set conditions for the watch period, we would prefer to know them.

**Trustee chair:** What would make you close the position?

**Portfolio manager:** If the issuers' capital ratios fall below the level we set when we bought, we sell, whatever the price. I can put that level in writing.
:::

**Difficult trade-off.** Candour versus defending the firm's position. The firm does believe in its strategy, and a coverage professional is not expected to talk the client out of it. The line is between advocating honestly ("here is why we still hold this") and obscuring ("market conditions were challenging").

**Client consequence.** The trustees can make an informed keep-or-terminate decision and can show their regulator and members that they monitored the manager properly {{V}} [@W2-S03].

**Commercial consequence.** {{L:B}} Honesty about underperformance does not guarantee retention. It does change what happens next: a client who believes the explanation may allow time, and a client who leaves after a straight account may return or give a fair reference. A client who feels misled does neither.

### 4.4 Responsiveness

**Definition.** Requests are acknowledged quickly and moved forward visibly. Responsiveness is about the client never wondering whether a message was received. It is not the same as answering instantly.

**Observable behaviour.** A same-day acknowledgement that restates the request, names who is handling it and gives a time. Updates if the time slips.

:::dialogue The holding reply {{IL}}
**Email from a consultant's researcher, 4.40pm:** Can you confirm the strategy's exposure to issuers on the attached list as at month end? Needed for a client paper tomorrow noon.

**Reply from the analyst, 5.05pm:** Received, thank you. To confirm what you need: market-value exposure, as a percentage of the portfolio, to each of the 14 issuers on your list as at 31 October, for the segregated account and not the pooled fund. The holdings data needs to be signed off by our reporting team before it leaves the building, which will happen first thing tomorrow. You will have it by 10am. If any issuer on the list maps to more than one entity in our system, I will show them separately so nothing is netted.
:::

**Difficult trade-off.** Speed versus accuracy. The fastest answer is an unchecked number pulled from a screen. Most firms require client data to pass a control before release, and a wrong number sent quickly has to be corrected publicly. The resolution is usually a fast acknowledgement and a slightly slower, checked answer.

**Client consequence.** The client's own deadlines are protected, and they are spared the work of chasing.

**Commercial consequence.** {{L:B}} Consultants and gatekeepers research many managers. One that is easy to get accurate data from costs them less effort to keep on a recommended list. This is unglamorous and it matters.

### 4.5 Confidentiality

**Definition.** Information learned from or about one client is not passed to another client, a competitor or anyone inside the firm without a need to know.

**Observable behaviour.** Declining to name other clients or describe their decisions. Speaking in aggregates. Not leaving one client's papers visible in another client's meeting.

:::dialogue When a client asks what others are doing {{IL}}
**Finance director (sponsor):** You must cover half the schemes in our industry. What is our nearest competitor's scheme doing about buyout? Are they ahead of us?

**Coverage lead:** I cannot tell you what any individual scheme is doing or even confirm who we work with, in the same way that I would not describe your plans to them. What I can do is tell you what we see across the market in aggregate, and point you to what schemes have published themselves in their annual reports. Your consultant will also have a market-wide view. Would the aggregate picture be useful?

**Finance director:** I thought you would say that. Yes, send the aggregate.
:::

**Difficult trade-off.** Being helpful and interesting versus being discreet. Market colour is valuable, and the most vivid colour is always specific. The person asking is usually testing, consciously or not, how their own information will be treated.

**Client consequence.** The client can speak openly about funding problems, sponsor weakness or internal disagreements, which is exactly the information that makes advice relevant.

**Commercial consequence.** {{L:B}} Candid clients produce better-fitted proposals. A single leak can end not only that relationship but others, because institutional communities are small and trustees sit on several boards.

### 4.6 Willingness to challenge

**Definition.** Raising a reasoned concern when what the client is asking for appears to conflict with the client's own stated objective, and accepting the client's decision once the concern has been heard.

**Observable behaviour.** A question that refers back to the client's own documents. The concern is put once, clearly, with evidence, and where appropriate copied to the adviser.

:::dialogue When a product is unsuitable for what the client has told you {{IL}}
**In-house CIO (client):** The committee likes the yield on your private credit fund. We are thinking of £80m.

**Coverage lead:** I would be glad to take you through it properly, and before we do I want to test it against something you told us in March. You said the sponsor's aim is to be ready for a buy-in within three to four years. This fund has a seven-year term with no right to redeem, and insurers are often unwilling to take illiquid fund units like these as payment for a premium, or will apply a discount. If the timetable holds, you could be a forced seller of an illiquid holding, probably at a discount. Has the timetable changed?

**In-house CIO:** No. The committee was looking at the yield, not the term.

**Coverage lead:** Then I do not think this fund fits, and I would rather say so now than after you have committed. If the aim is more yield without losing the ability to transact, there are liquid credit approaches that an insurer would be more likely to accept, and I can show you ours. You should also ask your consultant to compare them with other managers' versions. I will put this conversation in a short note so your committee has the reasoning.
:::

**Difficult trade-off.** Revenue now versus suitability. The private credit allocation would probably carry a higher fee than the liquid alternative. The coverage professional also has to avoid the opposite error of overriding the client: the manager is not the trustees' adviser, may not know everything, and the decision belongs to the client and their consultant.

**Client consequence.** The client avoids a mismatch between an asset's term and its own plans, or proceeds with the mismatch understood and documented.

**Commercial consequence.** {{L:B}} In the short run, possibly a smaller or lower-fee mandate. In the long run, a client and consultant who have seen the manager argue against its own higher-fee product once will weigh its future recommendations differently. This is a tendency, not a guarantee, and section 5 is frank about the cases where candour is not rewarded.

### 4.7 Transparency about limitations

**Definition.** Volunteering what the strategy, the firm or the individual cannot do, including capacity limits, conflicts of interest, key-person changes and conditions in which the strategy tends to do badly.

**Observable behaviour.** The pitch includes a section on when the strategy underperforms. Team departures are communicated promptly by the manager and not discovered by the client in the trade press. Conflicts are disclosed before the client asks.

:::dialogue Telling a client something they would not have found for a while {{IL}}
**Coverage lead:** Before the quarterly numbers, one piece of news. The deputy portfolio manager on your mandate has resigned and will leave at the end of next month. The lead manager is unchanged, and the firm has named a successor from within the team, whose background I will send you today. I am telling you and your consultant on the same day so that neither of you hears it from the other.

**Fund selector (wealth manager):** Is the successor on the fund's regulatory documents yet?

**Coverage lead:** Not yet. That update is in progress and I do not have a date. I will find out and confirm in writing, and I will not describe the change as complete until it is.
:::

**Difficult trade-off.** Disclosure versus the risk of triggering a review. Many consultants and selectors treat a team change as a reason to re-examine a rating. Telling them promptly invites that review. Delaying only changes when it happens and adds the question of why they were not told.

**Client consequence.** The client can meet its own monitoring duty with current information and is not embarrassed in front of its own committee.

**Commercial consequence.** {{L:B}} A disclosed limitation is priced in once. A discovered one is treated as evidence about everything else the manager has said.

### 4.8 Consistency

**Definition.** The same facts and the same level of candour for every audience, in good periods and bad, and from one representative of the firm to the next.

**Observable behaviour.** The trustee summary, the consultant's data request and the operations file reconcile. The explanation given in a bad quarter uses the same framework as the one used to claim credit in a good quarter. A new coverage person does not contradict their predecessor.

:::dialogue When two audiences compare notes {{IL}}
**Consultant's researcher:** Your trustee presentation says the strategy "does not use derivatives for leverage". The DDQ you sent us says interest rate futures are used and gross exposure can reach 130%. Which is it?

**Coverage lead:** Both documents are ours, so the inconsistency is ours. The DDQ is the precise one: futures are used to manage interest rate exposure, and gross exposure can exceed 100% as a result, while net market exposure stays within the guideline. The slide compressed that into a sentence that reads as a stronger claim than we can support. I will correct the slide, send the corrected version to the trustees with a note explaining the change, and copy you.
:::

**Difficult trade-off.** Tailoring versus contradiction. Different audiences need different levels of detail, and a trustee summary should be simpler than a DDQ. Simplifying must never change the answer.

**Client consequence.** The client's advisers are not put in conflict with each other, and the committee is not making decisions on a simplified statement that is false.

**Commercial consequence.** {{L:B}} Inconsistencies are among the easiest findings for a researcher to document. Each one moves a rating a little, and ratings drive whether a manager is put forward to other clients of the same consultant.

### 4.9 Why friendliness is not enough

Rapport is useful and is a poor substitute for any of the eight components. {{L:B}}

- **A friendly person who is wrong is still wrong.** Warmth without competence produces pleasant meetings and bad decisions.
- **The friend does not decide.** Mandates are awarded by committees on advice. A committee member who argues for a manager because they like the team will be asked for evidence.
- **Rapport biases judgement, which is why process exists.** Liking someone makes it harder to see their product's faults and harder to fire them. Tender rules, consultant research and written advice exist partly to correct for this.
- **Friendliness that avoids hard messages is a service failure.** The coverage professional who cannot bring themselves to deliver bad news promptly is being kind to themselves, not to the client.
- **It does not transfer.** When either person moves on, rapport leaves with them. The other components can be documented and handed over.

### 4.10 Why trust does not remove due diligence, contracts or duties

:::l1
Trusting a manager and checking a manager are not opposites. A hospital can have complete confidence in a surgeon and still count the instruments after every operation. The count is part of what makes the confidence reasonable.
:::

- **The law keeps the duty with the client.** Trustees must take proper advice before investing {{V}} [@W2-S02], and their protection when they delegate to a manager depends on continuing reasonable steps to satisfy themselves about the manager's competence {{V}} [@W2-S03]. "We trusted them" is not a defence.
- **Some decisions must be competed regardless.** Fiduciary management appointments over the 20% threshold require a tender with at least three unconnected bidders {{V}} [@W2-S04].
- **Contracts handle what goodwill cannot.** Guidelines, liability terms, reporting obligations and termination rights exist for the day when people change or things go wrong.
- **The manager has duties of its own.** UK conduct rules require an authorised firm to act honestly, fairly and professionally in accordance with the best interests of its client {{SI}} [@W2-S07]. The exact wording and scope were not read on the primary page and should be checked before being quoted.

{{L:B}} What trust changes is the cost and tone of verification. A trusted manager's data is checked by sampling, not line by line. Questions are asked to understand, not to catch out. Problems are raised early and informally. From the manager's side, welcoming scrutiny is itself a trust behaviour: a firm that makes due diligence easy is signalling that it expects to pass.

## 5. The economics of a relationship, without reducing clients to targets

:::l1
Why do long-term commercial relationships exist at all? If every investment product were fully described on a factsheet and every client could costlessly compare them, clients would simply pick the best one each year and nobody would need a relationship. Relationships exist because that world is not the real one: quality is hard to judge, looking for alternatives is expensive, moving money is costly, and both sides expect to deal with each other again.

Economics gives names to these frictions. The names are useful because they let you say precisely what a relationship is doing in a given situation. They are also dangerous if they become the whole story, because a client is a group of people with responsibilities, not a "switching cost" to be raised.
:::

Each framework below is a lens. For each: the idea, how it shows up in this business, what it assumes, and where it stops working. All of the reasoning is {{L:B}}.

| Lens | The idea in plain words | How it shows up | Where it breaks down |
|---|---|---|---|
| Information asymmetry | The seller knows more about the product than the buyer | The manager knows its positions, people and problems; the client sees reports | Consultants and in-house teams narrow the gap; sometimes the client knows more about its own liabilities than the manager ever will |
| Repeated interactions | People behave better when they expect to deal with each other again | Quarterly reviews for years; a reputation among consultants | End-game behaviour when a relationship is clearly finishing; individuals change jobs |
| Search costs | Finding and assessing alternatives takes time and money | Requests for proposal (RFPs), consultant research, buy lists | Search can be made cheap by intermediaries; cheap search is not always good search |
| Switching costs | Changing provider is costly even if a better one exists | Transition trading costs, legal work, time out of the market, committee time | High switching costs can protect a poor incumbent as easily as a good one |
| Incentives and agency | People act partly on what they are paid and judged for | Managers earn fees on assets; salespeople have targets; consultants have their own business models | People are not only their incentives; professional norms and regulation also shape behaviour |
| Governance | Who is allowed to decide what, and how often | Quarterly committees, delegated limits, tender rules | Formal governance describes the rules, not the informal influence |
| Uncertainty | Outcomes are noisy, so skill and luck are hard to separate | Three years of underperformance may or may not mean the process is broken | It can become an all-purpose excuse |

### 5.1 Information asymmetry

**Idea.** Investment management is close to what economists call a credence good: the buyer struggles to judge quality even after buying, because returns mix skill with luck over long periods. The manager knows things the client cannot easily see, such as how decisions are really made, how stretched the team is, and how the firm treats this client relative to others.

**What a relationship does about it.** It lets the manager transfer information credibly: access to portfolio managers, position-level transparency, early notice of problems, explanations that turn out to be right. Each accurate disclosure, especially an unwelcome one, makes the next statement more believable.

**Assumptions and limits.** The lens assumes the manager is the better-informed party. On the client's liabilities, governance and intentions, the asymmetry runs the other way, and the manager is the one who has to earn disclosure. The lens also understates the role of consultants, whose manager research exists to reduce the gap. And information can be used either way: the same access that informs a client can be used to steer one.

### 5.2 Repeated interactions

**Idea.** If two parties expect to deal with each other many times, the gain from cheating once is outweighed by the loss of future business. Reputation extends this beyond the pair: a consultant who sees one manager mislead one client adjusts their view for all their clients.

**What a relationship does.** It creates a long "shadow of the future". Telling a client today that a product does not fit costs a sale and buys credibility in every later conversation.

**Assumptions and limits.** The logic depends on a future. It weakens when a relationship is obviously ending, when a salesperson expects to have moved firms before consequences arrive, or when rewards are paid on this year's sales only. Firms respond with deferred pay, compliance oversight and client-coverage continuity. No public source examined for this chapter describes how any particular firm calibrates these, so nothing is claimed about Goldman Sachs here. {{NPE}}

### 5.3 Search costs

**Idea.** Finding, comparing and vetting managers is expensive. A full institutional search can involve a long questionnaire, shortlisting, presentations, site visits, operational and legal review, and months of committee time.

**What a relationship does.** An existing, well-documented relationship lowers the cost of the next decision: the client already holds the due diligence, knows the people and has working operational links. That is a real saving.

**Assumptions and limits.** Lower search cost is a legitimate advantage only if the incumbent would still be a reasonable choice in an open comparison. The Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) found in 2018 that half of pension schemes buying fiduciary management took it from their existing investment consultant, and that this reflected an incumbency advantage and low trustee engagement at the point of purchase {{V}} [@W2-S05]. Its remedy was to make a competitive tender mandatory {{V}} [@W2-S04]. That finding concerned consultant-led fiduciary management as the market stood then. It is cited as a documented case of convenience shading into a competition problem, not as a description of any firm today.

### 5.4 Switching costs

**Idea.** Even when a better alternative has been found, moving costs something: trading out of one portfolio and into another, legal fees, operational set-up, the risk of being out of the market during transition, and the governance effort of making and defending the decision. The CMA noted "relatively high costs of switching provider" in fiduciary management {{V}} [@W2-S05].

**What a relationship does.** A good one makes staying worthwhile on its merits. It can also, less admirably, make leaving feel harder than it is.

**Assumptions and limits.** Switching costs are neutral about quality. They protect good and bad incumbents alike. The lens therefore cannot tell you whether a retained client is a satisfied client. That needs a different test, set out in 5.8.

### 5.5 Incentives and agency

**Idea.** A principal (the person whose money or interest is at stake) relies on an agent (someone acting for them) whose incentives differ. This business has a chain of them: members rely on trustees; trustees rely on consultants and managers; the manager's firm relies on its coverage staff.

**How it shows up.**

- The manager is usually paid a percentage of assets, so it earns more when assets stay and grow. That aligns it with the client on performance and against the client on any decision to withdraw.
- A salesperson is typically assessed partly on new assets or revenue. That rewards persistence and can reward pushing.
- A consultant that also offers fiduciary management has an interest in clients moving to that service, which the CMA addressed by requiring marketing of the two to be separated {{V}} [@W2-S05].
- Trustees are mostly not paid on outcomes at all. Their incentive is to make defensible decisions, which favours well-known names and well-documented processes.

**Assumptions and limits.** Agency theory treats people as responding mainly to pay. In practice, professional reputation, regulation, personal standards and firm culture also operate, and the frameworks cannot measure them. Use the lens to locate where a conflict could arise, then look at what actually constrains it.

### 5.6 Governance

**Idea.** Decisions are shaped by who is authorised to make them and how often they meet. A committee that meets quarterly, acts on written advice and must minute its reasons will decide slowly and conservatively.

**What a relationship does.** It works with the client's governance instead of against it: papers arrive in time for the pack, the consultant is briefed, the proposal fits the committee's delegated authority. Much of what looks like relationship skill is governance literacy.

**Assumptions and limits.** Formal governance explains the timetable and the paperwork. It does not capture informal influence, such as a respected chair whose view carries the room, and it varies widely between a large scheme with an in-house team and a small one relying entirely on advisers.

### 5.7 Uncertainty

**Idea.** Returns are noisy. A sound process can underperform for years and a poor one can be lucky. Neither side can be sure which they are looking at.

**What a relationship does.** It supplies context for interpreting noise: was the underperformance consistent with what the manager said the strategy would do in these conditions? A client that was told in advance when the strategy struggles can distinguish "behaving as described" from "broken".

**Assumptions and limits.** "It is just noise" is unfalsifiable in the short run and therefore attractive to a struggling manager. The honest version commits in advance to what evidence would show the process has failed, as the portfolio manager did in the dialogue in 4.3.

### 5.8 Value created versus advantage from inertia

Two managers can each keep a client for ten years for entirely different reasons. {{L:B}}

| | Value through information and execution | Advantage through inertia or dependence |
|---|---|---|
| Why the client stays | It would choose this manager again in an open comparison | Leaving is too much effort, or nobody has looked |
| How the manager behaves at review | Supplies comparison data willingly; welcomes a retender | Delays data; emphasises how disruptive a move would be |
| Reporting | Designed so the client can check the manager | Designed so the client needs the manager to interpret it |
| When the client's needs change | Proposes a smaller or different mandate if that fits | Defends the existing mandate |
| What the client would say privately | "They earn it" | "We have been meaning to review them" |

The **re-tender test** makes this concrete: if this client ran a fresh, fully informed competition tomorrow, would the manager expect to be shortlisted on merit? If yes, the retained revenue reflects value. If the honest answer is "only because moving is a nuisance", the revenue is real but fragile, and it rests on the client's governance being weaker than it should be. Regulators have treated exactly that condition as a market failure to be corrected {{V}} [@W2-S05].

### 5.9 Working for the manager while helping the client decide well

This is the central tension of the job and it should be stated without euphemism.

:::l1
A coverage professional is employed and paid by the asset manager. They are not the client's adviser. The client's adviser, where there is one, is the investment consultant, who is engaged by the trustees for that purpose. So when a coverage professional helps a client think through a decision, they are doing so as a supplier with an interest in the outcome, and an honest one says so.
:::

What keeps the role from being simple advocacy {{L:B}}:

1. **Rules.** Authorised firms have conduct obligations to clients, including acting in accordance with clients' best interests {{SI}} [@W2-S07]. The FCA's Principle 7 requires a firm to communicate information to clients "in a way which is clear, fair and not misleading" {{V}} [@W2-S06]. These are floors, enforced by compliance functions and regulators.
2. **The client's own protections.** Trustees must take proper advice {{V}} [@W2-S02] and continue to monitor {{V}} [@W2-S03]. The system does not rely on the manager's virtue.
3. **Repeated dealing.** As in 5.2, misleading a client is usually bad business over any horizon longer than a year.
4. **Personal standards.** Some things a person will not say, whatever the incentive. This cannot be sourced or measured, and it matters.

None of these removes the tension. They bound it. An honest account of the role admits that there will be moments when what is best for the client costs the firm revenue, and that the individual will feel the pull of their own targets at that moment.

:::example When the best client outcome shrinks the manager's assets: de-risking to buyout
A real and common pattern in UK defined benefit pensions. As a scheme's funding improves, trustees and sponsors often aim to pass the liabilities to an insurer through a bulk annuity (a "buy-in", then "buyout"). LCP counted 367 buy-ins totalling £38.2bn in 2025, after £49.1bn in 2023 and £47.8bn in 2024 {{V}} [@W2-S08]. When a scheme insures, the assets are paid to the insurer as premium. The scheme's asset managers lose those mandates.

Consider a hypothetical manager with a £300m credit mandate at an illustrative 0.20% fee, about £600,000 a year {{IL}}. The scheme decides to target a buy-in in three years. The possible responses, in descending order of quality:

- **Help the client get there.** Propose restructuring the mandate so the portfolio holds assets an insurer is more likely to accept and so that cash is available on the transaction date. This may mean a lower-fee mandate and a known end date. The manager earns less for three years and then nothing.
- **Stay neutral and serve the existing mandate well** until notice is given, then run a clean exit.
- **Argue against the buy-in** because it ends the fees. A manager may hold a sincere view that running the scheme on is better for members, and some schemes do choose that. But the manager is conflicted, and the argument should come through the trustees' adviser with the conflict stated.
- **Obstruct quietly**: slow data, emphasise transition risk, hope the timetable slips. This is the inertia strategy from 5.8.

What the first response buys is not guaranteed. The trustees and the sponsor's finance team will work elsewhere later. The consultant advises other schemes. Some of the assets may end up with an insurer that itself hires external managers. None of that is certain, and a coverage professional who helps a client leave should do it because it is the right service, not as a disguised investment in future sales.
:::

**What a junior can and cannot do about this tension.** A summer analyst or first-year analyst does not decide what the firm recommends, cannot commit the firm to fee changes or mandate restructures, and should not offer personal views on whether a client should stay. What they can do is make sure the numbers and documents the client receives are accurate and complete, record what the client actually said, and raise with their manager anything that looks misleading before it is sent. That is a real contribution to the honest version of the job.

### 5.10 When a relationship should end

Retention is a commercial measure. It is not, by itself, evidence of a good outcome for the client. {{L:B}} A mandate should probably end, or shrink, when:

- **The need has gone.** The liabilities have been insured, the fund has been wound up, or the client's strategy no longer includes the asset class.
- **The strategy has stopped doing what it was hired to do,** judged against criteria agreed in advance, and the manager cannot credibly explain how that will change.
- **A materially cheaper equivalent exists** and the manager cannot show what the fee difference buys.
- **The firm can no longer serve the client properly:** the team has left, the strategy is at capacity, or the client is too small to receive adequate attention.
- **Trust has been broken** in a way that makes the client re-verify everything, at which point the relationship costs both sides more than it is worth.
- **The client's governance has changed,** for example an outsourced chief investment officer (OCIO) or fiduciary manager now selects managers and prefers its own line-up.

A good ending has observable features: accurate final reporting, cooperation with the transition manager, prompt return of assets, no disparagement of the successor, and a complete file. The coverage professional's conduct during an exit is seen by the consultant, the trustees and the operations teams, all of whom will be somewhere else in five years.

:::warn The cost of keeping a client who should have left {{IL}}
Suppose a manager retains a £300m mandate for two extra years by making a review feel too difficult, earning an illustrative £1.2m in fees. The client then leaves anyway. The consultant's researcher records that data was slow and the manager resisted comparison. That note sits behind the manager's rating across the consultant's other clients. The £1.2m is visible in the accounts. The mandates never offered are not. This example is an argument, not a measurement: nobody can calculate the second number, which is precisely why it is easy to ignore.
:::

## 6. The relationship over time: branches, not a success story

:::l1
Most descriptions of long-term relationships are told backwards from a happy ending: the client was won, trust grew, the mandate doubled. Real relationships branch. At several points over twenty years something happens that neither side controls, and what follows depends on how it is handled and on luck. This section follows one hypothetical client through those branch points. Everything in it is invented. {{IL}}
:::

**Starting position (year 0).** A £1.2bn UK DB scheme appoints a manager to run £300m of investment-grade credit after a consultant-led search. The coverage lead is a vice president; an analyst supports. The scheme has a two-person in-house team, a professional trustee as investment chair and a consultant with a "buy" rating on the strategy.

### 6.1 Years 0 to 5: early tests

| Branch point | What happens | If handled well | If handled badly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1: onboarding error | A guideline is coded wrongly and a small ineligible holding is bought | Manager finds it, reports it the same day, corrects at its own cost, explains the control fix. Client records a manager that self-reports | Client's custodian finds it first. Every later report is checked line by line |
| Year 2: underperformance | The mandate lags its benchmark by 1.8% | The conversation in 4.3. Consultant keeps the rating "on watch"; trustees set conditions; manager meets them | Explanation shifts between meetings. Consultant downgrades. Mandate terminated in year 4 |
| Year 3: client's investment manager leaves | The in-house contact who championed the manager moves to another scheme | Relationship was already spread across chair, consultant and operations. Successor inherits a documented history | Relationship was one friendship. Successor has no reason to defend an underperforming incumbent |
| Year 4: coverage lead leaves the manager | The vice president resigns to join a competitor | Handover notes, joint introduction call, analyst (now an associate) provides continuity. Client barely notices | Nobody tells the client for a month. The history left with the person. Client takes a call from the competitor |

Notice what did not decide the outcomes: nobody on the client side stayed or left because of a dinner. The branch that mattered each time was whether the relationship had been built on more than one person and whether the record existed.

### 6.2 Years 5 to 10: the client's needs change

By year 6 the scheme's funding position has improved. The sponsor wants to remove the pension risk from its balance sheet. Three branches open.

**Branch A: shrink and reshape.** The trustees decide to target a buy-in around year 9. The manager proposes converting the mandate to a lower-turnover credit portfolio designed to be held to the transaction date. Assets fall from £300m to £220m as the scheme sells growth assets and reallocates across managers. The illustrative fee rate falls. Revenue roughly halves. In year 9 the buy-in completes and the mandate ends with a clean transition. In year 11 the scheme's former investment chair, now chairing a different scheme, includes the manager on a tender list. In year 12 the sponsor's former treasurer, now at another company, asks for a meeting about cash management. Neither of these was promised and either might not have happened.

**Branch B: run-on.** The trustees and sponsor decide not to insure and to run the scheme on, seeking surplus. The mandate is retained and a second mandate is added in year 8. The relationship deepens. It also becomes more demanding: reporting requirements grow, fees are renegotiated downward with breakpoints, and the client benchmarks the manager against two competitors every three years. Growth here is earned repeatedly, not once.

**Branch C: the decision moves elsewhere.** The trustees appoint a fiduciary manager after a competitive tender, as the rules require above the 20% threshold {{V}} [@W2-S04]. The fiduciary manager now chooses the underlying managers. The original manager's client is, in practice, no longer the trustees but the fiduciary manager's research team, which has its own preferred list. The mandate is terminated in year 7 despite good performance and a good relationship with the trustees. Nothing was done wrong. The lesson is that a relationship is with a decision structure, and structures change.

### 6.3 Years 10 to 20: institutions change

Follow Branch B, the surviving relationship.

| Branch point | What happens | Possible paths |
|---|---|---|
| Year 11: strategy relevance | A low-cost systematic credit product appears that delivers most of the mandate's return for a fraction of the fee | The manager offers its own lower-cost version and keeps the assets at lower revenue; or defends the active mandate with evidence and keeps part of it; or loses it. Refusing to engage loses it slowly |
| Year 13: merger | The sponsor is acquired. Two pension schemes are merged, each with its own managers and consultant | A fresh review of all managers. Incumbency counts for little; the file and the record count for a lot. The manager may win the combined mandate, lose its half, or be asked to share |
| Year 15: trust lost | A pricing error overstates the mandate's value for two months; a trustee report is wrong | Rebuilt: immediate disclosure, independent review, compensation, senior attendance at the next meeting, a year of closer reporting. Or not rebuilt: defensiveness, a second error, termination. Rebuilding takes far longer than the original building did |
| Year 17: consultant change | The scheme changes consultant after its own periodic review. The new consultant does not rate the strategy | The manager submits to a full new research process. A long relationship with trustees earns the hearing, not the outcome |
| Year 20: end state | The two decades close | In one path the manager runs £500m across three mandates for the merged scheme. In another it was replaced in year 13 and has not been back. Both paths began from the same year 0 and the same good intentions |

:::warn What the branching shows
Good conduct raises the odds at each branch. It does not determine the result. Clients leave well-run relationships for reasons that have nothing to do with service, and poor relationships sometimes survive on inertia. Anyone who tells you that trust always pays is describing the branch they remember.
:::

### 6.4 Individual credibility and the institutional relationship

:::l1
A coverage professional's name becomes known to clients and consultants, and that personal credibility is real. But the mandate is a contract between the client and the firm. The fees go to the firm. The portfolio is run by other people. The records belong to the firm. The employee is the firm's representative in the relationship, not its owner.
:::

{{L:B}} Three implications.

- **Credibility is portable; clients are not.** A person who moves firms takes their reputation and their knowledge of how clients think. They do not take the mandate, which the client awarded to a firm's investment capability after a formal process, and employment contracts in the industry commonly restrict approaching former clients for a period. What any particular firm's contracts say is not public and is not asserted here. {{NPE}}
- **The firm's job is to make the relationship survive the person.** That means more than one point of contact on each side, senior and junior coverage, direct links between operations teams and between the portfolio manager and the client's investment staff.
- **The individual's job is to make themselves replaceable in the file and valuable in the room.** These are compatible. The person who documents everything is the one a firm can safely promote, because their clients can be handed on.

### 6.5 Relationship knowledge as organisational knowledge

What a coverage team learns about a client is an asset only if someone else can use it. In practice this means a customer relationship management (CRM) system holding contacts, call notes, meeting records, open actions and the history of decisions, with access controlled so that confidential client information reaches only those who need it.

:::workproduct A call note that turns a conversation into organisational knowledge {{IL}}
**Client:** hypothetical DB scheme. **Date:** 12 March. **Attendees:** in-house investment manager (client); coverage lead, analyst (manager).

- **Facts stated by the client:** sub-committee meets 14 May; papers due 5 May. Consultant is reviewing all credit managers in the third quarter. Sponsor has asked trustees for an indicative buy-in timetable by year end.
- **Client's concerns, in their words:** "The chair does not want another quarter like the last one explained in jargon."
- **What we committed to:** one-page plain-English attribution summary by 28 April (owner: analyst to draft, portfolio manager to approve). Climate methodology note by 5 May (owner: product specialist).
- **What we do not know:** whether the buy-in timetable would cover the whole scheme or the pensioner section only. To ask at the May meeting.
- **Judgement, labelled as such:** coverage lead's view is that the mandate is secure through the consultant review if the plain-English reporting improves. This is an opinion, not something the client said.
- **Sensitivity:** the buy-in timetable is not public. Restricted to the coverage team and the portfolio manager.

Notice the separations: fact from opinion, the client's words from the manager's interpretation, known from unknown, and who may see it. A note like this lets a successor take the next call without asking the client to repeat themselves.
:::

**What happens when the knowledge stays in one head.** {{L:B}}

- The client has to re-explain its situation to each new contact, and reasonably concludes the firm does not listen.
- Promises made verbally are missed because nobody else knew they existed.
- The firm repeats a proposal the client rejected two years ago for a reason nobody recorded.
- Sensitivities are lost: the new person raises the subject the previous person knew to avoid.
- The firm cannot tell whether a departing employee's clients are at risk, because it does not know what those relationships consisted of.
- From the client's side, their own institutional memory of the manager is also lost if their contact kept nothing, which is why careful clients minute manager meetings.

There is a tension here too. Recording everything takes time away from clients, and people are sometimes reluctant to write down the knowledge that makes them valuable. A firm that wants the notes written has to make them useful to the writer and has to reward handing clients on well.

## 7. Where a junior fits

A summer analyst will not own a relationship, lead a review meeting or negotiate a fee. It is still possible to say precisely how junior work connects to everything above. {{L:B}}

| Junior task | Trust component it supports | Client outcome | Commercial consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Checking every figure in a review pack against source | Competence, consistency | The committee decides on correct numbers | No public correction; the consultant's file stays clean |
| Maintaining the action tracker after meetings | Reliability | Promised items arrive | No minutable service complaint |
| Drafting a same-day holding reply for approval | Responsiveness | The client's deadline is protected | The manager is easy to deal with |
| Writing the call note | Institutional continuity | The client is not asked twice | The relationship survives staff changes |
| Researching a prospect's published accounts and governance before a first meeting | Competence | The meeting starts from the client's actual situation | Fewer wasted meetings; better-fitted proposals |
| Flagging a slide that says more than the DDQ supports | Honesty, consistency | The client is not misled | A problem is caught before a researcher finds it |

What a junior does not do: state house investment views as their own, commit the firm to dates, prices or terms without approval, share one client's information with another, or decide what a client is told about performance. Knowing these limits, and asking when unsure, is itself part of being trustworthy.

## 8. Translation table: from abstract words to observable facts

Use this to test any sentence you write or say about this subject. If a claim cannot be moved from the first column to the second, it is not yet saying anything.

| Abstract word | Observable action | The difficult choice it involves | Concrete consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Trust" | The client asks your view before deciding and accepts "I will check" | Admitting you do not know versus sounding fluent | Fewer re-checks by the client; earlier sight of new decisions |
| "Relationship" | More than one person on each side knows the history, and it is written down | Time spent documenting versus time spent with clients | The mandate survives when either contact leaves |
| "Understanding the client" | You can state who proposes, advises, decides, vetoes and implements, and what each is worried about this year | Asking basic questions versus assuming | Proposals arrive at the right time, to the right people, in a form they can approve |
| "Adding value" | The client changed, delayed or avoided a decision because of something you supplied, and can say so | Supplying information that argues against your own product | Demonstrable in a re-tender; survives a fee challenge |
| "Honesty" | Bad news delivered first, by you, with cause separated from excuse | Defending the firm's position versus obscuring it | An informed keep-or-leave decision; a fair reference either way |
| "Service" | Requests acknowledged the same day with an owner and a time; deadlines met or renegotiated before they pass | Speed versus checked accuracy | The client stops chasing; no service complaint on the minutes |
| "Partnership" | You proposed something that reduced your own fees because it fitted the client's objective | Revenue now versus suitability | Sometimes later mandates and references; sometimes simply lower revenue |
| "Loyalty" | The client would choose you again in an open, informed competition | Welcoming a re-tender versus making one feel difficult | Retention that reflects merit, not inertia |
| "Long-term" | Conduct during an exit is as careful as during a pitch | Effort for a client who is leaving versus effort for prospects | What the consultant and trustees say about you at their next organisation |
| "Passion for clients" | You read the scheme's annual report before the meeting and found the question nobody had asked | Preparation that may never be noticed versus visible activity | Occasionally, the insight that changes the meeting. Often, nothing visible at all |

- It describes general industry mechanics and UK rules. It does not describe Goldman Sachs's internal practices, incentives, contracts or client arrangements, which are not public. {{NPE}}
- The statutory and regulatory citations were read on the dates shown in the source register. Regulation 4 and the tendering guidance may have been amended since their last-updated dates; one FCA rule could not be read on the primary page and is marked {{SI}}.
- The economic lenses are simplifications. Each is labelled with what it assumes.
- Every dialogue, scenario, fee and outcome is illustrative. Real conversations are messier, and real outcomes depend on facts no example can include.
- The chapter argues that candour and good exits tend to be rewarded over time. That is a reasoned view {{L:B}}, not a measured result, and the chapter gives cases where they are not.
:::
