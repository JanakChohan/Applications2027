# Teams and business structure

This chapter reconstructs how Goldman Sachs Asset Management's client business is organised in Europe, the Middle East and Africa (EMEA), and what each part of it actually sells to whom. It starts from the former intern's account, tests each piece against public sources, and keeps the two apart.

Three warnings before the detail.

- Goldman Sachs does not publish an organisation chart for this business, and it discloses no revenue, assets, flows or headcount for the Client Solutions Group, for any EMEA client team or for the UK [@R2-S01]. {{V}} Everything below is assembled from job adverts, press releases, trade press and one first-hand account.
- A team name in a job advert shows that the name was in use on that date. It does not show who reports to whom.
- Where the only source is the call with the former intern, the statement carries the badge {{UA}}. Treat those as leads you could ask about, not as facts you could assert.

## 1. Why a single hierarchy misleads

:::l1
Imagine a shop that sells to schools, to restaurants and to other shops, in nine countries, and has one product so different from the rest (say, ice) that it needs its own delivery vans. You could draw that business by country, by type of customer, or by product. Each drawing would be true and each would hide something. The people in it would also tell you they do one of two jobs: finding new customers, or looking after the ones the shop already has.

Goldman Sachs Asset Management's client business is like that. It can be cut four ways:

1. **By where the client is.** UK and Ireland, Benelux, the Nordics, Germany and Austria, the Middle East and so on.
2. **By what kind of client it is.** An institution investing its own pool of money, or an intermediary choosing funds for other people's money.
3. **By product line.** Some products (cash funds, and whole-portfolio outsourcing) are different enough to have their own specialists.
4. **By job.** Winning new business, or serving and keeping existing clients.

The analogy stops being accurate in one important place. A shop's customers buy goods and leave. An asset manager's clients hand over money that stays, under a contract, for years, and can be withdrawn. So "looking after existing customers" is not after-sales service. It is where most of the revenue sits.
:::

:::l2
The former intern described a clean tree: coverage team (mostly regional), then client type inside each team, then sales versus relationship management inside each client type. {{UA}} Public evidence supports all four cuts but not the tree.

- **Geography and channel cross each other.** Job adverts show country or region teams (Benelux, Nordics, Germany and Austria, France, Italy, Switzerland) crossed with channel (Institutional, Third Party Wealth, Insurance, Liquidity) [@R1-S01][@R1-S05][@R1-S06][@R1-S18]. {{SI}} At the same time there are channel leaders above the countries: a co-head of EMEA Institutional Client Coverage [@R6-S02] and, until spring 2026, a head of the EMEA Third Party Wealth Business [@R4-S01][@R4-S02]. {{V}} A UK institutional salesperson therefore sits in a geography and in a channel at once.
- **Some businesses are global verticals.** Liquidity Solutions and Insurance each have named global heads [@R5-S01][@R5-S02]. {{V}} They are not regions.
- **One "team" is not in the client organisation at all.** The outsourced chief investment officer (OCIO) business sits within Multi-Asset Solutions, an investing group [@R6-S18][@R1-S15]. {{V}}
- **The job split is real in name but blurred in practice.** Goldman defines a Client Coverage Group "dedicated to retaining, expanding and supporting existing client relationships" [@R1-S11] {{V}}, yet a London Client Solutions Group team is described as "responsible for new business development and managing existing institutional client relationships" [@R1-S01]. {{V}}

A matrix states this honestly. A tree would have to invent reporting lines to look complete.
:::

## 4. UK Institutional

Profile: [UK Institutional](#T-uk-inst).

:::l1
An institutional client is an organisation investing a large pool of money that belongs to, or is owed to, other people: a pension scheme, a charity's endowment, a state investment fund. It buys investment management in big pieces, under a negotiated contract, after a formal selection. The team that covers UK institutions spends its time understanding what a small number of such organisations need, getting Goldman's strategies considered when they choose a manager, and keeping the organisations that have already hired it well served.
:::

### What is established about the team

The label "UK Institutional" is the former intern's. {{UA}} The nearest public anchors are a 2026-cycle London campus listing titled "Client Solutions Group, UK Institutional Sales", seen only in a search result after the page expired [@R1-S31] {{SI}}, and the senior title "head of the UK and Ireland institutional business", held by the person who is now co-head of EMEA Institutional Client Coverage [@R6-S05][@R6-S02]. {{V}} Goldman's EMEA institutional marketing defines the institutional business as pension funds, endowments, foundations, sovereign wealth funds and insurers [@R1-S20]. {{V}} A head of EMEA defined contribution within the Client Solutions Group was reported in June 2025 [@R6-S26]. {{SI}}

Not established: the current head of UK institutional sales, the size of the team, whether it is formally split into sales and relationship management, and which client types beyond pension schemes it covers day to day. {{NPE}}

### The clients, one type at a time

Chapter 1 explains defined benefit and defined contribution pensions, liability-driven investment and the chain from beneficiary to manager. This section adds what each client is trying to do in November 2026 and how it buys.

:::l2
**Corporate defined benefit (DB) schemes.** The scheme has promised pensions linked to salary and service. The promise is the liability. Trustees hold the assets and must invest in the best interests of members [@W2-S01]; before investing they must obtain and consider proper advice [@W2-S02]; they may delegate investment decisions to a fund manager [@W2-S03]. {{V}} The sponsoring employer stands behind any shortfall.

- *Objective.* Pay every pension, with as little reliance on the employer as possible.
- *Liabilities.* Long, and sensitive to interest rates and inflation, which is why schemes hedge with liability-driven investment (LDI).
- *Liquidity.* Rising. Most schemes are closed, so pensions paid out exceed contributions coming in, and LDI collateral calls need cash at short notice.
- *Position today.* Strong. The 4,838 schemes in the Pension Protection Fund's index had an aggregate surplus of £275.4bn and a funding ratio of 133.7% at the end of October 2026 [@R3-S29][@R3-S30]. {{V}}
- *The decision that follows.* With a surplus, the question is the endgame: sell the liabilities to an insurer (a buy-in or buyout), or run the scheme on and use the surplus. 2025 saw a record 370 buy-ins but lower total premium, about £38bn [@R3-S27][@R3-S28]. {{V}} The Pension Schemes Act 2026 makes it easier for trustees to release surplus to sponsors, with regulations expected in force in 2027 [@R3-S20]. {{V}}
- *Governance.* A trustee board, often with an investment sub-committee, meeting quarterly. Some lay trustees, some professional. Advised by an investment consultant.

**Defined contribution (DC) master trusts and providers.** Here there is no promise. Members' pots are invested in a default fund chosen by the provider.

- *Objective.* Good member outcomes at a cost low enough to win employers.
- *Constraint.* Price. Defaults are built to tight fee budgets, which limits what an active or private-markets manager can charge.
- *What is changing.* Scale and private assets. Under the 2026 Act, master trust and group personal pension defaults must reach at least £25bn by 2030 or be on track by 2035 [@R3-S20]. {{V}} Seventeen providers signed the voluntary Mansion House Accord in May 2025, aiming for 10% of default funds in private markets by 2030, at least half of it in the UK [@R3-S23]. {{V}}
- *Governance.* A small number of professional investment teams. Fewer, larger buyers every year.

**The Local Government Pension Scheme (LGPS).** Funded public-sector DB schemes run by local authorities.

- *Objective.* Pay pensions while keeping employer (taxpayer) contributions stable. Open to new members, so the horizon is very long.
- *What has changed.* Who chooses managers. From 30 June 2026 funds must delegate implementation of their investment strategy to their pool and take principal investment advice from it; the number of pools has fallen from eight to six [@R3-S21][@R3-S22]. {{V}}
- *Consequence.* {{L:B}} A manager now sells to six pools' investment teams, not to dozens of council committees. A mandate can be won or lost at pool level in one decision. The public record shows both for Goldman: an emerging-markets equity sleeve at Border to Coast in 2023 and a later change in another of its funds ([M-R7-21](#M-R7-21), [M-R7-22](#M-R7-22)).

**Endowments, foundations and charities.** A pool meant to fund a purpose indefinitely.

- *Objective.* Spend a steady amount each year while keeping the real value of the capital. There is no dated liability; there is a spending rule.
- *Liquidity.* Low need, which is why this group can hold more private and illiquid assets than a mature pension scheme.
- *Governance.* Trustees and an investment committee, often volunteers with senior investment backgrounds, usually advised by a consultant or an outsourced provider.
- *Caveat.* This paragraph is general industry knowledge. {{SI}} The research did not reach primary sources on UK charity investment rules before the search allowance ran out, and no public Goldman mandate from a UK endowment or charity was found. {{NPE}} The former intern's project on endowments and foundations suggests the team researches them; it does not show they are clients. {{UA}}

**Sovereign and official institutions.** Covered under the Middle East heading below, since that is where the public evidence sits.
:::

### How the selection actually runs

:::l1
An institution rarely picks a manager by itself. It pays an investment consultant to advise it, and the consultant usually organises the search: writes the brief, draws up a list of managers it rates, sends out a long questionnaire, scores the answers and invites three or four managers to present in person. People in the industry call that last meeting a "beauty parade". The trustees decide; the consultant shapes what they see.
:::

:::l2
This is why coverage has two audiences. The **scheme** (trustees, and the in-house investment staff where there are any) is the client. The **consultant** is the gatekeeper. A strategy with no consultant rating may never be shortlisted, whatever the relationship with the scheme. [Consultant Relations](#T-consultant) exists for that second audience: Goldman's own description is that it works with the regional institutional sales teams and builds a pipeline for them [@R1-S21]. {{V}}

In the public UK cases a named adviser ran or supported every selection: Redington for the BAE Systems trustees, Isio for Shell, PwC for Short Brothers, Barnett Waddingham for Calor [@R3-S02][@R3-S04][@R3-S15][@R7-S16]. {{V}} The consulting sector is itself consolidating into larger groups [@R3-S34] {{SI}}, which concentrates that gatekeeping in fewer firms (see [D-R3-08](#D-R3-08)).
:::

{{L:B}} **What the evidence suggests about the work.** Almost every publicly named UK institutional relationship of Goldman's is a pension scheme, and most of the recent ones are OCIO appointments. That does not mean the institutional team only sells OCIO. Single-strategy mandates are rarely announced, so the public record is biased toward the large delegations that produce press releases. It does suggest that the most visible UK growth has come from schemes delegating more, which makes the boundary between "institutional sales" and "OCIO" a working relationship, not a wall.

## 5. UK Third Party Wealth

Profile: [UK Third Party Wealth](#T-uk-tpw).

:::l1
"Third party" means the wealth firm is someone else's, not Goldman's. A private bank or wealth manager looks after its own clients' money and needs funds to put it in. Goldman's Third Party Wealth team tries to get Goldman funds chosen by those firms. The team's client is the firm. The person whose savings end up in the fund is the firm's client, and the team will usually never meet them.
:::

### What is established about the team

"Third Party Wealth" is Goldman's own name. A press release names the "EMEA Third Party Wealth Business" [@R4-S01] and a job advert names the "UK Third Party Wealth (TPW) Sales Team", "responsible for new business development" with "private banks, wealth managers, financial advisors and multi-managers" [@R1-S17]. {{V}} A second advert, seen through an aggregator, calls it the "UK & Ireland Third Party Wealth team" and adds funds of funds and independent financial advisers to the client list [@R4-S08]. {{SI}} Globally the business has two co-heads [@R6-S08] {{V}}; the EMEA head since 2021 left in spring 2026 and no successor had been named in the sources found [@R4-S02] (see [D-R4-11](#D-R4-11)). In Goldman's results the nearest reported channel is "Third-party distributed", $714bn of long-term assets under supervision at 30 September 2026 [@R2-S02] {{V}}, a firm-wide figure that is not proven to match the Third Party Wealth business in scope and says nothing about the UK.

Not established: the head of the UK and Ireland team, and whether it has a separate relationship-management side. The advert describes only new business development. {{NPE}}

### The intermediary chain

:::l2
Five different parties stand between the fund and the saver. Each can say no.

| Link | Who it is | What it decides | What it needs from the manager |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gatekeeper | A fund selector or research analyst at the wealth firm | Whether a fund is good enough to recommend internally | Process, people, performance and risk evidence; access to the portfolio manager |
| Investment committee | Senior investment staff | Whether the fund goes on the approved (buy) list or into a model portfolio | A clear role for the fund and a price that passes the firm's value test |
| Platform | The administration system the firm deals and holds assets through | Whether the right share class is available to buy | Operational set-up, share-class terms, data feeds |
| Adviser or portfolio manager | The person running client portfolios | Whether to actually use an approved fund | A reason to pick it over the other approved funds; updates they can pass on |
| End investor | The private client | Whether to invest at all, and to stay | Nothing directly from the manager |

**Why being on the approved list does not guarantee assets.** {{L:B}} Approval is permission, not a purchase. After it, separate people decide whether to allocate; the platform must carry the share class; and clients must have new money or be willing to switch. Each step can stall for reasons that have nothing to do with the fund. A buy list may hold several funds for the same slot, so approval can mean "one of four options". And firms increasingly run central model portfolios, where one committee's choice moves every client's money at once: the UK model portfolio market was reported at about £190bn [@R4-S37]. {{SI}} That raises the prize for being chosen and the cost of being removed. The fee calculator's two wealth cases show the arithmetic: the listing looks identical on day one whether advisers adopt the fund or ignore it.

**The rules that shape the conversation.** Platforms have been restricted in taking payments from fund providers since rules that took effect in April 2014 [@R4-S33], so shelf space cannot simply be bought. UK fund managers must assess the value of their funds at least annually [@R4-S32], and under the Consumer Duty, which has four outcomes including price and value [@R4-S31], distributors must be able to show their retail customers get fair value. {{V}} Most of Goldman's European funds are domiciled outside the UK, so the Overseas Funds Regime, under which EEA UCITS can be recognised for sale to UK retail investors, matters to what can be offered [@R4-S30]. {{V}} The commercial effect is that price and evidence of value are part of every listing discussion (see [D-R4-06](#D-R4-06)).
:::

### What the team has to sell

Recent product moves are aimed squarely at this channel. Goldman entered European active exchange-traded funds in 2025 and said in April 2026 that it had launched 13 active ETFs in 2025 [@R4-S01][@R4-S17]. {{V}} Its G-Series evergreen private-markets funds are sold through its own Private Wealth Management business and "select third-party distributors" [@R4-S19], and in March 2026 it added its first evergreen fund under the revised European long-term investment fund rules [@R4-S20]. {{V}} A separate "Third Party Wealth Alternatives" team in EMEA specialises in these products [@R4-S11]. {{SI}} No Goldman Long-Term Asset Fund (the UK vehicle) and no UK model portfolio service were found. {{NPE}}

One related point from the call checks out in part. Goldman's own Private Wealth Management describes an open-architecture alternatives platform that includes external managers [@R4-S27] {{V}}, so it is a buyer of other firms' products as well as a channel for Goldman's. The specific examples the former intern gave are not publicly established. {{NPE}}

## 6. OCIO and fiduciary management

Profile: [OCIO / fiduciary management](#T-ocio). This is the area where the former intern's account needed the most correction, so it is treated at length.

:::l1
Most pension trustees are not full-time investors. Running a multi-billion-pound portfolio means choosing an asset mix, hiring and firing a dozen specialist managers, hedging the liabilities, moving money between them and reporting on all of it. An outsourced chief investment officer is a firm the trustees hire to do that job for them. The trustees keep the big decisions (how much risk, what the goal is). The provider makes the day-to-day ones.

It is a different sale from "please hire us to run a bond portfolio". The client is handing over the keys, not buying one product.
:::

### Two names for nearly the same thing

Goldman uses "OCIO" and "fiduciary management" for this business and says the terms are used interchangeably and mean different things in different places [@R3-S08]. {{V}} Its own working distinction is useful:

| | OCIO (Goldman's usage) | Fiduciary management (Goldman's usage) |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets strategy | Trustees, usually with a third-party adviser | The manager advises, trustees approve |
| What is delegated | Carrying out the agreed policy | "All remaining functions", including asset allocation, reporting and analytics |
| Typical user, in Goldman's view | Larger schemes with experienced in-house staff | Smaller schemes with limited resources |
| Oversight | Scheme staff or a third party | Goldman suggests a separate strategic adviser |

Source for the table: [@R3-S08]. {{V}} It is one provider's framing, not an industry standard.

### Scope: not UK-only

The former intern was "pretty sure" OCIO exists only in the UK team. {{UA}} As a statement about Goldman Sachs it is contradicted. {{CE}}

- Goldman reported $480bn of OCIO assets under supervision at 31 March 2026 [@R3-S09], and its product page states "$540+ billion" at 30 June 2026, citing rankings by a research firm as the largest OCIO provider in the United States and second largest globally on 2024 assets [@R8-S22]. {{V}} The two figures come from different pages and dates and were not reconciled.
- The largest mandates are American: UPS's US and Canadian plans in 2024 [@R7-S21] and Verizon and Lockheed Martin in July 2026 [@R3-S09]. {{V}}
- Goldman cites growth in the Netherlands, Germany and Switzerland [@R3-S10], and a "Head of EMEA OCIO Institutional Business" title exists [@R3-S11]. {{V}} An OCIO Insurance team works from The Hague, with London and New York [@R1-S15]. {{V}}

{{L:B}} A charitable reading of what he meant: among the London coverage teams he could see, only the UK one had an OCIO sub-team. That may be true and is not public. Either way, do not repeat "OCIO is UK-only" in an interview.

### Where it sits

The fiduciary and OCIO teams sit within Multi-Asset Solutions, an investment group [@R6-S18][@R1-S15] {{V}}, supported by more than 300 people as at November 2025, a number described then as still growing [@R3-S07]. {{V}} So OCIO is not a client-coverage team in the way UK Institutional is. Client coverage works alongside it: the co-head of EMEA Institutional Client Coverage is quoted in the Shell announcement [@R6-S02], a head of sales and distribution for UK fiduciary management was reported in 2022 [@R6-S27] {{SI}}, and a "Client Relationship Manager (OCIO)" title has been advertised in London (search result only) [@R1-S33]. {{SI}} The remark that the team is "already over-hired" has no public support; the public evidence is of continued senior hiring in 2025 [@R3-S07]. {{NPE}}

### What the provider is responsible for

:::l2
From the public descriptions of Goldman's UK mandates [@R3-S15][@R3-S07][@R3-S08] {{V}}:

- **Asset allocation** within the risk budget the trustees set (and, in full fiduciary management, advice on the budget itself).
- **Manager selection**, using "strategies run by both internal and external managers". The use of external managers matters: the provider is not simply filling the portfolio with its own funds, and trustees will ask how it handles the conflict when it does choose its own.
- **Liability hedging** tailored to the scheme.
- **Implementation and transition**: moving the assets from the old arrangement to the new.
- **Reporting** to trustees.

What it does not take over: the trustees' legal responsibility for the scheme, the funding agreement with the employer, and the decision on the endgame. Arrangements range "from fully delegated fiduciary models to hybrid structures involving consultants and/or independent oversight providers" [@R3-S07]. At BAE Systems the trustees' strategic adviser stayed on to oversee implementation [@R3-S02]. {{V}}
:::

### The tender rule

UK trust-based schemes must run a competitive tender, inviting bids from at least three unconnected providers, before fiduciary managers manage 20% or more of scheme assets [@R3-S24]. {{V}} The duty came from a competition inquiry and has sat in pensions regulations since 1 October 2022. Its practical effect on the sale is large: there is always a formal process, usually run by an independent evaluator, with scored proposals and standardised performance data (see [D-R3-07](#D-R3-07)). A warm relationship with the trustees gets a provider invited. It does not get it appointed.

### Fees, as far as they are public

Goldman's OCIO fee rates are not disclosed. {{NPE}} Three pieces of public evidence show the structure.

- A UK scheme's annual report (scheme name withheld) describes "a fiduciary fee based on the asset value" of the return-seeking and cashflow-driven portfolios, with the fiduciary manager able to negotiate fees with the underlying managers and the LDI manager invoicing separately [@R3-S17]. {{V}} That is another provider's arrangement and shows the shape, not Goldman's price.
- In Goldman's own filings, alternative-asset accounts classed as "advisory and OCIO" averaged $112bn in 2025 and earned an average 17 basis points, against much higher rates for funds and discretionary accounts [@R2-S01]. {{V}} That is a firm-wide blend for one asset class, reported together with advisory accounts, and cannot be read as a mandate price.
- Across the UK market, fiduciary fees fell about 27% over five years, with the sharpest competition for mandates above £500m [@R8-S10]. {{V}}

{{L:B}} The shape is therefore a low rate on a very large base, charged for responsibility, with the underlying managers' fees on top. The calculator's OCIO case uses invented numbers to show it.

### Taking on the client's team

The former intern said Goldman "can bring the client's in-house investment team into the firm". {{UA}} That is true of some mandates and not of the model.

- **Where it happened.** BAE Systems' in-house investment team joined Goldman as part of the 2023 mandate [@R3-S01][@R3-S02]. {{V}} A 22-person UPS investment team joined in September 2024 [@R7-S21]. {{V}} Trade press describes these, with an earlier Canadian case, as "liftouts" and part of how the business was built, alongside acquisitions of US outsourcing firms between 2015 and 2018 [@R7-S04][@R3-S03]. {{V}}
- **Where it did not.** No transfer is reported for the smaller UK appointments, which are ordinary tendered mandates [@R3-S15][@R7-S16]. {{SI}}

{{L:B}} A team transfer happens when a scheme large enough to employ its own investment staff decides to stop doing so. It solves the sponsor's problem (those staff need somewhere to go) and gives the provider people who know the portfolio. Most schemes have no in-house team to transfer.

| Award | Date | What Goldman states |
|---|---|---|

For balance: Goldman was Fiduciary Management Firm of the Year at the Pensions Age Awards 2024 [@R3-S12], and did not win the fiduciary category at the 2026 UK Pensions Awards, which went to another firm [@R3-S14]. {{V}}

**What an award establishes.** These are trade-publication awards decided by a judging panel on written entries, weighted to the previous 12 to 18 months. They show that a panel preferred that entry in that category in that year. They are not a performance ranking, a client survey or a measure of market share. In an interview, "they won an award" is weak evidence; "they were appointed after competitive tenders run by four different independent advisers" is stronger and is also on the public record.

### The market it sits in

UK fiduciary management covered about £241bn across 832 mandates in one 2025 survey, the first fall in the number of mandates since 2008, with OCIO-type partial mandates making up 53% of assets [@R3-S25]. {{V}} About 18% of UK schemes used some form of it on an earlier survey that Goldman itself cites [@R3-S08]. {{V}} Goldman's stated reading is that outsourcing has moved from small schemes to large ones with in-house teams, as sponsors question whether running a pension scheme is core business and as maturing schemes shift to cashflow-driven strategies [@R3-S07]. {{V}} Its UK-only assets and market rank are not published. {{NPE}} Cards [D-R3-06](#D-R3-06) and [D-R8-07](#D-R8-07) follow the consequences.

## 8. Liquidity Solutions and Insurance: two different business models

The former intern listed these beside the regions. They are not regions, and they do not work like the regional teams.

### Liquidity Solutions

Profile: [Liquidity Solutions](#T-liq).

:::l1
Every large organisation has cash it will need soon: next month's payroll, a tax bill, money waiting to be invested. Leaving it all in one bank is risky and earns little. A money market fund spreads it across many very short, very safe loans and lets the organisation take it back the same day. Liquidity Solutions is the business that runs those funds and looks after the organisations that use them.

The client is usually a treasurer. Their order of priorities is fixed: do not lose the money, be able to get it today, and only then earn something on it.
:::

:::l2
**What is established.** "Our Liquidity Solutions team oversees $1.2+ trillion in money market and short-term assets" as at 30 September 2026, a global figure [@R5-S01]; firm-wide liquidity assets under supervision were $1,051bn on the same date [@R2-S02]. {{V}} Its stated clients are "financial institution, hedge fund, institutional, corporate, and wealth management clients" [@R5-S01]. {{V}} It has a global head of its client business and shares a global head with Insurance Asset Management [@R5-S01][@R5-S02]. {{V}} A London "EMEA Liquidity Solutions Sales (UK Corporates)" seat has been advertised (search summary) [@R5-S09]. {{SI}} The products on the UK page are money market funds in dollars and euros, short-duration strategies, fixed income ETFs and a dealing portal [@R5-S01]. {{V}}

**Client constraints that make it its own business.**

- *An investment policy, not a preference.* A corporate treasurer invests under a board-approved policy that lists permitted instruments, minimum credit ratings and a maximum share of any one fund. The sale is getting onto that list, and staying inside its limits.
- *The balance moves every day.* There is no funding event. Cash arrives before payroll and leaves on payday. The fee calculator's liquidity case shows why average balance, not opening balance, is what matters.
- *Structure matters as much as yield.* Chapter 1 explains the fund types. The stable-priced "low volatility" type may only round its price while the true value stays within 20 basis points of it [@W1-S06] {{V}}, so clients care how much room a fund has.
- *Regulation is live.* The UK regulator said in June 2026 that the UK money market fund regulation is expected to be repealed by the end of 2026, with new rules expecting stable-priced funds to hold 40% in assets maturing within a week [@R5-S32]. {{V}} Funds domiciled in the EU, which hold roughly 90% of sterling money market assets, are sold in the UK under a temporary regime that is being extended [@R5-S34]. {{V}} See [D-R5-02](#D-R5-02).
- *Operations sell.* Cut-off times, portal integration and reporting decide many choices between similar funds.

**How the money works.** The investor receives the portfolio's yield less the fund's charge. The manager's revenue is that charge on average balances. Firm-wide, Goldman's liquidity products earned an average 14 basis points in 2025, against 54 for equity [@R2-S01]. {{V}} So this is a scale business: very large balances, thin margin, and a fee that managers across the industry have at times waived in part when interest rates were near zero. {{SI}} No Goldman fund fee or waiver figure was read, and none should be quoted.

**The French question.** The former intern said both that an English-only candidate could work here and that everyone in the team speaks French. {{UA}} No job description supports a French requirement; the one French-speaker advert found is for the French sales team [@R5-S10]. {{NPE}}
:::

### Insurance

Profile: [Insurance](#T-ins).

:::l1
An insurance company collects premiums today and pays claims later, sometimes decades later. In between, it invests the money. Unlike a pension scheme, the insurer is a regulated company that must hold capital against every risk it takes, and reports profits to shareholders every quarter. So it does not ask "what will earn the most?" It asks "what will pay my claims, earn a steady income, and cost me the least capital?"
:::

:::l2
**What is established.** Goldman Sachs Asset Management states it manages "$651 billion in general account assets on behalf of insurance clients" as at 31 December 2025 [@R5-S02]. {{V}} ("General account" means the insurer's own balance-sheet assets backing its policies, as opposed to policyholders' unit-linked funds.) The offer runs from partial to full outsourcing across fixed income, alternatives and equities, with advice on asset-liability management, strategic asset allocation and capital-efficient strategies [@R5-S02]. {{V}} "Insurance Client Solutions Group" seats have been advertised in London and Frankfurt (search summaries) [@R5-S22][@R5-S21] {{SI}}, and a Goldman advert lists insurance beside institutional and third-party wealth as a peer channel [@R5-S42]. {{SI}} Whether EMEA insurance staff report to the region or to the global vertical is not established. {{NPE}}

**Client constraints that make it its own business.**

- *Capital rules.* Under Solvency II in the EU and its UK equivalent, each asset carries a capital charge, so two bonds with the same yield can have very different value to an insurer. Amended EU rules take effect on 30 January 2027 [@R5-S39]. {{V}}
- *Matching.* UK annuity writers can get a regulatory benefit (the matching adjustment) for holding assets whose cash flows match their liabilities. The UK regulator consulted in 2025 on letting insurers add some assets to those portfolios before formal approval, within limits [@R5-S38]. {{V}} The final policy was not read.
- *Accounting.* Realising a gain or loss changes reported profit, so an insurer may not want a bond sold even when a total-return investor would sell it. Guidelines often limit turnover and realised gains.
- *Income.* The measure is frequently the yield at which assets were bought (book yield), not performance against an index.

**What clients say they are doing.** In Goldman's fifteenth annual insurance survey, published 25 March 2026, 434 chief investment and finance officers were polled; 62% planned to increase private-asset allocations [@R5-S03]. {{V}} A provider's survey of its own client base is a view, not a census (see [D-R5-05](#D-R5-05)).

**The link to UK pensions.** {{L:B}} When a UK pension scheme buys out with an insurer, its assets move from the trustees to the insurer's balance sheet. The pension client disappears and an insurance client grows. A firm with both an institutional pensions team and an insurance team sees both sides of that transfer, which is one reason the two businesses cannot be understood separately.
:::

## 9. Six clients compared

The same firm, the same investment teams, six very different conversations.

| Client | Whose money, and what for | Who decides | What usually decides it |
|---|---|---|---|
| UK DB pension scheme | Members' promised pensions | Trustees, advised by a consultant | Consultant rating, a formal tender, fit with the endgame plan |
| Gulf sovereign investor | A state's long-term wealth | A small senior investment team and committee | Senior relationship, tailored partnership terms, private-markets access |
| Swiss private bank | Its private clients' portfolios | A fund selector, then each banker | Approval, share class and price, then adviser adoption |
| Nordic pension institution | Members' savings, run by professionals | A large in-house investment team | A specialist capability the team does not have, at an institutional fee |
| Insurer | Its own balance sheet backing policies | Chief investment and finance officers with actuaries | Capital efficiency, cash-flow matching, book yield |
| Corporate treasury | The company's operating cash | The treasurer, under a board policy | Safety, same-day access, operational ease |

| Client | Size and pace | Liquidity need | How the manager is paid | Typical way it ends |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK DB pension scheme | Large, slow, formal | Rising as the scheme matures | Fee on a mandate, or one fee for the whole portfolio in OCIO | Buyout with an insurer, re-tender, or consolidation |
| Gulf sovereign investor | Very large, very few, long courtship | Low | Bespoke: account fees and private-fund terms | Strategy change or the investor builds the capability itself |
| Swiss private bank | Many small holdings that add up | Daily dealing | Fund management fee on whatever advisers allocate | Removal from the approved list |
| Nordic pension institution | Large, direct, price-aware | Moderate | Institutional mandate fee, hard negotiated | Taken in-house or re-tendered |
| Insurer | Large, sticky, rule-bound | Matched to claims | Low fee on big fixed-income mandates | Regulatory or strategy change; merger |
| Corporate treasury | Balances that swing daily | Same day | Thin fund charge on the average balance | Cash is needed, or a policy limit is hit |

:::warn
These tables are interpretation. {{L:B}} {{SI}} They compress general industry practice into one line per client and are not statements about Goldman's clients. The Gulf, Swiss and Nordic rows in particular rest on general knowledge and thin sourcing; no primary source on how those client types select managers was read. Use them to organise your thinking, then test each cell against a real case before relying on it.
:::

**How to read the comparison.** {{L:B}}

- **The client is not always the owner of the money.** A pension trustee, a private bank and a treasurer are all acting for someone else, under rules. Much of what looks like caution or slowness is that duty at work. The sovereign investor and the insurer are closer to principals, which is partly why their terms are more bespoke.
- **"Winning" means different things.** For a pension scheme it is an appointment after a tender. For a private bank it is approval, which may be followed by nothing. For a treasurer it is a place on a permitted list, after which the balance does whatever the company's cash flow does. Quoting a "win" without saying which kind is a common way to overstate.
- **What ends the relationship is often nothing to do with service.** A scheme buys out. A treasurer needs the cash. A bank is acquired and its buy list is merged. Good coverage sees these coming and helps the client through them even when the assets leave.
- **The specialist dependency changes.** UK pensions coverage leans on liability and credit specialists; Gulf coverage on the private-markets business; wealth on product specialists who can talk to advisers; insurance on people who understand capital rules; liquidity on portfolio managers and operations. The coverage professional's skill is knowing which specialist the client needs and getting them into the room, not being all of them.
