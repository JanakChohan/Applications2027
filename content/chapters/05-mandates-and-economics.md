# Mandates, products and commercial economics

This chapter does three things. It shows what is publicly known about the business Goldman Sachs Asset Management (GSAM) has won and lost, and how to read an announcement without over-reading it. It explains how assets turn into revenue, using Goldman Sachs's own filings for the vocabulary and the numbers. Then it works through five illustrative cases end to end, so that you can connect a junior task to a client outcome and to a line of revenue.

Two warnings apply throughout. First, every fee rate in a worked example is an invented round number {{IL}}. Goldman Sachs does not publish mandate-level fees, and nothing here is one. Second, the public record is a sample chosen by whoever issued the press release. It over-represents large wins and under-represents quiet losses, small mandates and anything a client preferred to keep private.

## 1. Reading public commercial evidence

### What the register contains

The mandates register (the Mandates view in the application, Appendix C in the PDF) holds 32 publicly documented events. They are not all "wins", and they are not all the same kind of thing.

| Kind of event | Entries | Example |
|---|---|---|
| OCIO or fiduciary appointment | 10 | [BAE Systems, 2023](#M-R7-01) |
| Loss or termination | 6 | [Pensioenfonds Vervoer, 2010](#M-R7-26) |
| Acquisition or partnership | 5 | [NN Group, 2021 to 2022](#M-R7-11) |
| Product launch | 4 | [Active equity UCITS ETFs, 2025](#M-R7-18) |
| Awarded mandate (single strategy) | 3 | [Border to Coast, 2023](#M-R7-21) |
| Commitment, target, distribution deal, regulatory action | 4 | [Qatar Investment Authority, 2026](#M-R7-14) |

Twenty-six entries are verified from a page that was read. Six are supported inference, because the primary page could not be opened and the entry rests on a search-result passage or a secondary report. Each entry says which.

:::l1
**An OCIO appointment** means a client hands the running of its whole portfolio to an outside firm. OCIO stands for outsourced chief investment officer. In the United Kingdom the same service is usually called fiduciary management. A **mandate** is an instruction to manage a specific pot of money under agreed rules. Chapter 1 explains both.
:::

:::warn Selection bias in the register
The register is what was announced, reported and still findable on 14 November 2026. Trade press reports terminations far less often than appointments, and clients rarely announce that they are leaving. A register with ten appointments and six losses does not mean GSAM wins five mandates for every three it loses. No win rate, retention rate or market share can be calculated from it.
:::

### Five things that sound alike and are not

Headlines use the words "wins", "raises", "commits" and "launches" loosely. The commercial meaning differs a great deal, because only one of the five stages below is certain to produce a fee.

| Stage | What it is | Does it pay a fee yet? | Register example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fund launch | A product becomes available to buy | No. A new fund can hold nothing | [M-R7-18](#M-R7-18) |
| Fundraising target | An intention to raise or invest up to an amount | No | [M-R7-14](#M-R7-14) |
| Commitment | A legal promise to provide capital when called | Sometimes, depending on the fee base | [M-R7-12](#M-R7-12) |
| Awarded mandate | The client has chosen the manager | Not until contracts are signed and assets arrive | [M-R7-01](#M-R7-01) |
| Funded, fee-paying assets | Money is invested and being charged | Yes | Reported in aggregate only |

**Fund launch.** On 28 April 2025 GSAM announced its entry into active equity exchange-traded funds (ETFs) in Europe, starting with a US equity fund and naming four more to follow. {{V}} [@R7-S31] The release discloses no assets, because at launch there are none to disclose. A launch is an item for the sales team to take to clients. It is not evidence that any client bought.

**Fundraising target.** On 20 January 2026 the Qatar Investment Authority and Goldman Sachs signed a memorandum of understanding under which the authority would *target* $25 billion of combined commitments to GSAM-managed funds and co-investments. {{V}} [@R7-S28] One trade headline turned this into "Qatar Commits $25B". [@R7-S30] The joint wording is "target", subject to "terms, conditions and deliverables", so the register records it as a target. {{CE}} The 2025 memorandum with Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund is one step further back: it is non-binding and names no amount at all. {{V}} [@R7-S26]

**Commitment.** In February 2024 Mubadala and Goldman Sachs signed a $1 billion private credit partnership, structured as a separately managed account to be invested over time across Asia Pacific. {{V}} [@R7-S25] The $1 billion is capital to be deployed, not capital already invested. Goldman's own annual report shows why that matters: of $438 billion of third-party alternatives commitments raised from 2020 to 2025, $105 billion sat in "non-fee-earning alternative assets", and about $81 billion of that "will begin to earn fees (and become AUS) if and when the commitments are drawn and assets are invested". {{V}} [@R2-S01]

**Awarded mandate.** The BAE Systems announcement of 6 September 2023 said the trustees had appointed GSAM for approximately £23 billion of defined benefit assets, with the transition "expected" towards the end of that year. {{V}} [@R7-S01] Between award and funding sit legal negotiation, operational set-up and the physical transfer of assets. The scheme's later documents confirm the mandate did fund and remains in force. {{V}} [@R7-S06]

**Funded, fee-paying assets.** Goldman reports these only in aggregate, as assets under supervision. No public document gives fee-paying assets for a single client. The nearest thing is the July 2026 Verizon and Lockheed Martin release, which separates $30 billion of OCIO "assets under management" from $40 billion of "assets under advisement". {{V}} [@R7-S13] Managed and advised assets normally earn different fees, so "over $70 billion" is not one fee base.

:::example Checking a headline number
The March 2026 release on GSAM's evergreen European private credit range quotes "over $8 billion". The footnote defines this as the fair value of investments plus unfunded commitments, *including leverage* and excluding cash. [@R7-S33] It is not investors' net assets and it is not the size of the newly launched fund. The habit to build: before repeating any figure, find the sentence that says what it measures and on what date. {{V}}
:::

### Eight cases worth knowing properly

**1. BAE Systems, 2023: the largest UK appointment and a team transfer.** Client: the trustees of the BAE Systems Pension Scheme and the Executive Pension Scheme. Amount: about £23 billion, which is the schemes' defined benefit assets at announcement, not a fee figure. Strategy: OCIO, including liability-driven and cashflow-driven portfolios. The in-house investment team moved to GSAM and continued to serve the schemes. Stated rationale: the sponsor said "reducing the cost and improving the efficiency of asset management are essential"; the award followed "a highly competitive tender process". Advisers: Redington advised the trustees and PwC advised the sponsor. Decision-makers: the trustee boards. {{V}} [@R7-S01] [@R7-S02] What is public about the Goldman side: the release quotes the head of International OCIO and UK Fiduciary Management and two firm-wide leaders. Nothing says who originated or ran the pitch. The scheme still retains a separate investment adviser, so outsourcing the portfolio did not remove independent oversight. {{V}} [@R7-S06] This is the one client the former intern named that public sources confirm. See [M-R7-01](#M-R7-01).

**2. Shell, 2025: one sponsor, many decisions.** In September 2025 various Shell-related pension funds and a captive insurer appointed GSAM for $40 billion of OCIO and advisory work, of which about $15.6 billion was UK and $2.8 billion German. {{V}} [@R7-S08] Isio ran the tender. Shell described itself as co-ordinator and said decisions were made by the individual funds. [@R7-S11] The Dutch Shell fund is not part of it, having appointed a different provider. {{V}} [@R7-S09] The lesson is that "the client" was a set of separate trustee boards with separate duties. One relationship with the sponsor would not have been enough. See [M-R7-02](#M-R7-02).

**3. Calor, 2024: winning from an incumbent.** The £240 million Calor Group Retirement Benefits Plan appointed GSAM as fiduciary manager after a tender overseen by Barnett Waddingham. The plan had been under fiduciary management with another firm since 2019. {{V}} [@R7-S16] [@R7-S18] Fiduciary mandates are re-tendered, and UK rules require trustees to run a competitive tender with at least three bidders before delegating 20% or more of scheme assets. {{V}} [@W2-S04] The other side of this fact is that GSAM's own mandates face the same test. See [M-R7-04](#M-R7-04).

**4. Pensioenfonds Vervoer, 2006 to 2010: won and then lost on performance.** GSAM won fiduciary management of all €5.4 billion of this Dutch industry fund in 2006, one of the first such appointments in the Netherlands. {{V}} [@R7-S48] Four years later the fund terminated the mandate, by then €7 billion, citing underperformance. Its chief investment officer said the manager "did not produce any outperformance". {{V}} [@R7-S49] A whole-portfolio relationship is not safer than a single-strategy one. It concentrates responsibility, so when results disappoint there is no one else to point to. See [M-R7-25](#M-R7-25) and [M-R7-26](#M-R7-26).

**5. Local government pensions: mandates that end because the client's structure changes.** Kent County Council ended a £350 million equity mandate in 2006 in a strategic reallocation. {{V}} [@R7-S46] In 2023 GSAM was reported as one of two managers for the emerging markets sleeve of a fund run by Border to Coast, a pool that invests for several local authority funds, and a 2025 article confirms it was managing that allocation. {{SI}} [@R7-S43] [@R7-S44] In 2026 a trade report noted that Goldman Sachs no longer appeared on the factsheet of the pool's global equity fund. {{SI}} [@R7-S45] No reason was published, and "terminated" is an inference from the factsheet. The pattern is that the buyer has moved from the individual council fund to the pool, so one decision now moves more money in either direction. See [M-R7-21](#M-R7-21) and [M-R7-22](#M-R7-22).

**6. Gulf sovereign investors: partnerships, not fund subscriptions.** The Mubadala account, the Public Investment Fund memorandum and the Qatar target are all structured as bespoke partnerships with anchor roles, co-investment and local office commitments. {{V}} [@R7-S25] [@R7-S26] [@R7-S28] The amounts are intentions or capital to be deployed. They show the shape of the relationship more reliably than they show revenue.

**7. The wealth channel: many small tickets.** GSAM's first European private markets fund for wealthy individuals raised more than $200 million at its close in 2023. {{V}} [@R7-S34] Beside a £23 billion pension appointment that looks small, and it illustrates the economics of the channel: money arrives through intermediaries in many small amounts, at higher fee rates, over a long time. The 2013 agreement under which True Potential distributed Goldman Sachs multi-asset funds through more than 800 adviser firms shows the chain: the platform decided what advisers could access, and the advisers' clients were the investors. {{V}} [@R7-S53] See [M-R7-19](#M-R7-19) and [M-R7-30](#M-R7-30).

**8. A regulatory action: why due diligence asks about process.** In November 2022 the US Securities and Exchange Commission charged Goldman Sachs Asset Management, L.P. with failing to follow its own policies for two funds and one separately managed account strategy marketed as environmental, social and governance investments, covering April 2017 to February 2020. The firm paid a $4 million penalty without admitting or denying the findings. {{V}} [@R7-S54] A client's due diligence questionnaire asks whether written procedures exist and are followed. This case is why, and anyone answering such a question for a client needs to know that it is on the record. See [M-R7-31](#M-R7-31).

### Why no announcement can be attributed to a London team or person

:::warn Attribution
No source in the register says which sales or relationship team, or which individual, originated or won a mandate. The people quoted in releases are business heads speaking for the firm. An OCIO appointment of the BAE or Shell kind involves a tender run by an adviser, a solutions and portfolio management team that designs the proposal, legal, operations, risk and transition specialists, and senior sponsorship. Saying "the UK Institutional team won BAE" would be a reconstruction, not a fact, and it should not be said in an interview. What can be said is that the release named the head of the OCIO business and, for Shell, the co-head of EMEA Institutional Client Coverage. [@R7-S01] [@R7-S08]
:::

## 2. How revenue actually works

### Assets under management and assets under supervision

:::l1
**Assets under management (AUM)** is the usual industry term for the money a manager invests on behalf of clients. Goldman Sachs reports a wider measure, **assets under supervision (AUS)**. AUS includes money Goldman manages with discretion, and also money where it earns a fee for advice or other services without making the investment decisions. When you quote a Goldman Sachs figure, use "AUS", because that is the number the firm publishes.
:::

:::l2
Goldman's definition: AUS "includes our institutional clients' assets, assets sourced through third-party distributors and high-net-worth clients' assets where we earn a fee for managing assets on a discretionary basis". It "also includes client assets invested with third-party managers, private bank deposits and advisory relationships where we earn a fee for advisory and other services, but do not have investment discretion". It excludes clients' self-directed brokerage assets. {{V}} [@R2-S01] Two consequences follow. The liquidity line includes private bank deposits as well as money market funds, so it is not a clean figure for the Liquidity Solutions fund business. And AUS is not all charged at a management-fee rate, because advisory assets are in it.
:::

Total AUS was $3,606 billion at 31 December 2025 and $4,097 billion at 30 September 2026. {{V}} [@R2-S01] [@R2-S02]

:::warn Two published figures for the same date
The GSAM UK institutional website shows "$3.7+ Trillion" of AUS with a footnote dated 30 September 2026. The quarterly filing for the same date gives $4,097 billion. The website does not explain the difference, which may be a narrower perimeter. {{CE}} [@R2-S07] [@R2-S02] Quote the filing, with its date.
:::

### Goldman's segments and what "about 30%" describes

Goldman Sachs reports three segments: Global Banking & Markets, Asset & Wealth Management (AWM) and Platform Solutions. {{V}} [@R2-S01] Asset Management is not a segment. It is one of two businesses inside AWM.

| Whole firm | 58,283 | 100% | 18,679 |
| AWM segment | 16,679 | 28.6% | 5,051 |
| of which Asset management | 6,784 | 11.6% | 2,194 |

Figures are from the 2025 annual report and the Q3 2026 quarterly report; the percentages are arithmetic on them. {{V}} [@R2-S01] [@R2-S02] [@R2-S03]

The former intern said asset management is about 30% of Goldman's revenue. That figure fits the AWM segment in 2025 (28.6%). It does not fit Asset Management alone (11.6%). And it says nothing about the Client Solutions Group, for which Goldman publishes no revenue, assets, flows, headcount or fee rate. {{NPE}} [@R2-S01] The smallest units disclosed are the global client channel and the EMEA region.

The AWM segment's 2025 revenue came from four lines. {{V}} [@R2-S01]

|---|---|---|---|
| Management and other fees | 4,990 | 6,548 | 11,538 |
| Incentive fees | 489 | none reported | 489 |
| Private banking and lending | none reported | 3,347 | 3,347 |
| Investments | 1,305 | none reported | 1,305 |

Two of those lines are not fees for managing client money. Private banking and lending is interest income. "Investments" is the return on Goldman's own capital. Management and incentive fees together were $12,027 million, so treating all $16.7 billion as fee income would overstate it by almost 40%.

Management and other fees were 69% of AWM net revenues in 2025. That is the basis for describing the segment as a source of steadier income. Goldman's own wording is more careful: the fees "are affected by investment performance, as well as asset inflows and redemptions". {{V}} [@R2-S01]

:::l2
**Reading the flow numbers.** *Gross inflows* are all the money that came in. *Gross outflows* are all the money that left. *Net flows* are the difference. Goldman reports net flows only, so a net inflow of $65 billion in the institutional channel can hide large wins and large losses in the same period. Three further points from the filings. {{V}} [@R2-S02]

- Acquired assets count as inflows. The nine-month 2026 figure includes $31 billion from the purchase of Innovator, an ETF provider, and $5 billion from Industry Ventures, less $15 billion from the sale of a Polish fund company. The third-party distributed channel's $97 billion is therefore not all organic selling.
- Markets move the total without any client acting. In Q3 2026 net market depreciation took $27 billion off AUS, mostly in fixed income.
- Liquidity products can move the other way from long-term assets. Q3 2026 saw $97 billion of long-term net inflows and a $14 billion net outflow from liquidity.
:::

### Effective fee rates are blends, not prices

Goldman publishes an "average effective management fee" by asset class, in basis points. One basis point (bp) is one hundredth of one per cent, so 20bp is 0.20%.

| Basis points | 2024 | 2025 | 9 months 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alternative investments | 60 | 58 | 60 |
| Equity | 55 | 54 | 52 |
| Fixed income | 17 | 17 | 16 |
| Liquidity products | 15 | 14 | 13 |
| Total | 31 | 31 | 30 |

{{V}} [@R2-S01] [@R2-S02]

:::warn These are not what any client pays
Each figure averages every client, region, vehicle and size, worldwide. A large UK pension mandate, a retail share class and an advisory account are all inside the same number. Do not present 17bp as "what Goldman charges for fixed income". The figures teach two things only. The first is the mix effect: a pound in equity earns about three times as much as a pound in fixed income. The second is direction. Goldman's own risk factors say that if clients keep moving to lower-fee products, "our average effective management fee will decline further". [@R2-S01]
:::

A cross-check shows how the pieces fit. Thirty-one basis points on average AUS of $3,328 billion is about $10.3 billion, a little below the reported $11.5 billion of management and other fees, because that line also includes fees that are not charged on assets. {{SI}} [@R2-S01]

### What is and is not disclosed about OCIO

Three public figures exist, and they measure different things.

- In its filings Goldman reports OCIO only inside alternatives, combined with advisory accounts: an average of $112 billion in 2025, earning 17bp, against 77bp on funds and discretionary alternative accounts. {{V}} [@R2-S01]
- A July 2026 press release gives $480 billion of OCIO AUS globally at 31 March 2026. {{V}} [@R3-S09]
- GSAM's OCIO web page gives "$540+ billion" at 30 June 2026. {{V}} [@R8-S22]

No UK or EMEA figure is published, and no OCIO fee rate. {{NPE}} The 17bp in the filing applies to the alternatives slice of advisory and OCIO accounts. It is not "the OCIO fee". A public annual report for a UK scheme run by a different provider shows a typical structure: a fiduciary fee based on the value of the return-seeking and cashflow-driven portfolios, with the hedging manager invoicing the scheme separately and the fiduciary manager negotiating the underlying managers' fees. {{V}} [@R3-S17] Industry surveys report that UK fiduciary fees fell about 27% over five years, driven by competition for mandates above £500 million. {{V}} [@R8-S10]

### From assets to revenue, step by step

:::l1
A manager's fee income for a period is, at its simplest:

**average fee-paying assets × fee rate × fraction of the year**

Everything else is detail about what counts as fee-paying, which rate applies, and what gets subtracted afterwards. The base case used in this chapter is £500 million × 0.20% = £1 million a year. {{IL}}
:::

:::l2
**The asset bridge.** Closing assets = opening assets + net flows + market movement (+ acquisitions − disposals). Fees are earned on the *average* through the period, so the date money arrives matters as much as the amount.

**Fee base.** Goldman's accounting policy states the base by vehicle. Mutual fund fees are a percentage of daily net asset value (NAV). Separately managed account fees are a percentage of daily or monthly NAV, generally received quarterly. Private equity fund fees are a percentage of "monthly invested capital or committed capital". {{V}} [@R2-S01] So a private markets fee base has to be read from the fund terms. Assuming NAV is a common mistake.

**Breakpoints.** Large mandates usually have tiered schedules: one rate on the first slice of assets, a lower rate on the next. The marginal rate falls as the client grows, so doubling assets does not double fees.

**Management fees, incentive fees and carried interest.** A management fee is charged on assets whatever the result. An incentive (performance) fee is a share of return, or of return above a benchmark or target. Goldman recognises incentive fees only when a significant reversal is not probable, and life-of-fund fees can be clawed back. {{V}} [@R2-S01] In private funds the manager's share of profits is commonly called carried interest; Goldman calls its enhanced share on private equity and credit funds "overrides". Incentive fees were $489 million in 2025 against $11,538 million of management and other fees, so they are a small and lumpy part of the total.

**Distribution costs.** When a fund is sold through an intermediary, the manager may pay for placement. Goldman describes distribution fees paid "to brokers and advisors related to the placement of the firm's investment funds", calculated on the management fee, NAV or committed capital, and booked as an expense. {{V}} [@R2-S01] No amount is broken out. Gross management fee is therefore not net revenue.

**Operating profitability.** The AWM segment's pre-tax margin was 25% in 2025, with a stated target of about 30% in the medium term. {{V}} [@R2-S01] [@R2-S02] Across the industry, one consultancy's 2026 report puts margins near 30% and notes that from 2010 to 2025 costs grew slightly faster than revenues. {{V}} [@R8-S38] {{L:B}} Read together, these suggest that roughly 70 to 75 pence of each pound of fee goes on people, systems and distribution before any profit.
:::

### Three cautions about counting revenue

**Retained revenue is not incremental revenue.** Keeping a £1 billion client adds nothing new to the firm's income. It prevents a loss. Both matter, and they should not be added together as "revenue generated".

**No function can claim sole credit.** A mandate exists because portfolio managers produced a strategy worth buying, a specialist explained it, a coverage person understood the client and ran the process, operations made it work and the firm's name passed due diligence. Goldman publishes no revenue attribution by team. {{NPE}} Any statement of the form "sales generated X" is an internal accounting convention, not a fact about causation.

**Recurring is not guaranteed.** A management fee recurs for as long as the client stays, markets hold and the price is not renegotiated. The Vervoer termination, the Kent reallocation and the fee trend in UK fiduciary management are each an example of one of those three conditions failing.

## 3. Worked examples, end to end

The five cases below use fictional clients. The tables are calculated by the same model as the fee calculator in the application, and the method is set out in Appendix J: flows land at the start of a month, the month's fee is one twelfth of the annual fee on that month's balance, and market movement is applied at month end. Who leads each stage is described as general industry practice {{SI}}, not as Goldman's internal process. At every stage the junior person prepares, checks and records. Decisions on price, suitability of the proposal and investment content belong to senior coverage staff, portfolio managers and the client.

### Case A: a £500 million fixed-income mandate

**Client problem.** The fictional Harrow Vale Pension Scheme is a closed defined benefit scheme. It pays pensions every month and wants a bond portfolio whose income and maturities line up with those payments, to rely less on selling assets at uncertain prices.

**Discovery.** The scheme's investment consultant issues a request for proposal (RFP) to five managers. Before any proposal is written, the coverage lead needs to know the cashflow profile, the funding level, what the trustees already hold, who sits on the investment committee and what worried them about their previous manager. Much of this comes from the consultant and from the scheme's public documents.

**Internal collaboration and proposal.** The fixed-income portfolio managers design the portfolio and own every investment statement. A product specialist turns it into a proposal. Risk and legal review the guidelines. The coverage lead decides how to present it and what price to propose, within limits set by the business.

**Due diligence and negotiation.** The consultant sends a due diligence questionnaire covering people, process, risk controls, operations and regulatory history. The trustees then interview a shortlist. If selected, the parties negotiate the investment management agreement: guidelines, reporting, fees, termination notice.

**Onboarding and funding.** Accounts are opened with the scheme's custodian, identity checks are completed and the assets are transferred, often in stages managed by a transition specialist.

**Ongoing coverage.** Quarterly reports, review meetings, answers to trustee questions and early warning when anything changes.

**What the junior person produces.** A briefing on the scheme from its report and accounts. First drafts of standard RFP answers from the content library, each checked against the current source. A tracker of outstanding due diligence items. Meeting notes and a follow-up list. During onboarding, a checklist of documents chased to completion.

<!-- DATA:case:£500m fixed-income mandate -->

**Reading the four results.** Fully funded for a year, £500 million at 20bp earns £1 million. {{IL}} If half arrives in month 1 and half in month 7, the average balance is £375 million and the first-year fee is £750,000: the same headline mandate, a quarter less revenue. If markets fall 8% across the year the fee is about £962,800, although no one did anything wrong. If the fee is renegotiated to 16bp from month 7 the year earns £900,000 and every later year earns £800,000.

**Failure points.** The proposal answers the RFP but not the trustees' real concern. A due diligence answer is out of date. The consultant rates a competitor more highly. Legal terms take months. Funding slips into the next financial year. Each of these is ordinary, and several are affected by how carefully the junior work was done.

### Case B: a £1 billion client considering withdrawal

**Client problem.** The fictional Northmoor Staff Pension Fund has £1 billion in a multi-asset mandate at 15bp. {{IL}} Its funding level has improved and the trustees are considering buying annuities from an insurer for part of their liabilities, which would mean taking money out.

**Discovery.** The relationship manager's first task is to find out what is driving the review. Is it the endgame plan, performance, cost, a new consultant or a change of trustee chair? The answers lead to different honest responses.

**Internal collaboration.** Portfolio managers explain performance and what the portfolio could look like if the scheme's needs change. A solutions specialist can model a lower-risk version. Senior management decides whether any fee change is on offer.

**Proposal.** There are three possible positions. One is to show the trustees information that genuinely changes the decision, if any exists. A second is to redesign the mandate to fit the new objective, which may mean a smaller or cheaper mandate. The third is to accept that withdrawal is right for the members and to make the exit orderly. Chapter 7 discusses why the third is sometimes the correct professional answer.

**Due diligence, negotiation, onboarding and funding in reverse.** A withdrawal has its own process: notice under the agreement, a transition plan, sale or transfer of assets, final reporting and fee reconciliation. Done well, it is the last impression the client keeps.

**What the junior person produces.** A timeline of the relationship and every commitment made. An accurate performance and fee history. A note of each trustee meeting. If the client leaves, a transition checklist.

<!-- DATA:case:£1bn client considering withdrawal -->

**Reading the results.** If the client stays, two years earn £3 million. {{IL}} If it withdraws £400 million in month 7, the monthly fee falls from £125,000 to £75,000 and two years earn £2.1 million. If it leaves entirely in month 7, the total is £750,000 and the second year earns nothing. The £2.25 million difference between staying and leaving is revenue that was at risk, not revenue that was created.

**Failure points.** Learning about the review from the consultant instead of from the client. Defending performance when the real issue is the endgame. Offering a fee cut that does not address the reason for leaving. Arguing against a decision that is plainly in the members' interest, which costs credibility with the trustees and with their adviser.

### Case C: a relationship growing from £300 million to £600 million

**Client problem.** The fictional Calder Foundation, an endowment, holds £300 million in a global credit mandate. After a strategy review its investment committee wants to move another £300 million out of cash and passive bonds.

**Discovery.** The question is what the additional money is for. If it has the same objective as the existing mandate, the mandate can simply grow. If it has a different purpose, it may need a different strategy, or a different manager.

**Internal collaboration and proposal.** The portfolio managers confirm the strategy has capacity. The coverage lead prepares a proposal for the enlarged mandate, including the fee schedule that would apply. Concentration matters to the client too: a committee may not want half its assets with one firm, and saying so first is better than waiting to be asked.

**Negotiation.** Larger size is the client's strongest argument on price. A tiered schedule is the usual answer.

**Onboarding and funding.** An amendment to the existing agreement, then the transfer.

**What the junior person produces.** A comparison of the client's current and proposed allocation. A fee illustration under each schedule, checked line by line. An updated account plan.

<!-- DATA:case:Relationship expanding from £300m to £600m -->

**Reading the results.** At a flat 22bp, £300 million earns £660,000 a year and £600 million earns £1.32 million: double the assets, double the fee. {{IL}} Under the tiered schedule (25bp on the first £250 million, 20bp on the next £250 million, 15bp above) £300 million earns £725,000 a year, an effective 24.2bp, and £600 million earns £1.275 million, an effective 21.25bp. Assets doubled and the fee rose by 76%. Over the two years shown, the flat schedule totals £2.31 million and the tiered one £2.275 million.

**Failure points.** Treating the expansion as won before the committee has voted. Proposing more of the same when the new money has a different job. Fee illustrations that do not reconcile to the agreement.

### Case D: a wealth intermediary adds a fund to its approved list

**Client problem.** The fictional Fenwick Vale Wealth is a discretionary wealth manager with several hundred advisers. Its fund research team wants an additional global bond fund for its model portfolios.

**Discovery.** The first audience is the fund selector, sometimes called the gatekeeper. The sales person needs to know the selection criteria, which funds are already on the list, how the investment committee works, which platforms the firm uses and which share classes those platforms carry.

**Internal collaboration and proposal.** A product specialist and, at the right moment, the portfolio manager present the fund. The fund has to be available on the intermediary's platforms at an acceptable share class, which is an operational task, not a sales one.

**Due diligence.** The research team completes its own review and takes the fund to committee.

**Appointment.** The fund is added to the approved list. This is permission for advisers and portfolio managers to buy it. It is not a purchase.

**Funding.** Money arrives adviser by adviser and portfolio by portfolio. That needs a second campaign: explaining the fund to the people who actually allocate.

**Ongoing coverage.** Regular updates to the research team, because a fund can be removed from the list as easily as it was added.

**What the junior person produces.** A profile of the intermediary and its existing list. A pack of fund materials checked for the right jurisdiction and share class. A log of which advisers have been briefed. A monthly flow report by intermediary.

<!-- DATA:case:Wealth intermediary adds a fund to its approved list -->

**Reading the results.** In the adoption case, flows begin in month 5 at £4 million a month and the fund charges 60bp. {{IL}} Year one earns £72,000 on an average balance of £12 million. Year two earns £348,000 and year three £636,000, by which time £128 million is invested. In the second case the fund is approved and nobody uses it: three years, no assets, no fee. On the day of approval the two cases look identical.

**Failure points.** The fund is approved but missing from the platform the advisers use. The research team is convinced and the advisers never hear about it. Performance dips in the first year and the fund is put "on watch". The gatekeeper who championed it moves firm.

### Case E: three different business models

The last group puts an OCIO appointment, a liquidity balance and a private markets commitment side by side, because each earns money on a different basis.

**OCIO.** The fictional Wexcombe Group Pension Scheme has £2 billion of assets and a small in-house team. The trustees appoint a provider to run the whole portfolio within a strategy they set. Discovery here is long and involves the trustees, the sponsor and an independent adviser running the tender. The proposal covers strategy advice, manager selection, hedging, reporting and governance. Due diligence examines conflicts of interest closely, since the provider may place money in its own funds. The fee is charged on total assets at a low rate, and underlying managers' fees are normally separate. In the illustration, 10bp on the first £1 billion and 6bp above gives £1.6 million a year, an effective 8bp. {{IL}} Staff transfers, as at BAE Systems, appear only in the largest public cases and are not a standard feature. [@R7-S01] [@R7-S04]

**Liquidity.** The fictional Dunmore Industries keeps £750 million of operating cash in a money market fund at 10bp. {{IL}} In month 4 it redeems £500 million to pay for an acquisition. The decision has nothing to do with service or performance: the cash had a job to do. The year earns £375,000, half of what the opening balance implied. Coverage in this business is about understanding the treasurer's cash cycle, daily dealing and operational reliability. Balances can leave in a day.

**Private markets.** The fictional Aldbury Family Office commits £100 million to a closed-end fund. If the fee is 100bp on *committed* capital, year one earns £1 million whatever has been drawn. If the fee is 100bp on *invested* capital, with 30% drawn at the start and the rest in month 7, year one earns £650,000. {{IL}} The commitment is identical. The fee base is a term of the fund, and Goldman's own policy lists both bases as in use. [@R2-S01] Using NAV by default would be wrong for either structure, because NAV also moves with valuations.

<!-- DATA:case:Different business models -->

**What the junior person produces across these.** For OCIO, large sections of a tender response and, after appointment, trustee reporting packs. For liquidity, daily balance and flow reports and operational queries resolved the same day. For private markets, subscription document tracking, capital call notices checked against commitments, and a schedule showing which commitments have started to pay fees.

**Failure points.** OCIO: a conflict that was not disclosed clearly, or a transition that goes badly in the first quarter. Liquidity: a dealing error or a missed cut-off. Private markets: telling a client or a colleague that a commitment is "revenue" before it is drawn, under a fee base that charges on invested capital.

## 4. What this chapter lets you say, and not say

- You can explain how £500 million at 20bp becomes £1 million, and what timing, markets, withdrawals, breakpoints and renegotiation each do to that number.
- You can distinguish a launch, a target, a commitment, an award and funded assets, with a real example of each.
- You can describe Goldman's disclosures accurately: three segments, AWM at 28.6% of 2025 net revenues, Asset Management at 11.6%, $4,097 billion of AUS at 30 September 2026, and no figures at all for the Client Solutions Group.
- You should not quote an effective fee rate as a price, attribute any mandate to a team or person, or describe retained fees as new revenue.
