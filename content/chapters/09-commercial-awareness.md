# Commercial awareness, competitors and the future of the work

This chapter turns current events into client questions. It covers what has changed for each team's clients up to 14 November 2026, how Goldman Sachs Asset Management (GSAM) compares with rivals on specific client problems, and how the job itself is changing.

Three rules apply throughout.

- Every development is walked through the same chain: what happened, how it affects the client, what decision changes, what it means for money already invested, a possible GSAM response, what sales would do, what relationship management would do, and the counterarguments.
- "Possible GSAM response" is always {{L:B}} interpretation. It is what a reasonable outsider would expect a manager to do. It is not a statement of what Goldman Sachs is doing or selling.
- A forecast is labelled as a forecast. No probabilities are given, because none could be defended.

Each development has a card in the commercial-awareness view with the full chain, the sources and the dates. The links in this chapter open those cards.

## 1. How to read a development

:::l1
Commercial awareness is not knowing the news. It is being able to say what a piece of news changes for one particular client.

A headline such as "interest rates rose" means nothing to a client until it is translated. A pension scheme hears "our promised pensions just became cheaper to fund". A company treasurer hears "our cash earns more". A wealth manager hears "clients will ask why they should hold anything riskier than cash". The same event creates three different conversations.

The chain used in this chapter forces that translation:

1. **What happened**, with the date it happened and the date it was published.
2. **How it affects the client.** Which client, and through what mechanism.
3. **What decision changes.** If no decision changes, it is background, not an opportunity.
4. **What it means for existing investments.** The client already owns things. Those come first.
5. **A possible response from the manager.**
6. **What the sales person is responsible for.**
7. **What the relationship manager is responsible for.**
8. **Risks and counterarguments.** Why the obvious conclusion might be wrong.
:::

:::l2
Two tests keep the chain honest.

**The "so what, for whom" test.** If step 2 cannot name a type of client and a mechanism (funding level, capital charge, charge cap, liquidity rule, governance requirement), the analysis is still at headline level.

**The "who benefits from my conclusion" test.** A manager's view of a market change usually ends with the client needing something the manager sells. That does not make the view wrong, but it is a reason to state the counterargument before the client does. Several sources used here are produced by firms with something to sell: GSAM's own insurance survey, a competing manager's commentary on Dutch hedging, surveys by consultants that also run fiduciary businesses. They are cited for facts and flagged where they are opinion.
:::

:::warn Not every development is a reason to sell something
Some developments call for explaining to existing clients what happened to their own portfolio. Some call for doing nothing. One card in the set, the departure of the head of GSAM's EMEA Third Party Wealth business in spring 2026 ([card](#D-R4-11)), is a fact about the team and not a client topic at all. Each team section below says which developments fall into which group.
:::

## 3. UK Institutional: pension schemes, local government funds and other institutions

This section covers the clients the former intern described as the UK Institutional team's main work: pension funds first, then endowments, foundations and official institutions. {{UA}} Public sources confirm that GSAM uses the title "EMEA Institutional Client Coverage"; a separate UK-only institutional head was not established.

### 3.1 Defined benefit schemes: surplus and the endgame choice

:::l1
A defined benefit (DB) scheme promises a pension based on salary and service. For most of the last twenty years the typical scheme did not have enough assets to cover its promises, and the trustees' main job was closing that gap.

That has changed. Most schemes now have more assets than they need on the standard regulatory measure. The question for trustees is no longer "how do we repair the deficit?" It is "how does this end?" There are two main answers. The scheme can pay an insurer to take over the promises, called a buy-in followed by a buyout. Or it can keep running, called run-on, and decide what to do with any surplus.

For an asset manager these two answers have opposite effects. A buyout ends the mandate: the assets go to the insurer. Run-on keeps the scheme invested for decades.
:::

{{L:A}} The Pension Protection Fund's 7800 index showed an aggregate surplus of £275.4bn and a funding ratio of 133.7% at the end of October 2026, across 4,838 schemes. {{V}} [@R3-S29][@R3-S30] In 2025 a record 370 buy-ins completed, but total premium fell to about £38bn from £47.8bn in 2024, with growth almost entirely in deals under £100m. {{V}} [@R3-S27][@R3-S28] The Pension Schemes Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026. It makes it easier for trustees of private-sector DB schemes to release surplus to the sponsoring employer, with regulations expected in force in 2027. {{V}} [@R3-S20]

Cards: [DB surplus and the buy-in market](#D-R3-02), [surplus release and superfunds](#D-R3-03).

| Step | Surplus, buy-ins and the new Act |
|---|---|
| What happened | Aggregate surplus of £275.4bn at end October 2026 (published November 2026). Record number of buy-ins in 2025, lower total value. Act passed 29 April 2026; surplus rules expected 2027. |
| Client effect | Trustees are choosing an endgame, not repairing a deficit. Sponsors can now see a route to sharing surplus without winding the scheme up. |
| Decision that changes | Buyout or run-on. If run-on: what protections members get before any surplus is released. |
| Existing investments | Buyout-bound schemes move towards assets an insurer will accept. Run-on schemes need portfolios that pay pensions from income for decades. |
| Possible GSAM response {{L:B}} | For buyout-bound clients, an orderly transition. For run-on clients, cashflow-matching credit portfolios and delegated management. For the insurers receiving the assets, insurance asset management. |
| Sales responsibility | Find out which endgame a prospect has chosen before proposing anything. A scheme two years from buyout is not a prospect for a ten-year strategy. |
| Relationship management responsibility | Help an existing client reach its chosen endgame well, including when that endgame removes the assets. |
| Risks and counterarguments | The index uses a measure that is less demanding than insurer pricing, so a surplus on it does not mean a scheme can afford buyout. Surplus-release rules were not in force at the cutoff. A fall in yields could remove a surplus after it has been paid out. |

**Client question:** "Is your target buyout, run-on or still open, and which of your current holdings would be hardest to hand to an insurer?"

**When this is not a reason to sell.** When a well-funded scheme with a weak sponsor has decided to buy out. The right outcome for members reduces the manager's assets. Chapter 7 covers why helping that happen well is part of the job.

### 3.2 Defined contribution: scale and private markets

:::l1
In a defined contribution (DC) pension each member has a pot. The employer picks a provider, and most members stay in the provider's default fund. The provider, not the member, chooses the managers inside that default.

The government wants fewer, larger providers that invest more in private companies and infrastructure. That makes each remaining provider a very large buyer with a tight cost budget.
:::

{{L:A}} Under the Pension Schemes Act 2026, master trust and group personal pension default arrangements must reach at least £25bn by 2030, or be on track for it by 2035. The requirement cannot start before 1 January 2030. The Act also creates a reserve power to require minimum private-market and UK allocations. {{V}} [@R3-S20] Separately, seventeen workplace pension providers signed the voluntary Mansion House Accord on 13 May 2025, aiming for 10% of default funds in private markets by 2030, with at least half of that in the UK. {{V}} [@R3-S23]

Card: [DC scale and private markets](#D-R3-04).

| Step | DC scale and private markets |
|---|---|
| What happened | Scale test legislated April 2026. Voluntary 10% private-markets pledge signed May 2025. |
| Client effect | Providers below scale must grow or merge. Survivors need private-market exposure that works inside a daily-priced default fund. |
| Decision that changes | Which managers supply private-market components, in what vehicle, at what cost. |
| Existing investments | Defaults are mostly low-cost index funds today. Private assets are added in small slices alongside them. |
| Possible GSAM response {{L:B}} | Offer private-market and multi-asset building blocks designed for DC. Whether GSAM holds UK DC default mandates was not established in this research. {{NPE}} |
| Sales responsibility | Treat each large provider as a single sophisticated account with a long decision cycle and a cost ceiling. |
| Relationship management responsibility | Supply the evidence the provider needs for its own value-for-money reporting. |
| Risks and counterarguments | The Accord is voluntary and conditional. The reserve power had not been used at the cutoff. Private assets cost more and must justify it. Incumbents with index scale are better placed for the core of a default. |

**Client question:** "How will you reach your private-market target inside your charge budget, and what would make you remove a manager from the default?"

## 4. OCIO and fiduciary management

:::l1
In an outsourced chief investment officer (OCIO) arrangement, called fiduciary management in the UK, trustees keep the big decisions (the objective, how much risk, the endgame) and hand day-to-day investment decisions to one provider. The provider chooses the underlying managers, runs the liability hedge and reports back.

The client is not buying a fund. It is delegating responsibility. That changes the sale (a regulated tender, usually run by an independent adviser), the fee (a low rate on the whole portfolio) and the relationship (the provider is accountable for the total outcome).
:::

{{L:A}} GSAM uses "OCIO" and "fiduciary management" for the same business in the UK. {{V}} [@R3-S08][@R3-S01] Its OCIO page states more than $540bn of OCIO assets under supervision at 30 June 2026. {{V}} [@R8-S22] A July 2026 press release gives $480bn at 31 March 2026. {{V}} [@R3-S09] The two figures come from different Goldman Sachs pages and dates and were not reconciled here. The business is global: US appointments by Verizon and Lockheed Martin were announced in July 2026. {{V}} [@R3-S09] The former intern's belief that OCIO exists only in the UK team is not supported as a description of Goldman Sachs. {{CE}}

{{L:A}} Two large UK-related appointments are public. Goldman Sachs announced on 6 September 2023 that the trustees of the BAE Systems pension schemes had appointed GSAM for about £23bn of assets, with the schemes' in-house investment team joining GSAM ([card](#M-R7-01)). {{V}} [@R3-S01][@R3-S02] In September 2025 GSAM was appointed by pension entities and a captive insurer related to Shell for about $40bn across several countries ([card](#M-R7-02)). {{V}} [@R3-S04][@R3-S05] No transfer of an in-house team was reported for Shell, so staff transfer is a feature of some very large deals and not of the model. {{SI}}

{{L:A}} The UK market: Isio's 2025 survey put DB fiduciary assets at £241bn across 832 mandates, down from 865, the first fall since 2008. {{V}} [@R3-S25] Trade coverage in 2026 put assets above £250bn, up 58% over three years, with about 90% of the growth from schemes of £1bn or more. Full OCIO-style mandates were about 6% of mandates by number and 53% of assets. Fees fell about 27% over five years, driven by competition above £500m, while fees for mandates under £50m rose. {{V}} [@R8-S10][@R8-S09] Since 1 October 2022 trustees must run a competitive tender with at least three unconnected providers before fiduciary managers run 20% or more of scheme assets. {{V}} [@R3-S24]

Cards: [OCIO moves up-market](#D-R3-06), [fewer, larger mandates](#D-R8-07), [tendering rules](#D-R3-07), [consultant consolidation](#D-R3-08).

| Step | OCIO moves to large schemes while the market consolidates |
|---|---|
| What happened | Multi-billion appointments in 2023 and 2025. Mandate numbers fell for the first time (year to June 2025; published late 2025 and spring 2026). Fees fell at the top end. Providers merged or left. |
| Client effect | Large sponsors ask whether running an in-house pension investment team is core business. Large schemes have many bidders and pricing power. Small schemes have fewer bidders. |
| Decision that changes | Which decisions to delegate, to whom, on what fee, and how to get them back. |
| Existing investments | On appointment the whole portfolio is transitioned. On buyout the mandate ends. |
| Possible GSAM response {{L:B}} | Compete where bespoke capability matters, be explicit about the endgame path, and say what happens to the fee when the scheme buys out. |
| Sales responsibility | Win a regulated tender judged by an independent evaluator. The pitch is to the trustees, the sponsor and the adviser at once. |
| Relationship management responsibility | Report against the trustees' objectives, not against a product benchmark, and manage the conflict when the provider's own funds are used. |
| Risks and counterarguments | The pool of schemes shrinks as they buy out. A few clients are a large share of assets. GSAM's UK market share and fee rates are not public. {{NPE}} Other providers also win: BlackRock was appointed to the £8bn British Coal Staff Superannuation Scheme on 1 July 2026. {{V}} [@R8-S26] |

**Awards, precisely.** GSAM received the fiduciary management and OCIO award at the European Pensions Awards on 2 July 2026 and at the Irish Pensions Awards on 4 November 2026. {{V}} [@R3-S10][@R3-S11] It did not win the fiduciary manager category at the 2026 UK Pensions Awards. {{V}} [@R3-S14] An award shows that a judging panel rated a submission. It does not establish market share, performance or client satisfaction.

**Client question:** "Which decisions do you want to keep, which do you want to delegate, and how would you get them back if you changed your mind?"

## 5. Third Party Wealth

:::l1
Third Party Wealth sells to firms that invest other people's money: private banks, wealth managers, model-portfolio providers, fund-of-funds teams and adviser networks. The buyer is a professional selector. The money belongs to that firm's clients.

Two features shape everything. First, a chain of people stands between the manager and the end investor, and each link can say no. Second, getting onto a buy list is permission to be bought, not a purchase.
:::

{{L:A}} "Third Party Wealth" is a public Goldman Sachs name, including an "EMEA Third Party Wealth Business". {{V}} [@R4-S01] In Goldman Sachs's filings the channel is called "third-party distributed". Long-term assets under supervision (AUS) in that channel were $714bn at 30 September 2026, and it took $97bn of long-term net inflows in the first nine months of 2026. {{V}} [@R2-S02] Those are firm-wide figures. No EMEA or UK figure for the channel is disclosed. {{NPE}}

## 6. Insurance

:::l1
An insurer invests premiums until claims are paid. Its regulator requires it to hold capital against the risk in its investments. So an insurer does not ask "what does this yield?" It asks "what does this yield after the capital I must hold against it, and does it pay out when my liabilities fall due?"

That is why insurance is covered by specialists. A manager who cannot speak about capital, accounting and matching is not credible to an insurer's chief investment officer.
:::

{{L:A}} GSAM states it manages $651bn of general-account assets for insurers as at 31 December 2025. {{V}} [@R5-S02] Insurance appears to be run as a global client vertical with London and Frankfurt seats, not as a regional team. {{SI}} [@R5-S21] Its 2026 survey of 434 insurance investment and finance chiefs, published 25 March 2026, found 62% planning to increase private-asset allocations. {{V}} [@R5-S02][@R5-S03] Amended European Union Solvency II rules take effect on 30 January 2027. {{V}} [@R5-S39] In the UK the Prudential Regulation Authority consulted in April 2025 on letting annuity writers add certain assets to matching-adjustment portfolios before formal approval, within limits. {{V}} [@R5-S38] The final UK rules were not read in this research.

Cards: [capital rules](#D-R5-04), [insurers and private assets](#D-R5-05), and the link to pensions through [buy-ins](#D-R3-02).

| Step | Capital rules and private assets |
|---|---|
| What happened | EU changes effective 30 January 2027. UK consultation April 2025. Survey intentions published March 2026. |
| Client effect | The ranking of assets by return after capital charge shifts. UK annuity writers receiving pension buy-in assets may be able to invest in new asset types sooner. |
| Decision that changes | Which private and long-dated assets to add, and how fast. |
| Existing investments | Portfolios are re-tested under the new basis. Some holdings become more capital-efficient, some less. |

**When this is not a reason to sell.** When an insurer's constraint is liquidity under stress, more illiquid credit is the wrong answer even if the capital treatment improves.

## 7. Liquidity Solutions

:::l1
A money market fund (MMF) is a pooled fund that holds very short-term, high-quality debt. Companies, funds and pension schemes use it as an alternative to a bank deposit for cash they may need tomorrow.

The client's priorities are, in order: get the money back, get it back the same day, and earn a fair yield. The manager earns a small fee on the balance. Balances move quickly and for reasons that have nothing to do with service, such as a company paying a dividend or a pension scheme meeting a collateral call.
:::

{{L:A}} Goldman Sachs reports $1,051bn of liquidity products in its assets under supervision at 30 September 2026, a category that also includes private bank deposits. {{V}} [@R2-S02][@R2-S01] GSAM's own page says its Liquidity Solutions team oversees more than $1.2 trillion in money market and short-term assets. {{V}} [@R5-S01] The two measures differ in scope. ### The Dutch pension transition, as the worked example

:::l1
Dutch pension funds are moving from a system that promised a fixed pension to one where each member has a personal pot managed by age group. A promise needs protecting against falling interest rates, so the old funds held very large interest-rate hedges. A young member's pot does not need that protection. :::

{{L:A}} Thirty Dutch pension funds holding about €542bn had converted by 31 March 2026, most of them on 1 January 2026. The rest must transfer accrued entitlements by 1 January 2028. {{V}} [@R5-S35][@R5-S36]

### 11.3 Where people add measurable value, and where technology may do better

It would be easy to write "relationships cannot be automated". That is a slogan. A more defensible version separates the cases.

**Where technology is likely to do better.** Retrieving the approved answer to a standard question. Producing the first draft of a recurring report. Summarising a long document. Noticing that a client has not been contacted for six months. These are tasks where speed, recall and consistency matter and where a mistake is caught by review.

**Where a person adds value that can be observed.**

- *Accountability.* A named person signs what is sent to a client and answers for it. One industry analyst put it as AI being "unable to replace the credibility that comes from well-crafted and verified human communication". {{V}} [@R8-S41] That is one analyst's judgement, not a law.
- *Finding out what was not written down.* A request for proposal says what the trustees agreed to ask. It does not say which trustee is unconvinced or what went wrong with the last manager. That comes from conversation.
- *Deciding not to proceed.* Judging that a product does not suit a client, or that a prospect is two years from buyout and should not be pursued, is a judgement with a commercial cost attached.
- *Delivering bad news.* Explaining poor performance to a board that must then explain it to members.

**Where it is uncertain.** Whether clients will come to prefer a faster, cheaper, more automated service for routine matters and reserve people for the difficult moments. If so, the number of coverage people needed per client falls even though the value of each good one rises.

:::warn The apprenticeship problem
{{L:B}} Many junior tasks that are being automated (first drafts, notes, packs) were also how people learned the business. If those shrink, a junior person learns less by doing unless the firm replaces them with something else: earlier attendance at meetings, more responsibility for checking, structured questioning by seniors. A fair interview question to ask is how the team trains analysts now that drafting is assisted.
:::
