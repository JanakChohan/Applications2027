# Sales versus relationship management: what actually differs

## How to read this chapter

{{L:B}} Almost everything in this chapter is a description of **general industry practice** in institutional and wholesale asset management distribution. It is not a disclosed description of how Goldman Sachs Asset Management (GSAM) organises its people. Treat the whole chapter as {{SI}} unless a sentence carries a different badge. Three kinds of statement appear:

- **Regulatory facts**, cited to the regulator or the legislation and badged individually.
- **The former intern's labels** for the two sides of a team. These are {{UA}} and are discussed, not relied on.
- **Illustrative days, clients and work products**. Every client named in an example is fictional and every figure is {{IL}}.

:::warn What this chapter cannot tell you
It cannot tell you which side of which team you would join, how Goldman Sachs splits credit between sales and relationship management, or what either side is paid. Chapter 2 holds the verified programme facts and Chapter 4 audits the former intern's claims. Use this chapter to understand the work, then test it against what interviewers and team members actually say.
:::

## The short version

:::l1
An asset manager is paid a fee on the money it looks after. Two things therefore matter commercially: **new money arriving** and **existing money staying**. Most distribution teams divide their people along that line.

- **Sales** (also called business development) spends most of its time on organisations that are *not yet* clients, or on new mandates from existing ones. The typical output is a proposal that leads to an appointment.
- **Relationship management (RM)** spends most of its time on organisations that *already are* clients. The typical output is a client who understands what they own, gets answers quickly, and has a sound basis for deciding whether to stay, add or leave.

That is a useful first sketch. It becomes misleading if you take it literally, because the same person often does both, because the most valuable "sale" is frequently an existing client adding money, and because neither side makes the investment decisions that determine whether the client is ultimately satisfied.
:::

:::l2
A more precise way to state the split: both roles are **coverage** roles, meaning each person is accountable for a named list of organisations. They differ in *where the list sits in the lifecycle*. Sales lists are dominated by prospects and open searches, so the work is lumpy, forward-looking and judged on conversion. RM lists are dominated by funded mandates, so the work is periodic (reporting cycles, review meetings, contract events) and judged on retention, service quality and growth of the existing book. The boundary between "new" and "existing" is set by each firm's coverage model and its credit-allocation rules, and those rules differ by firm, region and client segment.
:::

## Where the labels come from, and why they are unreliable

The former intern used "CSG" for the sales side and "CCG (Client Coverage Group)" for the relationship side within a team, while also using "Client Solutions Group" for the whole umbrella. {{UA}} That is one person's shorthand from one team in one year. It may be accurate for that desk, it may be informal desk language, or it may have changed. Chapter 2 records what official sources establish about the names.

Three reasons to hold any label loosely:

1. **Titles are not standardised across the industry.** The person who retains clients may be called a relationship manager, client director, client portfolio manager, client advisor, account manager or client executive. The person who wins them may be called sales, business development, client solutions, distribution or coverage. Two firms can use the same title for different jobs.
2. **"Solutions" has at least three meanings.** It can mean the whole client-facing group, a team that designs multi-asset or liability-driven portfolios, or a marketing word for "we sell more than one product". Ask which one a speaker means.
3. **A label describes an organisational chart, and the work follows the client.** If a pension scheme's trustees phone the person they know, that person handles the call whatever their business card says.

A safe way to speak about this in an interview is to describe the *activity* ("covering prospective UK pension clients" or "servicing existing mandates") and let the interviewer supply their own team's name for it.

## What the two sides have in common

Before the differences, the shared ground, which is larger than most candidates expect:

- **Neither side manages money.** Portfolio managers decide what to buy and sell. Coverage professionals explain, represent and coordinate.
- **Both are regulated communicators.** In the UK, a firm's communications with clients must be "fair, clear and not misleading" under the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) Conduct of Business Sourcebook (COBS) rule 4.2.1R. {{SI}} [@W3-S06] Anything either side sends a client normally passes through compliance-approved templates or review.
- **Both depend on the same specialists**: portfolio managers, product or investment specialists, the request-for-proposal (RFP) team, legal, onboarding, operations, performance and reporting.
- **Both use the same client record.** Meeting notes, pipeline entries and contact maps live in a customer relationship management (CRM) system. The quality of those records is a junior responsibility on both sides.
- **Both are judged over long periods.** A mandate can take a year or more to win and can stay for a decade. Neither side sees quick feedback on most of its work.

## The comparison in detail

The tables below split the comparison into groups so they stay readable. "Where it blurs" is the column to study: it shows why a clean split rarely survives contact with a real client.

### Purpose and commercial responsibility

| Dimension | Sales | Relationship management | Where it blurs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core objective | Bring in new mandates and new client organisations | Keep existing mandates well served and appropriately sized | Extra money from an existing client can be led by either side |
| Commercial responsibility | New fee-paying assets, usually measured as gross or net new flows | The existing book: retention, renewal, growth, sometimes profitability | Credit for one outcome is often shared, with rules that differ by firm |
| Targets | Flows, pipeline value, number of qualified opportunities, meetings with target accounts | Retention, client satisfaction, review-cycle completion, growth of the book | Many firms give RM a new-business target and sales a retention interest |
| Typical time horizon | A search from first contact to funding | The life of the mandate, including re-tenders | Long sales cycles make "sales" feel like RM for a client that has not paid yet |

### Daily work

| Dimension | Sales | Relationship management | Where it blurs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily activities | Prospect research, outreach, discovery meetings, proposal and RFP coordination, pitch rehearsal, pipeline updates | Reporting oversight, review meetings, query handling, change requests, contract and fee events, escalation of problems | Both prepare and follow up meetings and both chase internal teams |
| Work products | Prospect briefing, capability pitch, RFP response, pricing proposal, pipeline report | Quarterly review pack, meeting minutes and action log, client service plan, breach or incident explanation, renewal paper | A review pack for a client who is considering a second mandate is a sales document in all but name |
| Client contact | Many first conversations, often with people who owe you nothing | Fewer organisations, repeated contact, wider range of contacts inside each | Senior decision-makers meet both |
| Rhythm | Irregular, driven by searches and deadlines set by the client or its consultant | Regular, driven by reporting dates and governance calendars | Problems arrive unscheduled on both sides |

### Knowledge and coordination

| Dimension | Sales | Relationship management | Where it blurs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investment knowledge | Broad: enough across many strategies to diagnose a need and bring the right specialist | Deep on what the client holds: guidelines, benchmark, attribution, risk limits, fee schedule | Both must explain a strategy accurately without overstating it |
| Client knowledge | Wide map of a market: who is searching, who advises them, when contracts end | Deep map of each client: governance, personalities, history, sensitivities | RM knowledge is the best source of sales intelligence about that client |
| Internal coordination | Assembling a pitch team and a response under deadline | Getting operations, legal, reporting and portfolio managers to deliver over years | Onboarding sits exactly on the seam and is where handovers fail |
| Negotiation | Fees, terms and guidelines before appointment, within limits approved internally | Fee reviews, guideline changes and service levels during the mandate | Neither side sets price alone; pricing normally needs approval |

### Pressure and progression

| Dimension | Sales | Relationship management | Where it blurs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure | Visible, numerical, binary: the mandate was won or it was not | Diffuse and continuous: many small obligations, occasional crises | A lost client is as visible as a lost pitch |
| Typical frustration | Months of work ending in a decision you cannot influence further | Being accountable for outcomes (performance, errors) you did not produce | Both explain decisions made by other people |
| Progression | Larger territories, bigger prospects, team leadership | Larger or more complex clients, senior client director roles, team leadership | People move between the two, and senior roles usually combine them |
| Compensation | See the evidence note below | See the evidence note below | Depends on firm, seniority and credit rules |

**Compensation evidence.** {{L:A}} London pay for these roles is not publicly established. Goldman Sachs's London, Frankfurt, Paris and Dubai listings leave the salary field empty [@R1-S02][@R1-S01]. {{V}} The only figures for London come from the former intern's call: an analyst base of £52,000 on the sales side and £48,000 on the relationship side, with a larger bonus in sales and a gap that widens with seniority [@R1-S41]. {{UA}} That is one person's account of one year, and second-hand for any seat other than his own.

Two official reference points exist, both from the United States, where pay-transparency rules require disclosure: a New York CSG Management & Strategy analyst listing shows a base range of $85,000 to $110,000, and a CSG business-strategy vice-president listing shows $125,000 to $250,000 [@R1-S13][@R1-S30]. {{V}} Neither is a client-facing role and neither is in London. A Chicago listing for the US adviser channel describes "a commission based role in addition to the base salary" [@R1-S12] {{V}}, which shows that pay structure varies by channel. Nothing public shows how London institutional or Third Party Wealth roles are paid. Self-reported salary websites could not be read and do not separate asset-management sales from relationship management.

{{L:B}} So the reported £4,000 gap may be accurate, but it cannot be checked, and it should not be quoted in an interview or used to rank the two roles. The fuller table of sources and their reliability is in the People and recruitment chapter.

:::warn Reading compensation claims
Pay differences between two roles tell you how one firm prices two sets of tasks in one period. They are not evidence about which role is more valuable, more skilled or a better fit for you. A difference in base salary at junior level, if it exists, may reflect seat scarcity, a variable-pay philosophy or simple history. Do not build a preference for sales or RM on an unverified pay figure.
:::

### The same comparison, in mechanics

:::l2
**Objectives.** A sales objective is naturally expressed as a *flow*: assets that arrive in a period. An RM objective is naturally expressed as a *stock*: assets and revenue that remain. Flows are volatile and easy to attribute to a moment. Stocks are stable and hard to attribute to anyone, because a mandate that stays may have stayed for reasons unrelated to service (good performance, client inertia, the cost of switching). This asymmetry is one reason sales results look more measurable than RM results even when RM work matters as much.

**Targets.** Where targets exist they are usually set on things the individual can influence only partly. A salesperson can control the number and quality of conversations, the accuracy of the diagnosis and the quality of the proposal. They cannot control the client's timetable, the consultant's rating of the strategy or the investment team's performance record. Good managers therefore watch *leading indicators* (qualified pipeline, meeting quality, win rate on searches entered) alongside the flow number.

**Negotiation.** Fee negotiation is constrained on three sides: the firm's pricing policy and approval process, the client's benchmark of what similar mandates cost, and, for some clients, "most favoured nation" clauses that promise a client it will not pay more than a comparable client. A coverage professional's judgement shows in knowing what to ask approval for, and in explaining to the client what a lower fee would and would not change.

**Internal coordination.** The scarce internal resource is specialist time, especially portfolio managers'. Sales competes for it before the client pays anything; RM draws on it to keep a paying client informed. Deciding when a client conversation genuinely needs the portfolio manager is a recurring judgement call for both.

**Commercial responsibility.** Retained revenue is not the same as incremental revenue. If a client was never going to leave, retaining it added nothing that would not have happened anyway. If a client stayed because a problem was explained honestly and fixed, the RM work was decisive. Firms cannot observe the counterfactual, so credit allocation is partly convention.
:::

## Why "sales wins, RM retains" is only a first approximation

Six situations where the slogan breaks down:

1. **Growth from existing clients.** A scheme with one mandate that awards a second is both retained and new business. Some firms route the second mandate to the original salesperson, some to the relationship manager, some to whoever the client called. The client usually neither knows nor cares.
2. **The consultant channel.** Many UK pension schemes choose managers on advice from an investment consultant. Trustees of occupational schemes are required to set objectives for the firms that give them investment consultancy services. {{V}} [@W3-S02] If a strategy is not rated by the consultant's research team it may never reach a shortlist, however good the client-facing salesperson is. **Consultant relations** is therefore a third function that both wins and retains, and it reports into different places at different firms.
3. **Small and regional teams.** A team covering a smaller market may have three or four people. Each covers a list of organisations from first meeting to final exit. The labels exist on paper only.
4. **Handover models.** Under a "hunter and farmer" model, sales hands the client to RM after funding. Under a "cradle to grave" model the same person stays. Under a paired model, a salesperson and a relationship manager cover the client together from the pitch onward. Each model changes what the two jobs feel like.
5. **Outsourced chief investment officer (OCIO) and fiduciary management.** Here the product *is* an ongoing relationship: the manager takes delegated responsibility for much of the scheme's investment decision-making. Winning is a formal, regulated process. From 1 October 2022, trustees of UK occupational schemes must run a qualifying competitive tender, inviting bids from at least three unconnected providers, before an appointment that would take fiduciary-managed assets to 20% or more of the scheme's manageable assets. {{V}} [@W3-S01] After appointment, the "relationship manager" may be a client director with real influence over how the portfolio is explained and governed. The sales and RM skill sets overlap heavily.
6. **Third-party wealth distribution.** When the buyer is a wealth manager's fund selector, there is rarely a single moment of "winning". A fund is researched, approved, made available on platforms, adopted by advisers and then either accumulates assets or does not. Keeping a place on an approved list is continuous selling and continuous servicing at once.

:::note A better mental model
Think of two *activities* that every coverage professional performs in different proportions: **origination** (finding and winning a new piece of business) and **stewardship** (looking after business already won). A job title tells you the expected proportion. It does not tell you the actual one.
:::

## How the split varies by firm and team

| Coverage model | How it works | Where it tends to appear | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separate teams (hunter and farmer) | Sales wins, then hands over to RM at or after funding | Large managers with many institutional clients | Specialisation, at the cost of a handover the client can feel |
| Paired coverage | A salesperson and a relationship manager cover each client together | Large, complex or strategic clients | Continuity, at the cost of two people's time |
| Single owner (cradle to grave) | One person covers a client through its whole life | Small regional teams, boutiques, some wealth channels | Clear accountability, at the cost of less time for prospecting |
| Client director plus servicing team | A senior commercial owner supported by a client service or operations team | OCIO and large segregated mandates | Senior attention, with day-to-day work delegated |
| Channel-based | Coverage organised by intermediary (consultant, platform, private bank) | Wholesale and third-party wealth | Deep channel knowledge, with less direct contact with end investors |

{{L:B}} Which model a given Goldman Sachs team uses is {{NPE}} in this chapter. Chapter 3 records what public sources show team by team.

## Adjacent roles: what this job is not

The roles below are often confused with client coverage in asset management. The table is compact by design; each role is a career in its own right.

| Role | Purpose | Who the client is | What is sold or decided | Decision rights |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asset management sales and RM (this role) | Match clients' investment needs to the manager's capabilities and keep the relationship sound | Pension schemes, insurers, sovereign funds, endowments, wealth managers | A mandate or fund holding, paid for by an ongoing fee on assets | Recommends and coordinates. Pricing and terms need approval. No investment discretion |
| Investment banking | Advise companies and governments on raising capital and on mergers and acquisitions | Corporate boards, founders, governments, financial sponsors | A transaction, paid for by a one-off fee | Advises; the client's board decides |
| Markets sales and trading | Help clients trade securities and manage risk through the bank's market-making desks | Asset managers, hedge funds, corporates, other banks | A trade or a hedge, paid through spread or commission | Sales advises on execution; traders commit the bank's balance sheet within risk limits |
| Portfolio management | Decide what a fund or mandate owns | The fund's investors, via the mandate | Buy, sell and sizing decisions | Full discretion within the investment guidelines |
| Investment research | Form views on securities, sectors or economies to inform investment decisions | Portfolio managers (buy-side) or external investors (sell-side) | A recommendation | Recommends; does not trade |
| Private wealth advice | Advise individuals and families on their whole financial position | Wealthy individuals, families, their offices | Advice, portfolios and banking services | Advises; sometimes manages on a discretionary basis under an agreed mandate |
| Fund operations | Make sure trades settle, assets are valued and records are right | Internal teams and, indirectly, every investor | Accuracy and control | Operates processes; escalates exceptions |
| Investment consulting | Advise asset owners on strategy and on which managers to hire | Trustees and investment committees | Advice, paid by fee; independent of any one manager | Recommends managers; the trustees decide |
| Product or investment specialist | Represent an investment team to clients in depth | Internal coverage teams and their clients | Explanation of a strategy, not a decision about it | Speaks for the strategy; does not usually trade |
| Client service | Handle reporting, queries and operational requests for existing clients | Existing clients' operations and finance staff | Timely, accurate service | Resolves within procedure; escalates commercial matters to RM |
| RFP team | Write formal responses to tenders and due-diligence questionnaires (DDQs) | Internal sales teams and, through them, prospects | A compliant, accurate written response | Owns the answer library and process; content owners approve the facts |

Three distinctions candidates most often blur:

- **Asset management sales is not markets sales.** Markets sales sells a transaction today and the bank earns a spread on it. Asset management sales proposes that a client delegate money for years and the firm earns a fee for as long as it stays. The time horizon, the product and the conflict-of-interest profile all differ.
- **Asset management RM is not private wealth advice.** A private wealth adviser owes advice to an individual about that individual's whole situation. An institutional relationship manager represents one manager to a professional client that has its own advisers and its own fiduciary duties.
- **A coverage professional is not an investment consultant.** The consultant is paid by the client to be impartial between managers. The coverage professional is paid by one manager. Being honest about that difference is part of being trusted.

## What each level of seniority realistically contributes

{{L:B}} The following is general practice, set out in full in the role cards (`data/roles.json`) with decision rights for each level.

- A **Summer Analyst** produces inputs: research on a client, a first draft of slides, a reconciled data table, notes from a meeting they observed. They do not own a client, quote a fee or send unreviewed material externally.
- An **Analyst** owns recurring inputs end to end: the briefing before every meeting, the pipeline file, the first draft of a review pack, the tracking of actions after a meeting.
- An **Associate** owns pieces of the process: coordinating an RFP response, running a smaller client's review cycle with supervision, checking an analyst's work.
- A **Vice President or Executive Director-level** professional owns client relationships or a territory and carries a commercial number.
- A **Managing Director** who leads a team owns the strategy for the segment, the largest relationships, pricing escalations and hiring.

How those titles map onto each other in a particular office is covered in Chapter 4. Do not assume the ladder is identical in every division or region.

## Five illustrative working days

Each day below is {{IL}}. They are composites written to show the *kind* of work, in a plausible order. They contain no clock times and no time-share percentages because no public source supports any. Real days are less tidy.

:::example Institutional sales (a mid-level salesperson covering UK pension schemes)
**Start of day.** Reads overnight notes: a consultant has published a search for a buy-and-maintain credit manager on behalf of the fictional Northfield Engineering Pension Scheme. Checks the CRM record. The scheme was met once, two years ago. Asks the analyst for a one-page briefing by midday.

**Morning.** Call with the consultant-relations colleague: is the firm's strategy rated by this consultant, and who is the field consultant advising Northfield? Then a call with the credit product specialist to test fit. The scheme wants a low-turnover portfolio matched to its benefit payments; one of the firm's two credit strategies fits and the other does not.

**Middle of the day.** Reviews the analyst's briefing, corrects the description of the scheme's funding position, and decides to enter the search. Kicks off the RFP: confirms deadline, page limit and which questions need fresh input from the portfolio managers.

**Afternoon.** A first meeting with a different prospect, a university endowment, which ends politely with no interest. Writes it up in four lines and sets a reminder for six months. Then a pitch rehearsal for a final presentation next week, where the portfolio manager's answer on downgrades is too long and gets rewritten.

**End of day.** Updates the pipeline: one opportunity added, one moved back a stage because the client has delayed its decision to the next trustee meeting.

*What this day shows:* most of the work is diagnosis and coordination. The single most consequential decision was choosing which strategy fits and declining to offer the one that does not.
:::

:::example Institutional relationship management (covering six funded pension clients)
**Start of day.** The quarterly report for the fictional Westmere County Pension Fund is due to go out. The analyst flags that the benchmark return in the draft differs from last quarter's methodology. Holds the report, asks the performance team to confirm, and tells the client's officer it will arrive a day late and why.

**Morning.** Preparation call with the portfolio manager before tomorrow's review meeting with a corporate scheme whose mandate has lagged its benchmark for three quarters. Agrees who says what: the relationship manager opens with the numbers plainly stated, the portfolio manager explains the two positions that caused most of the shortfall, and neither predicts a recovery date.

**Middle of the day.** A client's new head of pensions calls with an unfamiliar question about how the mandate would be affected if the scheme moved towards an insurance buyout. Does not guess. Promises an answer within two days and books time with the solutions team.

**Afternoon.** Works through a guideline change request: a client wants to add an exclusion list. Coordinates legal, the portfolio manager and compliance so the amendment is feasible and correctly worded before anything is promised.

**End of day.** Writes minutes and actions from last week's meeting, which should have gone sooner. Sends them with each action owned and dated.

*What this day shows:* the work is continuous and much of it is preventing small failures from becoming reasons to leave. The hardest moment was choosing accuracy over punctuality on the report.
:::

:::example Third-party wealth sales (covering UK wealth managers and private banks)
**Start of day.** Reviews flow data for the funds on the approved list at the fictional wealth manager Harbour & Vale. One fund is approved but has taken almost no money in six months. Approval did not produce adoption.

**Morning.** Meeting with a fund selector at a different firm who is reviewing global equity managers. The selector has forty minutes, a scoring template and three competitors to see this week. Leads with the two questions the selector asked last time, then lets the investment specialist cover process.

**Middle of the day.** Follows up with data the selector requested in a specific format: rolling three-year returns against a named peer group, in the selector's own template. The analyst builds it; it is checked against the official factsheet before sending.

**Afternoon.** Presents to a room of advisers at Harbour & Vale to explain where the under-used fund fits in their model portfolios. Hears the real obstacle: advisers find the fund hard to explain to clients. Takes that back to marketing as a request for a two-page plain-language guide.

**End of day.** Logs meetings and updates the list of selectors with open reviews.

*What this day shows:* the buyer is a professional intermediary with a process, and the end investor is two steps away. Selling continues long after the "sale".
:::

:::example Third-party wealth relationship management (same channel, existing relationships)
**Start of day.** A portfolio manager on a widely held fund has announced a departure. Drafts, with the product team and compliance, a factual note for every firm that holds the fund: what is changing, who takes over, what is not changing, when the client can speak to the new manager.

**Morning.** Calls the three largest holders personally before the note goes out, so they do not learn it from the trade press.

**Middle of the day.** A platform has queried a share-class fee discrepancy. Coordinates with operations to confirm which figure is right and sends a corrected data file. For funds held by retail investors, the FCA's Consumer Duty requires firms to "act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers", and manufacturers must ensure their products provide fair value, so pricing data errors are not treated as trivial. {{V}} [@W3-S04] {{SI}} [@W3-S05]

**Afternoon.** Annual due-diligence meeting with a private bank's research team: updates on the team, process, capacity and operational controls. Much of the meeting is confirming that nothing has changed.

**End of day.** Updates the watch-list tracker: which holders have put the fund "under review" after the manager change, and when each decision is due.

*What this day shows:* stewardship in a wealth channel is largely about information reaching many holders accurately and early.
:::

:::example Consultant relations (covering investment consultants' research teams)
**Start of day.** Reads a consultant's quarterly research agenda. Two of the firm's strategies are due for a rating review.

**Morning.** Prepares, with the investment team, the data pack the consultant's researcher requires: holdings history, team changes, capacity, attribution. The researcher is assessing the strategy for many clients at once, so a weak meeting has wide consequences.

**Middle of the day.** Accompanies a portfolio manager to the research meeting. Says little. Notes each question the researcher asks twice, because those are the unresolved doubts.

**Afternoon.** Debriefs sales and RM colleagues: which strategies are rated, which are not, and which searches the firm is therefore unlikely to be invited to. Tells a salesperson that pursuing one prospect is probably wasted effort until the rating changes.

**End of day.** Sends the researcher the follow-up material promised, exactly as specified.

*What this day shows:* the "client" is a gatekeeper who never invests a pound directly. Success is measured in ratings and invitations, and the function serves both origination and stewardship.
:::

## Sample work products

These are the things a junior person actually produces. All clients are fictional and all figures {{IL}}.

:::workproduct Client briefing (one page, before a first meeting)
**Northfield Engineering Pension Scheme: prospect briefing**

- **What it is.** Closed defined benefit (DB) scheme of a mid-sized engineering company. Assets about £1.2bn per latest published accounts. Source: scheme annual report, page reference noted.
- **Governance.** Nine trustees including an independent chair. Investment sub-committee meets quarterly. Investment consultant named in the report.
- **Position.** Funding level improved over three years. Statement of Investment Principles says the scheme is reducing return-seeking assets.
- **Current managers.** Listed in the report: four, including an incumbent credit manager appointed several years ago.
- **Why now.** Consultant has opened a search for buy-and-maintain credit.
- **Our history.** One meeting two years ago. No follow-up recorded.
- **What we do not know.** The reason for replacing or adding to the incumbent. The scheme's timetable to insurance buyout, if any.
- **Three questions to ask.** What problem is this mandate solving? What would make you uncomfortable in year three? How will the trustees judge success?
:::

:::workproduct Meeting preparation note (existing client, difficult meeting)
**Calder University Endowment: annual review, preparation**

- **Purpose of meeting.** Annual review. Mandate is behind benchmark over one and three years.
- **Attendees.** Client: chief investment officer, investment manager, one committee member. Us: relationship manager, portfolio manager, analyst (notes).
- **What they are likely to ask.** Why has it lagged? Has the process changed? Why should we stay?
- **Agreed messages.** State the shortfall in the first two minutes. Attribute it to two named decisions. Say what has and has not changed in positioning. No forecast of when it recovers.
- **Open items from last meeting.** Two closed, one late (carbon report). Apologise and give a date.
- **What we must not say.** Anything implying a guaranteed outcome, or a comparison to a peer that the data does not support.
- **Decision we need.** None. This is a listening meeting.
:::

| Opportunity | Stage | Change this week | Next step | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|

:::workproduct DDQ and RFP response extract
**Question 14.** *Describe any changes to the investment team in the last three years and explain how continuity was maintained.*

**Draft response.** "Two of the seven investment professionals named on the strategy joined in the last three years, and one left. [Names, dates and roles from the approved team table.] The departing analyst's coverage was reassigned to [name] within the existing team. **Analyst's checks before submission.** Dates matched to the HR-approved team table. Wording matched to the answer library, updated for the latest quarter. Sent to the portfolio manager for sign-off as content owner. Compliance review logged. The word "stable" removed, because the facts should carry the point.

:::workproduct Performance-review pack (outline)
1. **Summary page.** Value of the mandate, return against benchmark for the quarter, one year, three years and since inception, in one table. Three sentences of plain explanation.
2. **What drove returns.** Attribution by the two or three decisions that mattered, positive and negative.
3. **Positioning and changes.** What was bought and sold, and why.
4. **Risk and guidelines.** Compliance with each guideline; any breach, with cause and remedy.
5. **Outlook.** The team's view, labelled as a view.
6. **Service and operations.** Actions from last meeting, reporting timeliness, any incidents.
7. **Fees.** What was charged in the period and how it was calculated.
8. **Appendix.** Holdings, transactions, glossary.

*What the junior does:* populates from approved data sources, reconciles every figure to the official performance record, and flags anything that does not tie before a senior person sees it.
:::

## The lifecycle in brief

The interactive lifecycle (`data/lifecycle.json`) traces twelve stages for an institutional client and a seven-stage variant for wealth intermediaries. In summary:

## Five misconceptions, examined

### "Sales is just persuasion"

**What is true in it.** A final presentation is a persuasive event. Delivery, confidence and rapport affect outcomes at the margin, and trustees are human.

**What it leaves out.** Institutional buyers are professionals with advisers, scoring frameworks and legal duties. A scheme cannot hire a manager because its salesperson was charming; it must be able to justify the choice. Most of the work that determines the outcome happens before the pitch: understanding the actual problem, choosing whether the firm has a credible answer, and declining to compete where it does not.

**The harder point.** Persuasion without fit creates a mandate that disappoints and leaves within a few years, at cost to the client and to the firm's reputation with the consultant who watched it happen. The commercial incentive and the client's interest are aligned over a long horizon and can diverge over a short one. That divergence is where judgement is tested.

### "Relationship management is administration"

**What is true in it.** A meaningful share of RM work is procedural: reports, minutes, forms, chasing. A junior person will do a lot of it.

**What it leaves out.** The procedural work is how a client forms its opinion of the firm. A scheme rarely leaves over one quarter's performance. It leaves when poor performance coincides with late reports, unanswered questions and a sense of not being told the truth. The relationship manager is also the person who decides when to escalate, what the client needs to hear before they ask, and how to explain an error.

**The harder point.** Some RM seats genuinely are closer to client service than to commercial ownership. The title does not tell you. Ask what decisions the role makes and what it is accountable for.

### "Technical excellence alone wins business"

**What is true in it.** A strategy with weak performance, an unstable team or poor risk controls will not pass due diligence. Technical quality is a necessary condition.

**What it leaves out.** Several managers usually clear the technical bar. The decision between them turns on fit with the client's specific objective, clarity of explanation, fees, operational confidence, and whether the client believes these people will tell them bad news promptly. An excellent strategy that nobody on the client's side fully understands is hard to hold through a bad period.

**The harder point.** A coverage professional who cannot follow the technical argument cannot diagnose fit and cannot be trusted by a sophisticated client. Knowledge matters; it is insufficient by itself.

### "Performance alone retains clients"

**What is true in it.** Sustained underperformance is the commonest stated reason for termination, and no amount of service retains a mandate that has failed its purpose.

**What it leaves out.** Clients leave well-performing managers when their own strategy changes: a pension scheme de-risks, an endowment restructures, a wealth manager consolidates its list. They also stay with underperforming managers when they understand why, believe the process is intact and trust what they are told. Performance sets the context; communication determines how it is interpreted.

**The harder point.** Sometimes a client is right to leave despite good service, and a relationship manager who talks them out of it has not served them. Retention is not automatically a good outcome.

### "Every relationship should be expanded"

**What is true in it.** A client who already trusts the firm is a more likely buyer than a stranger, and firms reasonably measure growth of existing relationships.

**What it leaves out.** A client may have good reasons to limit exposure to any one manager. A second product may not suit them. A request to meet about "other capabilities" at the wrong moment, for instance while a service problem is unresolved, costs credibility. For funds reaching retail investors through intermediaries, UK rules add a specific discipline: a distributor "must not distribute a product unless its distribution arrangements are consistent with the product providing fair value to retail customers". {{SI}} [@W3-S05]

**The harder point.** The honest test is whether the proposal would be made if the relationship manager were advising the client independently. Where the answer is no, the right action is not to propose it, and to be able to explain that decision internally.

## The difficult parts

No role description lists these. They are the parts most likely to decide whether you enjoy the work.

- **Repetition.** The reporting cycle recurs every quarter. Briefings follow a template. Answer libraries are updated line by line. Skill shows in catching the one figure that does not tie, which requires attention on the fortieth repetition.
- **Uncertain cycles.** A search can be delayed by a trustee meeting, a change of consultant, a corporate transaction or a market shock. Months of work can be paused without explanation. You plan around other people's calendars.
- **Rejection.** Most searches entered are lost, because several credible managers compete for one mandate. Feedback is often thin. The useful habit is asking what the client was solving for and recording it, since that is information for next time.
- **Internal dependencies.** You commit to a client on behalf of people who do not report to you: a portfolio manager's time, a legal review, a reporting change. Much of the job is earning priority from colleagues who have their own deadlines.
- **Dissatisfied clients.** You will sit in meetings where a client is unhappy about results you did not produce. The task is to state the facts plainly, avoid defensiveness and leave with specific actions.
- **Fee pressure.** Clients benchmark fees and consultants negotiate hard. You will carry requests for reductions to people who do not want to grant them, and carry refusals back.
- **Explaining outcomes you did not control.** This is the defining discomfort of the role. You are accountable for the client's experience of decisions made by portfolio managers, operations teams and markets. Some people find that energising because it puts them at the centre of the conversation. Others find it frustrating because they would rather make the decision themselves. Knowing which you are is useful evidence about fit.

:::note A question worth answering honestly
If the first year consisted mainly of preparing other people's meetings, reconciling data and updating trackers, with client conversations led by someone else, would the work still interest you? What specifically about it?
:::

## Regulatory guardrails that shape both roles

Three UK rules explain behaviour that otherwise looks like bureaucracy.

- **Competitive tendering for fiduciary management.** The duties introduced by the Competition and Markets Authority's 2019 Order were moved into pensions regulations in force from 1 October 2022, overseen by The Pensions Regulator. {{V}} [@W3-S01] Trustees must set objectives for investment consultancy providers and run a qualifying tender for fiduciary management above the threshold. {{V}} [@W3-S02] *Consequence for the role:* OCIO sales is a structured, documented competition, and incumbency is not protection.
- **Consumer Duty.** The Consumer Principle is "A firm must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers". {{V}} [@W3-S04] The rules came into force on 31 July 2023 for products open to sale or renewal and on 31 July 2024 for closed products. {{V}} [@W3-S03] *Consequence for the role:* in third-party wealth, the manager and the intermediary each carry obligations for the end investor, so conversations about target market, value and information are part of ordinary coverage. The Duty is framed around retail customers; purely institutional mandates sit largely outside it.
- **Fair, clear and not misleading communications.** {{SI}} [@W3-S06] *Consequence for the role:* you do not improvise performance claims, and the review step that slows a document down is the control that lets the client rely on it.
