# Pictet Summer Intern Quote Card — research, breakdown, and drafts

**Request (Isabel, Comms):** one to two sentences answering *"How has this summer internship
helped you grow personally or professionally?"* and/or *"What is the most important thing you
have learned during your internship?"* Plus: confirm internship end date so a photographer slot
can be booked, ideally w/c 7 September.

**Route to publication:** your quote → manager approval → paired with a professional headshot →
sent back to you with a proposed caption → posted on Pictet's LinkedIn as part of a limited
London summer-intern series.

That route matters more than the words themselves, and it is the first thing to understand.

---

## 1. What this format actually is

This is not a testimonial, a reflection, or a LinkedIn post of your own. It is **corporate
employer-brand collateral with your face on it**, and it has three readers, in this order:

| Reader | What they need from it | What kills it |
|---|---|---|
| **Your manager** (approval gate) | Nothing that overstates your access, misrepresents the desk, or could be read as commentary on clients/markets | Anything specific enough to be confidential; anything that sounds like you ran the desk |
| **Comms** (Isabel) | Something quotable, short enough for the card, on-brand, that flatters the programme *without sounding scripted* | Length. Clichés. Praise so effusive it reads as coerced |
| **The scrolling stranger** | ~2 seconds of attention, next to your headshot | Anything they have read a hundred times before |

Practical consequences:

- **Length is a hard constraint, not a style choice.** A quote card is a square image with a
  headshot. Roughly **20–40 words / under ~250 characters** survives at legible type size. Go
  longer and Comms will cut it — and they will cut the half you cared about.
- **You are writing something to be read aloud in your voice.** Contractions ("I've", "it's")
  are good. Formal register is not.
- **Every word gets scanned by someone who will one day interview you.** This is a permanent,
  searchable artefact attached to your name. It should still read well in 2029 when you're
  applying for a graduate role — at Pictet or anywhere else.

## 2. Research: what interns at comparable firms actually say

LinkedIn's public search is heavily restricted, so most verbatim examples below come from firms'
own careers pages and university internship-spotlight features — the identical genre (short
first-person quote + headshot + employer branding), just a different surface.

### 2.1 The single most useful source: Pictet's own published quotes

Pictet already publishes people-quotes on its careers and career-stories pages. These are the
house style you are being asked to match, and they are **markedly shorter and plainer** than
the US-bank equivalent:

> "You are given important responsibilities from day one, which is great for establishing trust
> with the rest of your team." — Noah Asser, Graduate Programme *(20 words)*

> "Don't be afraid to ask questions." — Sara Pratt Dos Santos *(6 words)*

> "Taking your time in your career choices is not wasting time; sometimes, it's the opposite."
> — Camille Blondin *(15 words)*

> "Nothing is out of reach, but success does not happen by chance. Hard work is always rewarded
> and every cloud has a silver lining, even in unexpected situations." — Sami Kansara

> "It is important for the apprentice to build connections with the entire team and feel
> engaged." — Aurore Glorieux and Raphael Domergue

**Pattern:** plain vocabulary, no rhetorical flourish, no superlatives about the firm, frequently
*advice-shaped* rather than experience-shaped, and often a single sentence. Nobody says
"incredible". Nobody thanks anyone. This is a Swiss private-bank register: understated, and
confident enough not to sell.

**Read-across for you: aim for the short end.** A 25-word quote will look native. A 45-word quote
will look like it was written by someone trying hard.

> has been meaningful." — Lucas W.

> "I was able to make an immediate impact on my teams by providing meaningful contributions to my
> live deals." — Maddie N.
>
> "I'm incredibly grateful for the opportunity to intern with BGL, and I feel that this is the
> best place to build a long-term career." — Nick J.

University spotlight features (same genre, different publisher):

> "One of the most valuable things I've learned this summer is to be curious as an intern, and
> asking questions goes a long way." — State Street intern
>
> "The biggest advice I would give to other students looking for a similar experience is to always
> be the most thoughtful person in the room." — Barclays intern
>
> "I met so many industry leaders and intelligent people, so it felt like I was learning a textbook
> worth of information every day." — RBC intern
>
> "The most rewarding days were when I was most challenged." — asset management intern, 2025
>
> Schroders intern, on her summer: at the start "I did feel nervous, but over time I felt more
> comfortable to ask those questions, which definitely allowed me to work much more efficiently
> and learn more."

**Diagnosis of this set.** Note which ones you actually remember five minutes later. It's the short,
odd ones — "the most rewarding days were when I was most challenged", "be the most thoughtful person
in the room". The ones that vanish are the ones stacking generic positives: *meaningful, impact,
grateful, best place, opportunity*. Nick J.'s quote is the weakest on the wall precisely because it
is the most complimentary — praise of the employer reads as procured, not felt.

### 2.3 The six archetypes, ranked

Nearly every intern quote in this genre is one of six things. Ranked by how well they work on a
Pictet card:

| # | Archetype | Typical wording | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Expectation inverted** — "I came in thinking X; actually Y" | Schroders' "nervous at first → comfortable asking questions" | **Best.** Contains a miniature story, so it survives the 2-second scan. Almost nobody uses it. |
| 2 | **Trusted with real work** | Pictet's own "important responsibilities from day one" | **Strong and on-brand** — responsibility is a stated Pictet value. Risk: it's also verbatim recruitment-brochure language at Barclays and others, so it needs your own concrete detail attached. |
| 3 | **Ask the questions** | State Street, Schroders, Pictet's Sara | **Safe but crowded.** Only works if you invert it (see draft C) rather than state it. |
| 4 | **The people / the culture** | BGL's "senior bankers care about interns" | **Weak alone.** Says nothing a reader couldn't guess. Works only as evidence for a larger point. |
| 5 | **Technical skills gained** | "learnt modelling, valuation, Excel" | **Weak on a card.** Reads like a CV bullet, dates instantly, and invites "so what?" |
| 6 | **Gratitude** | "grateful for the opportunity" | **Avoid.** Pictet's own published quotes never do this. |

## 3. Calibrating to Pictet specifically

Pictet describes itself as *"an independent investment partnership known for our long-term mindset,
responsible approach to business and entrepreneurial spirit"* — principles it says have *"defined us
since 1805"*, alongside a commitment to *"enduring quality"*. The stated values are **independence,
long-term thinking, partnership, responsibility and entrepreneurial spirit**. Structurally: no
external shareholders, seven owner-managers, only 47 partners in 220 years, average partner tenure
over 21 years — continuity is the whole identity.

**The opportunity:** a quote that quietly demonstrates one of those values is far stronger than one
that praises the programme.

**The trap — and this is the main thing to avoid:** do *not* say the words. A quote containing
"long-term thinking", "responsibility" and "entrepreneurial spirit" reads as though Comms wrote it
and you signed it. That is the single most common failure mode in employer-brand content, and
readers detect it instantly.

**The technique: show the value, never name it.**

| Pictet value | ❌ Naming it (dead) | ✅ Showing it (alive) |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term thinking | "I learned the importance of long-term thinking." | "…nobody here was in a hurry to have an answer by Friday." |
| Responsibility | "I was given real responsibility from day one." | "…my work went into something a client would actually read." |
| Partnership | "The collaborative culture was fantastic." | "…people who had no obligation to give me their time gave me an hour." |
| Enduring quality | "Pictet is committed to excellence." | "…the difference between finished and right is usually one more check." |

## 4. Word-level rules

You said each word matters. These are the ones that do the work.

**Structure**
- **Two sentences, not one long one.** Sentence 1 = a concrete, specific observation only you
  could make. Sentence 2 = the transferable lesson. That ordering — specific then general — is
  what makes a quote feel earned rather than asserted.
- **Best shape by a distance: expectation → reality.** "I expected X. In fact Y." It smuggles a
  narrative into 30 words.
- **Land on a strong noun or verb.** Never end on a trailing subordinate clause ("…, which was
  really useful for my development"). The last three words are what people remember.
- **One em-dash maximum. Zero exclamation marks. Zero semicolons.**

**Diction**
- **Contractions yes** — "I've", "it's", "wasn't". They make it sound spoken, which is the point.
- **"I've learned" over "I learned"** — present perfect, because you're still in it.
- **Concrete verbs over abstract nouns.** "checked", "sat in on", "drafted", "asked" beat
  "exposure", "insight", "development", "opportunity".
- **First person singular throughout.** "We" borrows credit you don't yet have.

**Banned list** — every one of these appears in the corpus above and every one is invisible:

> invaluable · incredible · amazing · fantastic · eye-opening · steep learning curve ·
> hit the ground running · hands-on experience · grateful for the opportunity ·
> welcoming culture · the people are what make it · real responsibility from day one ·
> gave me exposure to · fast-paced environment · take my career to the next level ·
> I can't thank X enough

Two extra flags: **"real responsibility from day one"** is literally Barclays' and others'
recruiting copy, so it reads as brochure; and **any superlative about Pictet** ("the best place
to start a career") reads as procured.

**Compliance / manager-approval hygiene**
- No client names, no fund names, no deal or mandate references, no AUM or performance figures,
  no internal system names, no headcount or team-structure detail.
- Nothing that could be read as a market view or investment opinion — you are not authorised to
  hold one publicly on the firm's channel.
- Nothing that overstates your role. "I built the model the team used" invites a manager edit and
  a slightly awkward conversation. Understatement is safer *and* reads better.

## 5. Draft quotes

Ranked. Each is annotated with what it's doing and where it's risky. `[bracketed]` text is a slot
only you can fill — and filling it is what separates yours from every other intern's.

### Tier 1 — lead with one of these

**A. Expectation inverted + trusted with real work** *(35 words)*
> "I arrived expecting to spend the summer watching other people work. Within a fortnight I was
> [doing X], and being trusted with something real turned out to be the fastest way to get good
> at it."

*Why it works:* the strongest shape in the genre, and it demonstrates Pictet's "responsibility"
value without naming it. The final clause is a genuine claim about learning, not a compliment.
*Slot:* `[doing X]` = the most ordinary-sounding real task you did. Ordinary is better than
impressive — "checking a reconciliation that actually went out" beats "contributing to strategy".

**B. Curiosity, inverted** *(36 words)*
> "I came in thinking I had to sound like I already knew the answer. The most useful thing I've
> learned is that the sharpest people here are usually the ones asking the simplest questions."

*Why it works:* takes the single most crowded archetype in the corpus and flips it, so it reads as
observation rather than advice. Self-aware without being self-deprecating. Flatters the firm
entirely by implication. This is the most *quotable* option — the one Comms will want in the pull-out.
*Risk:* none material. Safest of the Tier 1 set for manager approval.

**C. Craft / enduring quality** *(31 words)*
> "Nobody here rushes a piece of work to be finished. I've learned that the gap between something
> that's done and something that's right is usually one more check — and that it's always worth it."

*Why it works:* the most authentically *Pictet-sounding* of the set. Maps directly onto "enduring
quality" and a 220-year-old partnership's self-image, without using a single word of brand
vocabulary. Understated register matches Noah Asser's and Camille Blondin's published quotes.
*Risk:* slightly implies other places rush. Soften to "Nobody here rushes a piece of work" if your
manager is cautious.

### Tier 2 — strong, use if the desk fits

**D. Partnership / access** *(30 words)*
> "I'd assumed seniority meant distance. What I didn't expect was how much of my summer was spent
> in conversations with people who had no obligation to give me their time."

*Why it works:* expectation-inverted shape again, and it's the most generous thing you can say about
a firm's culture without saying "great culture". Reads as a real observation.

**E. Long-term thinking** *(29 words)*
> "The biggest shift for me has been learning to think in decades rather than quarters. In a market
> that rewards reacting quickly, the harder discipline is knowing when not to."

*Why it works:* the most on-brand idea available to you at a firm whose identity is continuity across
generations.
*Risk:* "decades not quarters" is close to a finance idiom, and this is the draft most likely to be
read as brand parroting. Use it only if you genuinely observed it — and be ready for the manager to
love it, because it flatters the house view.

**F. Confidence / personal growth** *(34 words)*
> "I've stopped being afraid of being the least experienced person in the room. You learn more
> sitting next to someone who has done this for twenty years than you ever will reading about it."

*Why it works:* answers the *personal* growth prompt cleanly, which most interns won't. Honest,
likeable, ages well.

**G. Purpose / client** *(31 words)*
> "It's easy to picture this industry as numbers on a screen. What I'll take away is that behind
> every decision is a family or an institution relying on someone getting it right."

*Why it works:* well-suited to a private bank serving clients across generations; warm without being
soft. *Risk:* "numbers on a screen" is a mild cliché — the second half rescues it.

### Tier 3 — the safe both-prompts answer

**H. Explicit two-part** *(31 words)*
> "Professionally, I've learned to hold a view and defend it with evidence instead of hedging.
> Personally, I've learned that asking for ten minutes of someone's time is almost never an
> imposition."

*Why it exists:* answers both of Isabel's questions explicitly, which makes it very easy to approve.
*Cost:* the "Professionally / Personally" scaffolding is mechanical and slightly essay-like. Use as
your fallback, not your lead.

### Modular kit — build your own

| Openers (set up the reversal) | Pivots | Closers (land it) |
|---|---|---|
| "I arrived expecting…" | "In fact…" | "…the fastest way to get good at it." |
| "I came in thinking…" | "What I didn't expect was…" | "…usually one more check." |
| "I'd assumed…" | "The most useful thing I've learned is…" | "…knowing when not to." |
| "Before this summer I thought…" | "What actually stuck with me was…" | "…is almost never an imposition." |
| "Nobody here…" | "The biggest shift has been…" | "…someone getting it right." |

**Assembly rule:** one opener + one concrete detail of your own + one closer. If the finished
sentence could have been said by an intern at any firm in the City, it isn't finished.

## 5b. Your two angles: the 220-year-old firm, and the AI work

These are the same observation from opposite ends. **The AI work is your evidence. The
entrepreneurial mindset is your conclusion.** A quote that only asserts the conclusion is a
brochure line; a quote that only describes the tooling is a CV bullet. Fused, they beat everything
in section 5 — nobody else in the corpus has either.

They carry opposite risks, and you should know which is which.

### Angle A — two centuries old, and not set in its ways

> **The risk here is ventriloquism, and it is severe.** Pictet's own innovation page is headed
> *"Timeless values. Unconventional thinking."* The old-firm-that-innovates observation **is already
> their marketing line**, and *entrepreneurial spirit* is a stated corporate value. Say it back to
> them unattached and you have written their copy for them. The only thing that rescues this angle
> is a concrete detail of something that actually changed — which is what Angle B gives you.

**I. Long horizon → appetite for risk** *(29 words — strongest of A)*
> "I'd assumed history made an organisation cautious. What I've learned is that knowing you'll still
> be here in fifty years is what makes it safe to try something new."

*Doesn't just note the paradox — explains it. The long horizon is the cause of the risk appetite,
not a counterweight to it. Demonstrates long-term thinking and entrepreneurial spirit in one breath
without using either phrase.*

**J. Survived every fashion** *(31 words)*
> "A firm that has been around since 1805 has already survived every fashion. I've learned that
> being genuinely open to a new idea and being careful with it are not opposites."

*"Already survived every fashion" is the most quotable phrase in the set. Risk: implies a view on
which ideas are fashions — keep it unspecific.*

**K. Single sentence, Pictet length** *(24 words)*
> "The thing I didn't expect from a 220-year-old institution was how often someone asked me whether
> there was a better way of doing it."

*Shortest option available and the closest in length to Pictet's own published quotes. If Comms
wants something that fits the card at large type, this is it.*

**L. Who actually listened** *(32 words)*
> "Two centuries of history turned out to be a reason to experiment, not a reason not to. The people
> most willing to hear a new idea were usually the most senior."

*The second sentence is the most flattering thing you can say about a partnership without
complimenting anyone.*

### Angle B — the AI work

> **Your genuine differentiator, and the one Comms will scrutinise hardest.** An AI quote on a
> private bank's public channel is read by Risk as well as Comms, so the framing does the work:
> **make it about judgement and people, never about speed or headcount.** Never name a tool, model
> or vendor. Never imply client data went near anything. Never suggest a control was removed.
> "I made the team faster" invites questions; "I learned what shouldn't be automated" gets approved
> on sight — and reads better anyway.

**M. Adoption, not automation** *(30 words — strongest of B)*
> "Automating a task is the easy half. Earning enough trust that someone actually uses what you
> built is the part I hadn't thought about, and the part I'll take with me."

*About people, not technology, which is why it survives on a card. The most credible thing an intern
can say about building tools inside an institution. Zero compliance surface.*

**N. First draft vs. the thinking** *(31 words — strongest of B)*
> "I spent the summer finding places where a machine could do the first draft and a person still had
> to do the thinking. The judgement was never the part worth automating."

*Says "enhance, not replace" without going near that exhausted phrase. "Never the part worth
automating" is the line a caption would pull out.*

**O. Changed nothing about how they decide** *(33 words)*
> "The most useful thing I built this summer saved my team a few hours a week and changed nothing
> about how they made decisions. That's the line I've learned to look for."

*Understatement doing heavy lifting. "Changed nothing about how they made decisions" is exactly what
a risk function wants to hear from someone who touched a workflow.*

**P. Knowing what to leave alone** *(35 words)*
> "I came in assuming the value I could add was technical. The hard part of building something useful
> isn't the building — it's understanding the work well enough to know what to leave alone."

*Expectation-inverted shape plus the AI substance, and it flatters the team's expertise rather than
your own.*

**Q. What someone will stop checking** *(30 words — sharp, higher risk)*
> "The question was never what a model can do — it was what someone is willing to stop checking.
> Most of my summer went on the second question."

*Sharpest observation on the page and the most likely to get engagement. Risk: "stop checking" can be
misread as loosening controls. Soften to "willing to rely on" if your manager is cautious.*

### Angle C — fused, and the best of the three

**R. Careful ≠ closed** *(29 words — lead with this)*
> "The oldest organisation I've worked in was also the one most open to me changing how something was
> done. I've learned not to mistake being careful for being closed."

*Both angles in twenty-nine words, at Pictet's own quote length, closing on a genuine lesson rather
than praise. Quotable, generous, and true of the firm without naming a single value.*

**S. What it should be allowed to touch** *(32 words — lead with this)*
> "I spent the summer building tools inside a firm founded in 1805. The useful question was never what
> the technology could do — it was what it should be allowed to touch."

*The 1805 / new-tools juxtaposition makes the entrepreneurial point silently, then the second sentence
lands the judgement point. "Allowed to touch" is governance-friendly: thoughtful to Risk, confident to
everyone else.*

**T. Expectation inverted, with the detail** *(28 words)*
> "I'd assumed a firm with two centuries behind it would be the last place to change how something was
> done. I spent the summer doing exactly that."

*Cleanest expectation→reality shape available, and the flat second sentence is the whole joke.
Deliberately vague about what changed — if Comms wants specificity, that belongs in the caption.*

**U. Both prompts, both angles** *(32 words — fallback)*
> "Professionally, I've learned to build things that make a team faster without changing how it
> decides. Personally, I've learned that a 220-year-old firm can be the easiest place to suggest
> something new."

*Almost impossible to reject. Also the least memorable of the fused set.*

### The AI vocabulary trap

This vocabulary aged badly and fast. Every phrase below is what a chief executive says on an earnings
call, which is exactly why it cannot appear next to your face:

> enhance not replace · augment not replace · AI-powered · leveraged AI · unlocked efficiencies ·
> human in the loop · game-changer · cutting-edge · transformative · supercharged ·
> the future of finance · disrupt · seamless · robust

**"Enhance, not replace" in particular.** It is the right *idea* — it is also now the single
most-used sentence in corporate AI communication. Keep the thought, discard the words: M, N, O and S
all say it without saying it.

### One real fork: do you use the word "AI" at all?

None of the fused drafts contain it. That is a decision, not an oversight.

| Say "AI" | Don't say it |
|---|---|
| Comms may actively want it — highest-engagement word on LinkedIn, and "220-year-old private bank lets intern rebuild a workflow with AI" is a genuinely good post for them | Routes past a slower approval path, won't date, and describing the work ("the first draft", "the tools I built") is more concrete and better writing than the label |
| The most distinctive thing about your summer, and the reason this card would outperform the rest of the series | In 2029 "I used AI" reads like "I used Excel". The judgement point ages well; the tool label doesn't |

**How to have both:** send a quote that doesn't use the word, and tell Isabel in the email that the
work behind it was building AI tooling for the team — so she can put that in the **caption**, where
Comms controls the framing. You get the distinctiveness without spending your thirty words on a label.

## 6. What to send Isabel

Send **one preferred quote plus one alternative** — not five. Three or more signals you can't
choose; one gives Comms no room and usually results in them editing you. Two is the professional
number.

Draft reply:

> Hi Isabel,
>
> Thank you for thinking of me — I'd be glad to take part.
>
> My internship ends on **[date]**, so the week of 7 September works [/ so I'd need the shoot
> before [date] — any slot that week would work on my side].
>
> My quote, in preference order:
>
> **1.** "[Tier 1 draft]"
>
> **2.** "[alternative]"
>
> Happy to adjust the wording if it's long for the card, or if [manager] would like it framed
> differently. Just let me know the date, time and location once the photographer is confirmed.
>
> Best wishes,
> Janak

Notes on that email:
- **Lead with the end date.** It's the thing Isabel actually needs to action, and it's time-critical
  — the shoot has to be booked before your last day.
- **Offering to shorten it pre-empts the edit.** If Comms trims a quote themselves, they trim for
  space, not for meaning; volunteering makes you the one who controls which half survives.
- **Send it to your manager first, or at least flag it.** Isabel says the quote needs manager
  approval anyway. Giving your manager first sight — "here's what I'm planning to send Comms, happy
  to change it" — costs one message and removes any chance of it coming back edited in a way you
  don't like.
- **Don't thank her twice.** One line of warmth is right; more starts to read like the gratitude
  archetype.

## 7. Checklist before you send

- [ ] Under 40 words, two sentences
- [ ] Contains **one concrete detail** that only you could have written
- [ ] Contains **zero** words from the banned list
- [ ] Contains **zero** Pictet brand vocabulary said out loud (long-term, responsibility,
      entrepreneurial, partnership, independence)
- [ ] Contains **zero** superlatives about Pictet
- [ ] No client, fund, deal, figure, or system named
- [ ] Reads well aloud in your own voice
- [ ] Ends on a strong noun or verb
- [ ] You'd still be happy to see it under your face in 2029
