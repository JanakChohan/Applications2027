PAGE 1 · THE BASIC IDEA

Why do people pay someone to help with their money?

Imagine you're 58. You have £300,000 spread across three old work pensions, an ISA and some savings. You want to retire at 62. You have questions like these:

Most people don't have the time or knowledge to answer these themselves, and a mistake is expensive. So they pay a financial adviser, a qualified professional who looks at their whole situation and tells them what to do.

Key idea Advisers are usually paid a small percentage of the money they look after, every year. For example, 0.8% of £300,000 is £2,400 a year. So the more money a client has, the more the adviser earns. That one fact explains a lot of how SJP works.

Three ways people can manage their money

1. Do it yourself

Use an app or website to pick your own investments. Cheap, but you make all the decisions. Example: AJ Bell, Hargreaves Lansdown.

2. Get advice

A person plans your finances and recommends what to do. Costs more, but you get expertise and someone to call. Example: SJP.

3. Hand it over completely

A manager makes all the investment decisions for you. Usually for wealthier people. Example: Rathbones.

SJP is firmly in option 2. Its whole business is built around advisers giving advice.

PAGE 2 · VOCABULARY

The ten words you need

Learn these ten and you'll understand 90% of any conversation about SJP.

1. Wealth management

Helping individuals plan and look after their money over their whole life.

2. Financial adviser

A qualified person who recommends what to do with your money. At SJP they're called Partners.

3. FUM (Funds Under Management)

The total money a firm looks after for its clients. SJP's is about £240 billion.

4. Fund

A big pot where many people's money is pooled and invested in shares, bonds and so on by a professional manager.

5. Wrapper

The "box" your investments sit in, which decides the tax rules. Examples: pension, ISA, investment bond.

6. Pension

A wrapper for retirement savings. You get tax relief going in, but can't take the money out until your mid-50s.

7. ISA

A wrapper where up to £20,000 a year can be invested and grow tax-free.

8. Inflows / outflows

Gross inflows = new money coming in. Net inflows = new money in minus money taken out.

9. Retention

The share of money that stays with the firm each year. SJP keeps about 95%.

10. FCA

The Financial Conduct Authority, the UK regulator that sets the rules for how firms treat customers.

With most advisers, the client pays several different companies. At SJP, one company owns them all. This is called vertical integration.

PAGE 5 · THE ADVISERS

The Partnership: SJP's advisers

SJP's advisers mostly don't work for SJP as employees. They're self-employed business owners called Partners. Together they're known as "the Partnership".

What the Partner does

Finds clients (mostly through referrals), gives the advice, and owns the relationship. Earns the advice fees. Can grow their business and eventually sell it.

What SJP head office does

Provides the brand, products, funds, technology and training. Checks the advice is good, because SJP is legally responsible for its Partners' advice in the regulator's eyes.

Where new advisers come from: the SJP Academy

SJP runs its own Academy that trains people, often career-changers, to become advisers. More than 89% of people who finish it are still at SJP three years later.

Interview tip Your graduate role is not in the Academy and you won't be a Partner. You'd be in head office, helping the Partners. Section 17 explains this.
Why the model works, and its weak spot Partners are entrepreneurs, so they work hard to win and keep clients, and the business grows. But SJP is responsible for thousands of advisers it doesn't directly manage. That's where problems have come from (see page 14).

PAGE 7 · THE BIG ONE

Why pensions matter so much

Pensions are 55% of SJP's money and 63% of its new money (2025). If you remember one product, remember pensions.

A pension over a lifetime

Four reasons pensions are so important to SJP

1. Retirement got complicated

Since 2015, retirees can keep their pension invested and draw from it ("drawdown") instead of buying a fixed income for life. That means ongoing decisions, so people want advice.

2. People have lots of small pots

Combining old workplace pensions into one is a common, valuable piece of advice.

3. Tax

Pensions get tax relief. From April 2027, unused pensions will count for inheritance tax. That's a big change, and it creates more need for advice.

4. Money stays for decades

Only ≈4% of pension money leaves SJP each year, against ≈7% for ISAs and unit trusts. Long-lasting money means long-lasting fees.

Something you can say "Pensions are over half of SJP's money, and the 2027 inheritance tax change makes retirement planning even more complex. That's a tailwind for advice."

PAGE 10 · PROFIT

How SJP makes its profit

SJP's profit is basically: money looked after × the small slice SJP keeps − costs.

Where SJP's 2025 income came from

After paying staff, offices, technology and other costs, SJP made £462m profit in 2025.

The biggest money-maker Yearly percentage charges on client money, and pensions most of all, because they're the biggest pile and stay the longest. It works like a subscription: once money is in, it pays SJP every year it stays.

The four levers that move profit

1. Stock markets ↑↓

If markets rise 10%, the money looked after rises, and so do the fees. In 2025, market growth added £23.6bn, nearly four times the £6.2bn of new money.

2. Net new money

Money coming in minus money leaving. Winning clients and keeping them.

3. The slice SJP keeps

It fell when fees were simplified in 2025, and is expected to creep back up as older money moves onto the new fees.

4. Costs

SJP is cutting ≈£100m of yearly costs by 2027.

PAGE 14 · THE DIFFICULT PART

What went wrong, and how it was fixed

You must know this story. Interviewers expect you to know it, and to talk about it calmly and fairly.

Problem 1: the exit fee

Clients who moved their money out within 6 years paid up to 6%. Critics said this trapped unhappy clients and hid the real cost. Fixed: scrapped for new money from Aug 2025. Older money still carries it until about 2031.

Problem 2: reviews not delivered

Clients paid a yearly fee for an annual review, but some couldn't be shown to have had one. In Feb 2024 SJP set aside £426m to refund them (covering fees back to 2018). Now: it needed less than expected; £110m was left in mid-2026 and the review should finish in 2026.

Why it came to a head: "Consumer Duty" New FCA rules from July 2023 changed the standard from "did you tell the client?" to "is the client getting fair value, and can you prove it?". Bundled fees and unproven reviews didn't pass that test. Other firms (Quilter, Rathbones) have had similar ongoing-advice issues.
Don't say"The FCA fined SJP £426m." That's wrong. The £426m was SJP's own refund budget, not a fine.
Something you can say"Consumer Duty raised the bar from disclosure to proving fair value. SJP responded by simplifying its fees, removing exit charges and refunding clients, and that review is nearly complete."

WHAT MAKES SJP DIFFERENT · 1 of 4

What makes SJP different (beyond "we give good advice")

Every wealth firm says it has great advice and great people. These are the specific systems SJP has built that rivals mostly don't have, or don't have at this scale.

The big idea that ties them together Most advice firms are small and independent: one firm, a few advisers, and no plan for when the founder retires. SJP has built a complete life-cycle system for advisers. It creates them (Academy), equips them (tools, products, brand), stands behind them (guarantees their advice), and lets them buy and sell client books internally (BSP). So clients and their money stay inside SJP even when individual advisers come and go. That's a big reason retention stays around 95%.

The adviser life-cycle: the Academy and BSP

This is probably SJP's most distinctive piece of structure, and it's exactly the area your division supports.

Why it matters: the UK has an ageing adviser population. SJP manufactures its own replacements instead of competing for scarce experienced advisers.

② Business Sale & Purchase (BSP), since 1992

• An internal marketplace where Partners buy and sell client businesses.
• 5,000th deal completed in March 2026.
• About 250–300 deals a year, worth about £200m a year.

Why it matters: it gives a Partner's business a sale value, which makes them want to stay. And when they retire, clients pass to another SJP adviser instead of leaving.

Why rivals find this hard to copy An independent adviser who retires usually sells to an outside buyer, often a private-equity-backed "consolidator", and clients may move platform or leave. At SJP the buyer is inside the system and SJP finances the deal. It's a succession machine, and it took 30+ years and a huge adviser base to build. The CFO called BSP "a critical part of what we offer our advisers… from recruitment to retirement".
WHAT MAKES SJP DIFFERENT · 3 of 4

Investment model, advice guarantee, back office

③ SJP stands behind its Partners' advice

In SJP's own words (H1 2026): "We guarantee their advice and provide the technical insights to help them understand and adapt their advice for changes in the tax, pension, regulatory and political landscape."

Because SJP is the regulated "principal", clients complain to SJP, and SJP pays any redress. The £426m refund provision is the proof that this is real. A small independent adviser has to carry that risk alone, and its insurance may not cover it. For clients, it means a FTSE 100 company backs the advice. For advisers, it's protection plus a central technical team on tax and pension changes. Your division runs much of this.

④ The "hire and fire" fund manager model

SJP mostly doesn't pick shares itself. It acts like a football manager: it picks outside expert fund managers, gives each a slice of the money, and replaces them if they underperform. Clients never have to move their money, because the fund keeps its SJP name while the managers inside it change.

Real example (2025): SJP switched its £1.9bn Global Smaller Companies fund from one manager to seven, hiring six new managers alongside the existing one. For its new cheaper Polaris Multi-Index range (2025), State Street built 14 index funds, while SJP's in-house team sets the overall mix.

⑥ One shared back office

SJP outsources its administration to the technology firm SS&C, and has done since 2002. All products run on SS&C's Bluedoor platform, which handles £100bn+. Partners don't have to choose or pay for their own platform, compliance systems or fund research, as an independent adviser would. They plug into SJP's.

Note: the Bluedoor details come from SS&C/trade press coverage, around 2019. Check that the current set-up hasn't changed.

PAGE 16 · THE INDUSTRY

What "wealth management" really means

Wealth management means helping a person plan and look after their money across their whole life: growing it, protecting it, drawing on it in retirement, and passing it on.

The advice cycle: what an adviser actually does

Wealth management vs asset management (people mix these up)

Wealth management

Serves people. Asks "what should this person do with their money?" Long relationships. Example: SJP.

Asset management

Makes funds. Asks "how do we make this fund perform?" Sells to millions; never meets most investors. Example: BlackRock.

SJP is mainly a wealth manager. It has an investment arm, but uses outside asset managers to run most of the money.
