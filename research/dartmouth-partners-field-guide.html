How to use this document

You asked for the ins and outs — not the marketing copy. This guide is built in two halves. Parts I to IV explain the machine: what Dartmouth Partners sells, to whom, for how much, and what happens inside the firm between a client’s phone call and a candidate’s offer letter. Parts V and VI turn that understanding into actions you can take this week.

The confidence labels

Recruitment is a deliberately opaque industry. Firms publish polished summaries of their process and almost nothing about their internal mechanics. Rather than blur the line between fact and informed reconstruction, every substantive claim in this document carries one of three labels:

LabelMeaning
ConfirmedStated by Dartmouth Partners or Kernel Global in their own published materials, or recorded in a public register (Companies House), or set out in legislation.

One honest caveat before you start

No one outside the firm has seen Dartmouth’s internal scorecards, its client fee agreements, or its consultant commission plan. Anyone who claims otherwise in a blog post is guessing. What can be established with precision is the shape of the machine, because the shape is dictated by economics and law that apply to every UK recruitment agency, and by a LinkedIn product whose behaviour is publicly documented. That shape is enough to give you the asymmetric advantage you are after.

Part I

What Dartmouth Partners actually is

The corporate anatomy, the ownership, the scale, and the five things people routinely mistake it for.

1.1  The one-paragraph answer

Dartmouth Partners is a specialist recruitment agency — a commercial intermediary whose entire product is access to people. Investment firms, banks, funds and corporates hire it to find, filter and deliver candidates for roles they need to fill. It is paid by those hiring firms, never by candidates. It was launched as a brand in 2012 by Logan Naidu, it specialises in financial and professional services — private equity, credit, M&A, senior finance, investor relations, public markets and adjacent fields — and it deliberately spans the entire career arc, from school-leaver apprenticeships through to chief financial officers. It is headquartered in the City of London with US and Asian offices, employs a few hundred people across its group, and is itself owned by its founder alongside private-equity investors.

The mental model to hold

Do not think of Dartmouth as a careers service that happens to charge companies. Think of it as a market maker in a market with terrible information. Funds cannot easily see which good people are quietly willing to move; good people cannot easily see which funds are quietly hiring. Dartmouth stands in the middle, holds both sets of information privately, and charges the side with money for a match. Everything else — the events, the compensation reports, the campus programmes, the LinkedIn outreach — exists to deepen the two piles of private information it sits on.

1.2  Corporate anatomy: Dartmouth, Kernel, Pure, Catalyst

Understanding the group structure explains behaviour you would otherwise find puzzling — such as why a consultant covering tax roles might have your CV, or why a firm that headhunts private-equity principals also runs school outreach programmes.

Why the group matters to you. Three practical consequences. First, if you register with Dartmouth you are, in effect, registering with a platform that also covers tax, legal, treasury, audit, compliance and HR — so a chartered accountant who tells Dartmouth they are open to industry may find a Pure Search consultant calling. Second, group-level growth targets flow down to desk-level activity targets, which is why outreach volume is high. Third, the group can afford things a small agency cannot: proprietary compensation research, a global events series, a charitable foundation, campus teams. Those are candidate-acquisition assets. When you attend one of their events, you are entering the database.

1.5  What Dartmouth is not

Five misconceptions, each of which leads candidates to behave badly.

  1. Not connected to Dartmouth College. The name is a City naming convention — Naidu’s earlier firm was “The Cornell Partnership”. Ivy-League-sounding names are a deliberate branding trope in UK financial recruitment. There is no academic affiliation.
  2. Not your employer, and not your agent. When they place you, you sign a contract with the client, not with Dartmouth. Their contractual and commercial duty runs to the fee-payer. Their interest in you is real but conditional: it lasts exactly as long as you are placeable. This is not cynicism, it is structure — and knowing it lets you use them correctly instead of feeling betrayed by them.
  3. Not a pure executive-search house. Classic executive search (Heidrick & Struggles, Egon Zehnder, Spencer Stuart) is retained-only, board-level, and paid in thirds regardless of outcome. Dartmouth does offer retained and executive solutions, but the bulk of its volume — certainly at analyst and associate level — is contingent: no placement, no fee. The economics of contingency drive everything you will experience.
  4. Not a careers service. The CV workshops, mock interviews, compensation reports and student network are genuine and useful, and they are also the top of a funnel. Take the help. Understand why it is free.
  5. Not a monopoly gatekeeper. Coverage in London is split across a dozen firms, fund by fund and often mandate by mandate. Dartmouth cannot show you a role it does not hold. Anyone who tells you a single headhunter relationship is sufficient is either lazy or selling something.
Part II

The business model: who pays, and why

Follow the money and every behaviour becomes predictable — the outreach, the urgency, the chasing, the silence.

2.1  The two-sided market

Dartmouth operates a classic two-sided market. On one side sit clients: private equity funds, credit funds, investment banks, asset managers, PE-backed portfolio companies and corporates. On the other sit candidates. Value flows both ways, but cash flows only one way.

2.2  Contingent, retained, interim: the three ways they get paid

Dartmouth publicly offers permanent, interim, executive, retained and succession services. Those collapse into three commercial models, and it is worth knowing which one you are inside, because it changes how hard the consultant will work for you and how much control they have.

ModelHow it worksWhat it means for you
Contingent
The default for analyst → VP		 No fee unless a candidate the agency introduced is hired and starts. Often several agencies compete on the same role. Fee is a percentage of first-year base salary, invoiced on start date, payable typically within 7–30 days, with a rebate if you leave early. Speed is everything. The consultant is racing rivals and racing the client’s own direct applicants. Expect fast turnarounds, pressure to say yes, and a strong preference for candidates who are decisive and interview-ready now.
Retained / executive
Senior, confidential, or scarce		 Client pays in instalments — classically a third on engagement, a third on shortlist delivery, a third on placement — regardless of outcome. The agency is exclusive. Slower, more thorough, more honest. A retained consultant will spend real time with you because they are paid to run a proper process, and they have no rival agency to beat. If you are told a search is retained and exclusive, the consultant genuinely controls access.
Interim / contract
Cover, projects, transformations		 The agency acts as an employment business: it may pay the worker itself and bill the client a day rate with a margin on top, or introduce a contractor for a placement fee. IR35 status and right-to-work checks sit here — which is why their privacy policy collects NI numbers, bank details and payroll data. Different rules apply: you may be paid by Dartmouth rather than the client, and you should always know your margin, your rate, and your IR35 determination before signing.

2.3  A worked fee example, end to end

Say a mid-market private equity fund needs one associate. It calls three agencies, Dartmouth among them, on a contingent basis. Dartmouth maps the market, contacts perhaps sixty relevant people, screens fifteen, submits four, and one is hired at a base salary of £90,000.

The three consequences you can actually use

1. Your base salary is their revenue line. A consultant negotiating your offer upward is not being kind; they are being paid more. This is the rare moment when your interests and theirs align almost perfectly. Use them hard at the offer stage.

2. The rebate makes them care whether you stay. If you resign in week six, the agency may have to refund part or all of the fee. That is why a good consultant will push back on a bad fit — and why they will call you a month after you start.

3. Early-career placements are low-fee, high-volume. A £5,000 fee cannot support a bespoke, hand-held search. Early Careers is therefore a process business: standardised tests, batch screening, 40,000 CVs a year. Being memorable inside a batch process requires different tactics than being memorable in a bespoke one — see §6.1.

2.4  Why a 12-person fund pays £30,000 to hire one associate

This is the question you asked most directly: why do small boutique firms use Dartmouth? The answer is a build-versus-buy calculation that almost always resolves the same way.

Run the arithmetic. A boutique hiring two people a year would pay roughly £36,000–£70,000 in agency fees. An in-house recruiter capable of running buy-side searches costs more than that and sits idle ten months of the year. Below roughly ten to fifteen hires a year, buying is simply cheaper than building — which is exactly the range most boutique funds occupy. Large banks, by contrast, cross that threshold easily, which is why they run enormous internal talent-acquisition teams and use agencies only for niche or senior roles. Inferred

The asymmetry hiding in that table

The reason funds pay is information, not labour. If you make yourself legible and reachable — easy to find, easy to categorise, easy to represent — you make the agency’s product cheaper to produce. Consultants systematically over-represent candidates who are cheap to represent: whose CV needs no explanation, whose motivations are clear, who answer within an hour, who have no messy gaps to justify. That is the whole game, and it is almost entirely within your control.

2.5  Desk economics — and what they mean for you

Dartmouth does not publish consultant targets, and the numbers below are an industry-standard reconstruction rather than internal data. But the shape is reliable, and the shape is what determines whether your email gets answered. Inferred throughout

Four behavioural predictions fall out of this funnel, and you will observe every one of them:

  • Response speed is triaged by placeability, not politeness. A consultant with a live mandate you fit will answer in minutes. The same person will leave a thoughtful message unanswered for three weeks if you fit nothing currently open. This is queue management, not rudeness.
  • They prefer candidates already in process elsewhere. A candidate with two competing offers proves the market values them and creates urgency with the client. Mentioning (truthfully) that you are in another process raises your internal priority.
  • They will ask where else you have applied — and there is a reason. If another agency already submitted you to a fund, Dartmouth cannot claim the fee for that introduction. “Ownership” of a candidate-client pairing is contractual and time-limited. Answer honestly; it protects you as much as them.
  • Consultants change desks and firms often. Recruitment has high turnover. The person who owns your relationship may be gone in eighteen months, which is why you should maintain a relationship with the firm (registered, in the database, on the events list), not only with one individual.

2.6  The legal floor: it is free for you, by statute

UK recruitment operates under the Employment Agencies Act 1973 and the Conduct of Employment Agencies and Employment Businesses Regulations 2003, enforced by the Employment Agency Standards Inspectorate. The provisions worth knowing: Confirmed

  • No fees to work-seekers. An agency must not charge you for finding or attempting to find you work. The narrow statutory exceptions concern entertainment and modelling, not finance. Optional extra services such as CV writing may be charged for, but must be genuinely optional and separately agreed — and no agency may make finding you work conditional on buying them.
  • The fee is a business-to-business matter. The Regulations do not cap what agencies charge employers; that is a commercial negotiation. So there is no published rate card and never will be.
  • Terms must be agreed with you in writing before the agency acts for you, including whether they are acting as an employment agency (permanent) or employment business (temporary/interim).
  • You cannot be charged for, or penalised for, refusing a role. Pressure to accept is a sales tactic, not an obligation.
If something feels wrong

Two escalation routes exist and both are free. For data issues — being contacted about roles you never asked for, or wanting to see and correct your file — use UK GDPR rights and complain to the Information Commissioner’s Office. For conduct issues — fees demanded, your CV sent somewhere without permission, misleading role descriptions — the Employment Agency Standards Inspectorate is the regulator. Sending your CV to a client without your consent is a genuine breach, not a grey area, and it is worth telling any agency early and politely that you expect to approve every submission by name.

4.2  Where candidates come from: the five sourcing channels

When a consultant needs twenty plausible names by Friday, they draw from five pools in a predictable order of cost. Cheapest first, always.

Why this ordering is the single most useful fact in this document

A consultant with a live mandate searches their own database first, because it costs nothing and the records already contain salary, motivation and quality notes that LinkedIn cannot show. Only when the database is thin do they open LinkedIn Recruiter and start paying for InMails and cold conversations.

Which means: the candidate who registered eight months ago, uploaded a clean CV, had a twenty-minute call and updated their record when they changed teams is structurally ahead of an identical candidate who is merely findable on LinkedIn. Everyone optimises LinkedIn. Almost nobody optimises being in the database properly. Do both.

What each layer is really testing

  • Layer 1 (machine). Whether your record contains the literal strings a consultant types. “M&A” is not “Mergers and Acquisitions” to a naive keyword search; “Summer Analyst” is not “Intern”. Redundancy in your own wording is free insurance — write both forms somewhere in your CV and profile.
  • Layer 2 (CV scan). In finance recruitment the scan looks for a small number of hard tokens: university and degree class, A-levels or equivalent (still routinely asked for at junior level in the UK), employer names, exact dates, promotion velocity, and deal or project names. Anything requiring interpretation costs the reader time they do not have, and time is the thing they are minimising.
  • Layer 3 (testing). For early careers this is genuinely mechanical — numerical and verbal reasoning under time pressure, plus a personality instrument. These are practisable and most candidates do not practise. For investment roles, expect technical questions on accounting, valuation and LBO mechanics, and structured competency questions.
  • Layer 4 (the call). The consultant is answering one question: if I put this person in front of my client, do I look good or stupid? Everything they probe — why you want this, what you actually did on that deal, what you earn, what you would accept, when you can start — feeds that judgement.
  • Layer 5 (calibration). Purely internal, and the reason strong candidates get told nothing. A client with a known preference for a particular bank, or a partner who dislikes career-changers, shapes the shortlist invisibly. You cannot win this layer; you can only be so obviously strong that you are hard to leave out.
  • Layer 6 (client CV review). Dartmouth’s own Early Careers process lists this explicitly as a step. Your CV is judged again, by someone with different priorities, often against internal candidates you will never hear about.

Part V

LinkedIn: the machine that finds you

Your colleagues were not discovered by intuition. They were returned by a database query. This part explains the query — and how to be inside it.

5.1  What LinkedIn Recruiter is, and what it can see

LinkedIn Recruiter is a paid enterprise product, entirely distinct from the LinkedIn you use. Its published capabilities: search across LinkedIn’s full member base of roughly a billion profiles, more than forty filters, Boolean keyword search, saved “projects” that function as talent pipelines, bulk InMail with a monthly credit allowance, integration with the agency’s own applicant-tracking system, and “Spotlights” that re-rank results by likelihood of engagement. Reported — LinkedIn product documentation and vendor guides

Three properties of that product matter enormously and are widely misunderstood:

  1. It is a structured search, not a feed. Recruiters are not scrolling and spotting you. They are constructing a query — title, location, employer, seniority, years of experience, skills, school — and receiving a ranked list. If your profile does not satisfy the filters, you do not exist for that search. There is no partial credit.
  2. Roughly nine in ten Recruiter searches use the job-title field. Your current and past job titles are the highest-leverage text on your entire profile. A brilliant “About” section cannot rescue a title field that says “Intern” with no context.
  3. InMail is metered. Recruiter seats come with a fixed monthly credit allowance, and credits are typically returned when a candidate responds. That creates a real, quantifiable incentive to message people likely to reply — which is exactly what Spotlights are for, and why the “open to work” and recency signals in §5.5 are worth more than they appear.

6.1  Getting into the database on purpose

Being findable on LinkedIn gets you into channel #2. Being in the database gets you into channel #1 — the one they search first, and the one that persists whether or not a particular consultant ever sees your profile. There are four doors, and they are not equivalent.

  2. Apply to one genuinely relevant live role. Their board carries a few dozen open roles across sectors and cities.   3. Email the right desk directly, once, well. Sector inboxes are published (Appendix A). Do not attach a cover letter. Do not email eleven desks.
  4. Turn up to something. Events and campus programmes are candidate-acquisition channels — which is precisely why attending puts you in the system with a human association attached. See §6.5.

6.3  Tiering — the uncomfortable truth

Headhunters sort candidates. Not formally, not published, but consistently: the strongest are shown the best mandates first, and everyone else is shown what remains. Reported practice in London is that this sorting happens fast, largely on the first call, and that it is durable.

Three things actually move you up a tier, and none of them is charm:

  • A change in your objective facts — promotion, a marquee deal closing, a qualification passed, a language reaching fluency, a move to a better-regarded firm. Tell them when it happens; records go stale silently.
  • Demonstrated market realism. Candidates who know what they are worth and what they will accept are cheap to place. Candidates chasing a number the market does not pay are expensive, and get quietly deprioritised without ever being told why.
  • Reliability. Turning up, replying fast, not pulling out late, not renegotiating after accepting. One late withdrawal costs a consultant a client relationship, and they remember for years.

6.7  Twelve mistakes that get you quietly deprioritised

  1. Treating the first call as informal. It is the interview that sets your tier.
  2. Saying “I’m open to anything.” It reads as no thesis, and a candidate with no thesis is hard to pitch.
  3. Inflating deal involvement. Verified constantly; unrecoverable when caught.
  4. Being vague about compensation. Makes you unpriceable, which makes you unsubmittable.
  5. Slow replies. In a contingent race, a 48-hour reply can cost you the process outright.
  6. Going silent mid-process. The single fastest route to Tier 4.
  7. Withdrawing after a final round, or accepting then reneging. Costs the consultant a client; you will not be represented again.
  8. Using a counter-offer as leverage. Legitimate to accept a counter-offer — but say so early. Discovering it at the offer stage burns the relationship.
  9. Emailing every desk with the same message. Marks you as unfocused before anyone has read your CV.
  10. A CV that requires interpretation. One page at junior level, exact dates, grades, deal names, numbers.
  11. Never updating them. A record that says “first-year analyst, 2024” in 2026 will be searched as a first-year analyst.
  12. Expecting them to work for you. They work for the client. Your leverage is being the easiest strong candidate to sell — not appealing to loyalty that structurally does not exist.

Two honest limitations

Forum evidence cuts both ways. Candidate forums contain both praise and sharp criticism of Dartmouth — disorganised processes, patchy feedback — as they do for every large agency. Appendix F — Glossary

ATS / CRM. Applicant tracking system / candidate relationship management — the database where your record lives. Dartmouth’s is called AppTracker.

Buy-side. Firms that invest capital — PE funds, credit funds, hedge funds, asset managers. As opposed to the sell-side (banks and advisers).

Contingent search. No placement, no fee. The dominant model at junior and mid level, and the source of most recruiter behaviour.

Datasheet. A standardised candidate summary a headhunter compiles — background, grades, deals, preferences — and circulates to clients. Central to US on-cycle; used less formally in London.

Desk. A team covering one sector or niche. Dartmouth publishes eleven.

Employment agency vs employment business. Legal distinction: an agency introduces candidates for permanent roles; an employment business supplies temporary workers it pays itself. Different obligations apply.

Exclusivity. A client commitment to use one agency for a search — usually accompanying a retained fee.

InMail. LinkedIn’s paid direct message to someone you are not connected to. Metered, which is why recruiters target likely responders.

Longlist / shortlist. Everyone plausible (dozens to hundreds) versus the three to six CVs actually submitted to the client.

Mandate. An instruction from a client to fill a role. Everything in an agency revolves around live mandates.

Market map. A systematic list of every person at every relevant firm who could do the job — the research artefact underpinning a search.

Off-cycle. Hiring that happens whenever a seat opens, rather than in a synchronised annual process. The London norm on the buy-side.

On-cycle. The compressed, synchronised US private-equity associate recruiting process.

PSL. Preferred supplier list — the small set of agencies a large client permits to work on its roles.

Rebate / clawback. Contractual refund of part of the fee if the placed candidate leaves within a defined window, typically 8–12 weeks.

Retained search. Fee paid in instalments regardless of outcome, in exchange for exclusivity and a committed process.

Spotlight. A LinkedIn Recruiter re-ranking signal — open to work, recently active, connected to the firm, following it, or previously in its ATS.

360 / 180 desk. Whether one consultant does both business development and candidate delivery (360), or the two are split between specialists (180).

SAR. Subject access request — your statutory right to obtain the personal data an organisation holds about you.
