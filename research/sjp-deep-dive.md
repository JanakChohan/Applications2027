## 1. The business model

### What SJP actually is

SJP is the UK's largest wealth management business, measured by number of advisers and by money managed. It is listed on the London Stock Exchange (ticker STJ) and has its head office in Cirencester.

At 30 June 2026 it looked after **£240.8bn of funds under management** for **1,064,000 clients**, through **4,951 advisers** (all PRIMARY, H1 2026 results).

- **Funds under management (FUM):** the total value of client money a firm looks after.
- **What SJP is not:** it is not mainly a stock-picker, and it is not a bank. It is best understood as **a distribution and advice business that owns the products and funds its advisers recommend.**

### The Partnership: self-employed advisers

SJP employs relatively few of the people who give the advice.

- **Who the advisers are:** most are **self-employed "Partners"**. They run their own advice businesses, often small practices with a few advisers and support staff, under the SJP brand.
- **Regulatory set-up:** legally, Partners are **appointed representatives** of SJP's regulated advice company. An appointed representative is a firm that is allowed to give regulated advice because a larger, FCA-authorised firm (the "principal") takes regulatory responsibility for it. So SJP is answerable to the FCA for its Partners' advice, but the Partners own their client relationships and business economics.
- **How Partners are paid:** from the advice charges clients pay. Partners can build up the value of their business over time and sell it. SJP also lends to Partners to fund practice purchases; it reports a provision against "business loans to Partners" (PRIMARY, FY2025 accounts).
- **New advisers:** SJP's **Academy** trains career-changers into advisers. More than 89% of people who complete the Academy are still with SJP three years later (PRIMARY, FY2024 release).
- **Why it matters [Interpretation]:** the Partners are entrepreneurs, so the model grows fast and the incentives to win clients are strong. The same incentives are where the conduct risks come from. The FCA holds SJP responsible for thousands of advisers whose day-to-day work it does not directly manage.

**Note on headcount:** SJP reports **4,934 advisers at end-2025** (PRIMARY). An industry survey (ISS Market Intelligence, reported by Professional Adviser, Feb 2026) puts it at **5,153**. The definitions differ, so use SJP's figure and say "around 5,000 advisers". I could not verify a current count of Partner *businesses* (as opposed to individual advisers), so **DON'T QUOTE** a figure for that.

### "Restricted" versus "independent" advice

These are FCA labels.

- **Independent advice** must be based on a "comprehensive and fair analysis of the relevant market" and be "unbiased and unrestricted". An independent financial adviser (IFA) can in principle recommend any suitable product from any provider.
- **Restricted advice** is everything else. The adviser recommends from a limited range of products or providers.
- **Where SJP sits:** SJP advisers are restricted. They mostly recommend SJP's own wrappers and SJP's own funds.
- **The criticism:** the client never sees a whole-of-market comparison.
- **SJP's answer:** it researches the market for you and picks and monitors the fund managers on your behalf.

"Restricted" is legal and common; Quilter's own advisers are also restricted. It does mean the adviser has a structural conflict of interest, which has to be managed rather than eliminated.

### Vertical integration: advice, product, platform and funds under one roof

**Vertical integration** means one group owns several stages of the value chain that a client pays for. A typical UK client with an IFA pays four separate parties:

| Layer | What it is | Typical independent route | SJP route |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Advice** | Financial planning, recommending what to do | An IFA firm | SJP Partner (appointed representative of SJP) |
| **Wrapper / product** | The tax "container": ISA, pension (SIPP), investment bond, unit trust | Provider or platform | SJP's own life company (bonds, pensions) and SJP's unit trust company (ISAs, unit trusts) |
| **Platform / administration** | Holds the assets, does the dealing and paperwork | A platform such as Transact or Quilter | SJP's own proposition. The back-office work is outsourced to a third-party administrator that SJP pays a "policy servicing tariff" (PRIMARY) |
| **Fund management** | Investing the money | Any fund house | SJP's own funds (e.g. the **Polaris** range, £94bn at end-2025, PRIMARY). SJP's investment team chooses and oversees **external fund managers** to run the money, a "manager of managers" model (PRIMARY: "investment management fees paid to external fund managers") |

**[Interpretation]** Owning every layer lets SJP:

1. Capture a margin at each layer.
2. Make the experience seamless for the adviser.
3. Keep clients "sticky", because moving means unwinding several products at once.

The cost is that it is hard for clients to compare prices, and that comparability is exactly what regulators have pushed on.

### Who the typical client is and what they're worth

- **Average FUM per client:** £240.8bn ÷ 1.064m clients ≈ **£226,000** (derived from PRIMARY figures). This is a mean, so the median client is probably smaller.
- **[Interpretation]** The core client is "mass affluent" to "high net worth" (HNW):
  - professionals, business owners and pre-retirees or retirees;
  - typically £100k to a few £m of investable assets;
  - who want someone to handle pensions, ISAs, inheritance tax planning, protection and retirement income for them.
- **Compared with an ultra-high-net-worth (UHNW) private bank:** UHNW is conventionally US$30m+ of net worth. Private banks such as Coutts, JP Morgan Private Bank and UBS serve fewer, much wealthier clients. They offer lending, complex structuring, bespoke portfolios, alternatives and family-office services, at fees in percentage terms often lower than SJP's because the pots are so large.
- **SJP's model is the opposite:** high volume, relationship-driven, advice-first, standardised in-house fund range. Saying "SJP is a private bank" in an interview would be a mistake (see the end).

## 2. How it makes money

### The current charging structure (in force since 26 August 2025)

All PRIMARY, from SJP's announcement of the new charges and its FY2025 results.

**1. Initial advice charge** (one-off, on new money):
- 3% on the first £250,000
- 2% on the next £250,000
- 1% above £500,000
- Capped at **£30,000** (REPORTED, per SJP charges pages as quoted by MoneyWeek and others)

**2. Ongoing advice charge:** **0.80% a year** of the investment's value. Under the old structure it was 0.5% (REPORTED).

**3. Ongoing product charge** (the wrapper and platform layer), tiered across the client's whole SJP portfolio:

| Portfolio value | Investment bonds and pensions | ISAs and unit trusts |
|---|---|---|
| First £500k | 0.35% | 0.27% |
| Next £500k | 0.32% | 0.24% |
| Next £1m | 0.29% | 0.21% |
| Next £1m | 0.27% | 0.19% |
| Over £3m | 0.25% | 0.17% |

**4. Fund charge:** this varies by fund. For **Polaris 3**, a commonly used mid-risk fund, it is **0.52%**. SJP gives the total ongoing cost for that fund as **1.59% (ISA)** or **1.67% (pension)** (REPORTED, from SJP's charges material as quoted in the press; single example, so check the current figure on sjp.co.uk).

**5. Early withdrawal charge (EWC):** **abolished for new investments.** It still applies to bonds and pensions taken out before the change (see section 3).

Charges not covered here: transaction costs inside funds, discretionary fund management (DFM, where a manager runs a bespoke portfolio for you), and other services.

### Worked example: a client investing £500,000 into an ISA/unit trust in Polaris 3

| Charge | Calculation | £ per year |
|---|---|---|
| Initial advice (year 1 only) | 3% × £250k + 2% × £250k | **£12,500** (2.5%) |
| Ongoing advice | 0.80% × £500k | £4,000 |
| Product (ISA/unit trust tier 1) | 0.27% × £500k | £1,350 |
| Fund (Polaris 3) | 0.52% × £500k | £2,600 |
| **Ongoing total** | **1.59%** | **£7,950 a year** |
| **Year 1 all-in** | | **£20,450** (4.1%) |

- **Same money in a pension or bond:** the product charge is 0.35%, so the ongoing total is 1.67%, or **£8,350 a year**.
- **Over 10 years:** at a flat £500k value, that's about £12.5k up front plus about £79.5k of ongoing charges, roughly **£92k**. In reality the pot grows and the charges grow with it, because they are percentages.

### Who captures each layer

| Layer | Who mainly receives it | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Initial advice charge | Mostly the **Partner**. SJP says initial advice charges "broadly offset new business-related expenses", which include "payments to Partners", so SJP itself makes roughly **zero** on new business under the new structure and expects this to continue. | PRIMARY |
| Ongoing advice charge (0.8%) | Mostly the **Partner**. Trade press reports that under the new structure SJP retains a share of the recurring advice fee for the first time. The split is not public. | REPORTED (split UNVERIFIED) |
| Product charge | **SJP** (the product provider), which pays the third-party administrator out of it | PRIMARY (structure) |
| Fund charge | Split between **SJP** (its fund management margin) and the **external fund managers** it hires | PRIMARY (structure) |

- **What SJP keeps overall:** SJP reports a **net income from FUM margin** (what it keeps after paying Partners, external managers and the administrator).
  - **Old structure:** **0.54% to 0.56%** of mature FUM.
  - **New structure:** **0.43% to 0.45%** (PRIMARY, FY2025).
- **Rough split on the £500k example [Interpretation]:**
  - SJP keeps roughly **£2,150 to £2,250 a year**.
  - The Partner gets most of the **£4,000** advice fee.
  - External fund managers and the administrator take the rest, roughly £1,500 to £1,800.
  - This is an estimate built on SJP's margin guidance. SJP does not publish a per-client split.
- **2026 guidance:** SJP moved to a new "adjusted IFRS" reporting basis in 2026. On that basis its guidance is **47 to 49 basis points of average FUM**, with an expected rise of about 3 bps a year to 2031 (REPORTED, from H1 2026 results coverage). This is **a different basis** from the 0.43–0.45% figure, so don't compare the two numbers directly. A basis point (bp) is 0.01%.

### What changed in the fee restructure, when, and why

**The old structure (pre-26 August 2025), simplified (REPORTED, plus PRIMARY on gestation):**

- **Initial charges** of up to about 4.5% on new investments. On bonds and pensions there was no explicit up-front fee. Instead the cost was recovered over time.
- **"Gestation"** (PRIMARY): for the **first six years** after a bond or pension investment, SJP earned little or no ongoing product margin. That money only became profitable "mature FUM" in year 7. SJP still had **£52.9bn of FUM in gestation at end-2025** (PRIMARY), which stays on the old terms until it matures.
- **The early withdrawal charge (EWC):** to make sure SJP recovered its up-front costs, a client leaving within six years paid an exit charge. It started at **6% in year one and fell by 1 percentage point a year to zero after year six** (REPORTED, Trustnet, Telegraph, MoneyHelpDesk).
- **Charges bundled together:** it was hard for a client to see what they were paying for advice versus the product versus the fund.

**The new structure (from 26 August 2025, PRIMARY):**

- Charges are **unbundled**. Advice, product and fund charges are shown separately, and all ongoing charges apply **from day one**.
- **No EWC** on new money.
- **Tiered** initial and product charges, so large clients pay less in percentage terms.
- **Fund charges reduced:** SJP says almost 95% of its funds with an IA peer group comparison rank below average on cost (PRIMARY, SJP's claim). The IA is the Investment Association, the UK fund industry's trade body.
- **Cost to implement:** **£119.4m post-tax** in total (PRIMARY).

**Timeline:**

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| Oct 2023 | The FT reports regulatory pressure over "opaque and expensive charges" and exit penalties; shares fall ~15% that day (REPORTED). SJP then announces it will scrap EWCs on new business and overhaul charges. |
| Dec 2023 | Mark FitzPatrick (ex-Prudential) becomes CEO, succeeding Andrew Croft (REPORTED). |
| Jul 2024 | FitzPatrick sets out the "Strengthen" strategy (2024–26) to make SJP "simpler, more efficient, and more transparent", alongside a ~£100m cost-reduction programme (PRIMARY). |
| 26 Aug 2025 | The new structure goes live (PRIMARY). |

**Why the change happened:**

- **(a) Consumer Duty and its "fair value" test**, which made opaque, back-loaded charges hard to defend (see section 3).
- **(b) Press and regulator scrutiny.**
- **(c) Comparability:** SJP itself calls it the "simple, comparable charging structure".
- **[Interpretation]** It was also commercially necessary. A business whose biggest cost to clients was hidden in exit fees was exposed to both regulatory and reputational risk.

**The trade-off for shareholders (PRIMARY):**

- **Short term:** lower profit, because margins on new and mature business both fell. SJP calls this a "dip in profitability" in 2025–26.
- **Medium term:** faster profit growth, because new business is profitable from day one and the £52.9bn gestation book matures with "no additional associated expenses".
- **Ambition:** SJP aims to **double the Underlying cash result between 2023 and 2030** (PRIMARY).

## 3. The difficult part

### a) The early withdrawal charge (EWC)

**What it was:** the charge of up to 6% (falling 1 point a year) described in section 2.

**Why it was criticised (REPORTED):**

- **It locked clients in.** A client unhappy with their adviser, or with poor fund performance, faced a penalty of thousands of pounds to leave. That blunts competition, because the threat of losing clients is what normally disciplines a provider.
- **It hid the real cost of advice.** Bonds and pensions had no visible up-front fee, so clients could believe advice was "free" when the cost was being recovered through ongoing charges and the exit fee.
- **It looked out of line with peers.** Most of the advised market had moved to transparent, unbundled fees after the 2013 **Retail Distribution Review (RDR)**, which banned commission on new investment advice. Critics argued SJP's bundled model kept a pre-RDR economic shape. **[Interpretation]**: SJP's structure was compliant, so this is a "spirit, not letter" criticism.
- **The Ombudsman has generally upheld the charge.** The **Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS)** is the free, independent body that settles consumer complaints against financial firms. It has generally upheld the EWC where it was clearly disclosed (REPORTED, FOS decisions). The legal case was weaker than the reputational one.

**Status:** scrapped for new investments from 26 August 2025. **Still outstanding:** pre-August-2025 bond and pension money remains subject to the EWC until it runs off, which takes up to six years, so until around 2031 (PRIMARY: existing clients move to the new charges "once they come to the end of their early withdrawal charge period").

### b) The £426m provision (February 2024)

**What happened (PRIMARY and REPORTED):**

- **28 February 2024, FY2023 results:** SJP made a **£426m pre-tax provision**. A provision is money set aside in the accounts for a likely future cost.
- **What it was for:** to refund clients who had paid **ongoing advice charges** (the annual fee for an annual review and ongoing service) **since the start of 2018** where SJP could not show sufficient evidence the service had actually been delivered.
- **Why:** SJP said it followed a **significant rise in complaints** in late 2023, many driven by press coverage and **claims management companies** (firms that solicit and file complaints on consumers' behalf for a fee).
- **Same day:**
  - The dividend was cut from **52.78p (2022) to 23.83p (2023)** (REPORTED; the 23.83p is PRIMARY).
  - The post-tax cash result fell sharply.
  - SJP warned of lower future payouts.
  - The shares fell **around 30%** to their lowest level in more than a decade (REPORTED).
- **Formal name:** SJP now calls it the **Ongoing Service Evidence (OSE)** review.
- **Recovering costs from Partners:** SJP has said it will recover part of the cost from Partners depending on circumstances (REPORTED).

**How the provision has moved (PRIMARY, FY2025 accounts note 9, and H1 2026):**

| Point | Balance | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| Recognised (1 Jan 2024) | £426.0m | |
| 31 Dec 2024 | £425.1m | £18.5m used, plus discounting adjustment |
| 31 Dec 2025 | £272.3m | £52.5m used; **£109.5m released** (no longer needed) |
| 30 Jun 2026 | **£110.0m** | **£110.4m further released** (REPORTED from H1 2026 coverage; Money Marketing headline "SJP cuts ongoing-advice provision to £110m") |

- **Why the releases happened:** (a) a **revised redress methodology** aligned to **new FCA industry guidance on ongoing advice issued in February 2025**, and (b) experience from actually doing the reviews (PRIMARY).
- **Where the released money goes:** SJP is returning released money to shareholders through buybacks:
  - £63.4m (H1 2025)
  - £18.7m (FY2025)
  - £82.8m (H1 2026)
  - (all PRIMARY)
- **Scale of the review:** 77 million documents have been sourced for it (REPORTED).
- **[Interpretation]** Of the original £426m, only around £70m had actually been used by end-2025. Roughly £220m was released in 2025 and H1 2026 combined. The original estimate now looks substantially too high, but the final cost won't be known until the review completes.

**DON'T QUOTE:** some press cite "£430m" or "£430.6m" for the redress bill. SJP's own accounts say £426.0m. Use **£426m**.

### c) Consumer Duty and what the FCA actually required

- **What Consumer Duty is:** an FCA rule set (Principle 12 plus detailed rules). It applied to products on sale from **31 July 2023** and to closed or legacy products from **31 July 2024**. Firms must deliver **good outcomes** for retail customers across four areas:
  1. products and services;
  2. **price and value** (is the price reasonable for what the customer gets?);
  3. consumer understanding;
  4. consumer support.
- **Why it raised the bar:** the old standard was "was it disclosed?". The new one is "is it fair value, and can you prove it?". That is why both SJP's bundled charges and charging for unevidenced annual reviews became exposed.
- **What the FCA did (REPORTED):** in early 2024 the FCA wrote to around 20 of the largest advice firms asking whether clients paying ongoing advice fees were receiving the service. Sources say "20" or "22". It later published findings and, in **February 2025, industry guidance on ongoing advice services**, which SJP used to set its refund method (PRIMARY: SJP Annual Report 2025).
- **What I did *not* find:** any **FCA enforcement action, fine or formally imposed redress scheme against SJP**. SJP's review is presented as **firm-led**, carried out under Consumer Duty and in the context of FCA supervision.
  - **Careful phrasing:** say "SJP launched a review and provision after complaints rose and the FCA scrutinised the industry". **Don't** say "the FCA fined SJP" or "the FCA ordered SJP to repay £426m".
  - Check the FCA website before stating anything stronger.
- **It's industry-wide, not just SJP (REPORTED):**
  - Quilter had a "skilled person" appointed (an independent reviewer the FCA can require a firm to appoint) to assess ongoing advice in its network.
  - Rathbones has flagged about £60m of FCA-related remediation costs.

### d) What's resolved and what's outstanding

**Resolved:**

- The new charging structure is live, and EWCs are gone for new business.
- Most of the provision has been used or released.
- SJP expects the OSE review to complete **during 2026** (PRIMARY).
- The dividend policy has been reset, and returns are rising again (see section 4).

**Outstanding:**

1. **Final completion of the OSE review** and its final cost; £110m remained at June 2026.
2. **Legacy EWC book:** pre-Aug-2025 bonds and pensions still carry exit charges until they run off, up to 2031.
3. **Complaints:** FOS complaints about SJP rose sharply, to **1,426 in H1 2025** against 485 in H1 2024 (REPORTED, International Adviser/FOS data). Claims management firms remain active. **DON'T QUOTE** other complaint totals; sources mix complaints made to SJP with complaints to FOS.
4. **Ongoing Consumer Duty fair-value scrutiny:** this is continuous, not a one-off test.
5. **FCA's planned simplification of advice rules,** including how often ongoing reviews must happen. A consultation was reported in spring 2026 with final rules expected late 2026 or early 2027 (UNVERIFIED reference number; check the FCA site).

### The strongest criticism

> SJP built a high-cost, vertically integrated model whose economics depended on clients not being able to see, compare, or easily escape its charges. Exit penalties locked people in, bundled fees disguised the price of advice, and an incentive structure rewarding asset-gathering produced a culture where thousands of clients paid for annual reviews they didn't get. SJP reformed only after press exposure, a new regulatory regime (Consumer Duty), and a collapse in its share price forced it to. Even now, a ~1.6–1.7% all-in annual cost (plus up-front fees) is expensive against platforms, robo-advisers and lower-cost advisers. The advice is restricted to SJP's own products, so the client never sees what they could have had elsewhere. The provision releases are going to shareholders, and the legacy exit-fee book will run until around 2031.

### The strongest defence

> SJP's structure was compliant and disclosed; the FOS has generally upheld the EWC. What clients buy is a long-term relationship and holistic planning (pensions, IHT, protection, retirement income), not a fund. Behaviour shows they value it: FUM retention stayed around 95% straight through the worst of the crisis, and gross inflows *rose* 19% in 2025 to £21.9bn. When problems surfaced SJP acted: it provisioned conservatively, the provision turned out larger than needed, and it scrapped EWCs. It also rebuilt its pricing into one of the clearest unbundled tariffs in the market, with tiering that cuts costs for larger clients and fund charges SJP says are below the peer average. Ongoing-advice evidence problems have surfaced at Quilter, Rathbones and others, so this was an industry issue that SJP, as the biggest firm, confronted first and at the largest scale. And the UK has a large "advice gap": far more people need advice than receive it.

**[Interpretation]** Both are partly true. The criticism is strongest on *history*: the lock-in and opacity were real, and reform was reactive. The defence is strongest on *client behaviour since*, through retention and gross inflows.

**Why the gap between gross and net matters [Interpretation, built on PRIMARY data]:**

- **What 95% retention means:** at ~95% retention, about 5% of FUM walks out every year, which is **£11bn+ a year** at today's size. SJP has to raise about £11bn of new money just to stand still.
- **The 2025 picture:** in 2025, £21.9bn gross became only £6.2bn net, so roughly **70% of gross inflows were offset by outflows**.
- **Q3 2026:** net inflows fell to £0.57bn because surrenders rose (£3.08bn in the quarter against £2.65bn a year earlier, REPORTED). SJP blamed a strong comparison quarter, boosted by clients investing ahead of the charging change.
- **SJP's defence:** much of the outflow is natural. An older client base draws income in retirement, and clients withdrew tax-free cash ahead of Budget speculation in late 2025 (PRIMARY: SJP said this hit late-2025 retention).
- **The critics' point:** a 2.5–3% net flow rate is low for a firm with ~5,000 advisers when platforms are growing at ~9%+ (see section 5).
- **Where FUM growth comes from:** **mostly markets, not flows.** In 2025, investment returns added ~£23.6bn (12% of opening FUM) against £6.2bn of net inflows (PRIMARY). In H1 2026, returns added £18.1bn against £2.7bn net inflows (PRIMARY).

**What is SJP's actual advantage? [Interpretation]**

1. **Distribution scale.** About 5,000 advisers is the biggest face-to-face advice force in the UK, more than double the nearest restricted network. Clients typically come by **referral** from existing clients and professional contacts, which is cheap and trusted.
2. **Relationship stickiness.** About 95% retention through a conduct crisis and a share-price collapse is strong evidence that clients are loyal to *their adviser*.
3. **Adviser pipeline and economics.** The Academy manufactures new advisers, and Partners can build and sell valuable businesses. That attracts entrepreneurial advisers and keeps them. The CEO has cited partner retention of about 90% (REPORTED, Citywire via secondary).
4. **Brand and scale economics** in technology, investment oversight, compliance and marketing that a small IFA can't match.

**Is it durable? [Interpretation]**

- **The distribution and relationship advantage is durable.** Human advice for complex life events (retirement, inheritance, selling a business) is hard to commoditise, and the UK advice gap is large.
- **The pricing advantage is not.** Historically SJP earned above-market margins partly because pricing was opaque. Full transparency removes that, which is why margins have been reset down. The moat is now **the advisers, not the products**.
- **The main long-term risk is adviser defection.** If top Partners can earn more as independents (taking clients to cheaper platforms), the integrated model's weakest link is its strongest asset.

## 6. The structural question: does advice-led vertical integration survive full fee transparency?

### Bull case

1. **Clients stayed when the price became visible.** After the charging change (Aug 2025), H1 2026 gross inflows held at £10.5bn and retention *rose* to 95.4% (PRIMARY). The fee shock did not trigger an exodus.
2. **Demand for advice is structural.** Several things push more people toward advice:
   - an ageing population;
   - pension freedoms, which mean retirees must manage drawdown themselves;
   - frozen tax thresholds;
   - **pensions coming into inheritance tax from April 2027** (REPORTED; announced in the October 2024 Budget).

   SJP calls advice "under-penetrated in the UK" (PRIMARY).
3. **A visible earnings recovery.** £52.9bn of gestation FUM matures into full margin over six years with no extra cost (PRIMARY). New business is profitable from day one. The £100m cost programme lands by 2027. Management expects "sharply accelerating earnings from 2027" (REPORTED) and targets doubling the 2023 Underlying cash result by 2030 (PRIMARY).
4. **Transparent pricing is now defensible.** At ~1.6–1.7% all-in for a full advice, wrapper and fund package, SJP is within the range of the advised market. Tiering makes it more competitive for larger clients, its core.
5. **Integration helps with Consumer Duty.** One firm controlling advice, product and fund can evidence outcomes end to end, which is harder in a fragmented chain.

### Bear case

1. **Net flows are weak relative to scale.** A ~2.5% annualised net flow rate (H1 2026) and £0.57bn in Q3 2026, against ~9% at Quilter and AJ Bell (PRIMARY and REPORTED), suggests SJP is struggling to grow its share. FUM growth has been mostly market-driven.
2. **Transparency squeezes margins.** SJP's net margin on mature FUM fell from 0.54–0.56% to 0.43–0.45% (PRIMARY). Once clients can compare, the fund and product layers face constant price pressure from passive funds and platforms. SJP's own launch of a cheaper Polaris Multi-Index range (passive-based, >£1bn FUM, PRIMARY) is arguably an admission of this.
3. **Disruption at the lower end.** The FCA's **targeted support** regime (live **6 April 2026**, REPORTED) lets firms give semi-personalised suggestions without full advice. AI tools may automate the planning tasks advisers charge for. The Feb 2026 sell-off shows investors take this seriously.
4. **Conduct and complaints tail.** The legacy EWC book runs to ~2031. FOS complaints rose sharply in 2024–25. Consumer Duty fair-value tests are ongoing. The restricted model is a permanent conflict the FCA will keep scrutinising.
5. **Adviser economics.** Changes to how Partners are paid, with SJP now taking a share of the recurring advice fee (REPORTED), could make independence or rival networks more attractive to the best advisers.

### Which is more convincing? [Interpretation: my view]

I find the bull case **more convincing on survival** and the bear case **more convincing on returns**.

- **The evidence that matters most is client behaviour after transparency arrived.** Retention around 95% and flat gross inflows through 2025–26 suggest clients buy the adviser relationship, not the product wrapper. So the integrated, advice-led model survives transparency.
- **It survives as a lower-margin, volume-driven business,** where profit growth depends on maturing legacy FUM, cost cuts and keeping advisers, not on pricing power.
- **The real test is 2027–28,** SJP's "Amplify" phase. Can it lift net flows from ~2.5–3% toward 4–5% of FUM with transparent prices, while holding margin guidance and stopping its best advisers from leaving?
- **The single metric to watch is net inflows as a % of opening FUM.** If that keeps falling while competitors grow, the bear case wins.

## Five things to say in an interview

1. **"SJP's real asset is its distribution: about 5,000 advisers and roughly 95% client retention. The products sit behind that."** This shows you understand that the moat is the Partnership and the relationship, not fund performance.
2. **"The 2025 charging change swapped higher short-term margin for cleaner, comparable pricing. New business is profitable from day one, and the six-year gestation book unwinds over time, which is why management expects earnings to accelerate from 2027."** This shows you understand the mechanics, not just the headline.
3. **"Consumer Duty moved the standard from 'was it disclosed?' to 'is it fair value, and can you evidence it?'. That's what exposed charging for annual reviews that weren't delivered. It's an industry-wide issue, but SJP was the biggest and went first."** This is balanced, accurate and shows regulatory awareness.
4. **"The number I'd watch is net inflows as a percentage of opening FUM. Gross inflows are strong, but with 95% retention SJP has to raise about £11bn a year just to stand still."** This shows commercial and analytical maturity.
5. **"The structural tailwinds for advice are real: pension freedoms, frozen thresholds, and pensions coming into inheritance tax from 2027. I'm drawn to SJP because the job is the long-term planning relationship, and the Academy route builds that from day one."** This links the firm's strategy to why you want the role. Check the IHT timing again before saying it.

## Five things to avoid saying, and why

1. **"SJP is a private bank" or "SJP serves ultra-high-net-worth clients."** It doesn't. The average client has about £226k with SJP, and the model is mass-affluent to HNW advice. Saying this shows you haven't done the basics.
2. **"The FCA fined SJP £426m."** False. The £426m was SJP's own *provision* for refunds, much of which has since been released. I found no FCA fine on this. Precision on this point is a credibility test.
3. **"SJP's advisers are independent."** They are **restricted**. Getting this wrong suggests you don't understand UK advice regulation or SJP's model.
4. **Dismissing the controversy ("that's all in the past") or dwelling on it.** Interviewers expect you to know about it. The strong answer acknowledges it, explains what changed (charges, EWC, review), and points to what's still being worked through. Either extreme reads badly.
5. **Quoting precise contested numbers** such as the exact AI sell-off percentage, a precise adviser headcount, today's share price, or "£430m". Sources conflict on these. Use ranges or SJP's own figures. One wrong precise number costs more than an accurate approximation.
