# PART A: THE BUSINESS

## A1. SJP in one page

**One sentence:** SJP is a **financial advice business that also owns the products and funds its advisers recommend**. It makes money by charging an annual percentage on every pound its clients invest with it.

| | |
|---|---|
| Funds under management (FUM) | **£240.8bn** at 30 June 2026 (PRIMARY); £242.4bn at 30 Sept 2026 (REPORTED) |
| Clients | **1,064,000** (PRIMARY, H1 2026) |
| Advisers | **4,951**, almost all self-employed **"Partners"** (PRIMARY, H1 2026) |
| Average money per client | **~£226k** (derived: FUM ÷ clients) |
| Average money per adviser | **~£48.6m** across **~215 clients** (derived) |
| Profit (Underlying cash result, 2025) | **£462.3m** after tax (PRIMARY) |
| Leadership | Group CEO **Mark FitzPatrick**; CFO **Caroline Waddington**; Chair **Paul Manduca** (PRIMARY). CEO of SJP Wealth Management **James Rainbow**, ex-Schroders, appointed 2025 (REPORTED). CEO, Investments **Justin Onuekwusi** (REPORTED) |
| Head office | Cirencester. London office: Lombard Street, where your role is based |

**The mental model [Interpretation]:** think of SJP as two businesses bolted together.

1. **A distribution business.** It finds clients and keeps them, through ~5,000 advisers who build personal, long-term relationships.
2. **A manufacturing business.** It owns the "products" those clients invest through (pensions, ISAs, bonds) and the funds inside them.

The **advice** is what the client thinks they are buying. Most of SJP's **profit** comes from the **annual charges on the products and funds** that hold the client's money.

## A2. What SJP sells

### The products: where the money actually sits

Every client pound sits in a **"wrapper"**, a tax structure, and inside that in **funds**. SJP owns both layers.

| Product (wrapper) | What it is, in plain English | FUM at end-2025 | Share of FUM | 2025 gross inflows | Share of new money |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pensions** (personal pensions, SIPPs, drawdown) | Retirement savings with tax relief going in. Money is locked until age 55 (57 from 2028), then drawn as income. | **£119.9bn** | **55%** | **£13.9bn** | **63%** |
| **Investment bonds** | An insurance-based wrapper, popular for tax planning: deferring tax, trusts, inheritance planning. Mostly used by wealthier clients. | £44.1bn | 20% | £3.0bn | 14% |
| **Unit trusts / ISAs and DFM** | ISAs: up to £20k a year invested tax-free. Unit trusts: ordinary taxable investment accounts. DFM (discretionary fund management) is a bespoke portfolio run by a manager. | £56.0bn (incl. DFM £3.7bn) | 25% | £5.0bn | 23% |

All figures PRIMARY (FY2025 results). Shares of the total are derived from them. SJP's Asia and Middle East business (SJP AME) is included above, at £2.3bn of FUM (PRIMARY).

**Pensions are the engine.** They are over half the money and nearly two-thirds of new money. Why, **[Interpretation]**:

1. **Pension freedoms (2015).** Retirees no longer have to buy an annuity, a guaranteed income for life bought from an insurer. Instead they can keep their pension invested and draw income from it ("drawdown"). That requires ongoing investment decisions, which is exactly where advice is valuable.
2. **Consolidation.** People build up many small workplace pensions over a career. Bringing them together into one plan is a common, high-value piece of advice.
3. **Tax.** Pensions get income tax relief on contributions, and historically sat outside the estate for inheritance tax (IHT). That changes from **April 2027**, when unused pensions come into IHT (REPORTED). That change creates *more* need for advice.
4. **Stickiness.** Pension money stays for decades. The pension surrender rate was **4.0%** of FUM in 2025, against 7.4% for unit trusts and ISAs (PRIMARY).

### The funds: what the money is invested in

- **SJP's own fund range:** SJP's investment team doesn't mainly pick shares itself. It **selects, hires and monitors external fund managers** to run SJP-branded funds, a "manager of managers" model (PRIMARY: "investment management fees paid to external fund managers").
- **Polaris:** the flagship multi-asset range, **£94bn** (PRIMARY, end-2025). It has four risk levels, so a client can be matched to their risk appetite.
- **Polaris Multi-Index:** a cheaper, index-based range launched in 2025, above £1bn of FUM (PRIMARY).
- **Where the money is invested** (end-2025, PRIMARY):
  - 38% North American equities
  - 17% bonds (fixed income)
  - 14% European equities
  - 14% Asia-Pacific equities
  - 9% UK equities
  - the rest in cash, alternatives and property

### The services: what the adviser actually does

The client experiences this as **"advice"**, which covers:

- **Retirement planning:** how much you need, when you can stop work, and how to draw income tax-efficiently.
- **Investment planning:** using ISA and pension allowances, and matching risk to goals.
- **Inheritance and estate planning:** gifts, trusts, IHT.
- **Protection:** life cover, critical illness and income protection (REPORTED, SJP website).
- **Business owners and professionals:** extracting profits, exit planning, workplace financial education (REPORTED, SJP website).
- **Mortgages and wills:** offered through **separate firms**, and described by SJP as "separate and distinct" services. Wills are not FCA-regulated (REPORTED, SJP website).

**Why SJP sells this mix [Interpretation]:**

- **It makes the client "whole of life".** One relationship covers pensions, savings, protection, inheritance and the next generation, which raises retention.
- **Advice is the front door.** Every plan ends in SJP products. That is the commercial logic of vertical integration (owning several stages of what the client pays for), and also the source of the "restricted advice" conflict.
- **The client's family is the long-term prize.** Estate planning brings the next generation in before wealth passes down.

## A3. Who the target clients are, and why

### The profile [Interpretation, built on PRIMARY averages]

- **Wealth:** "mass affluent" to "high net worth" (HNW). Typically £100k to a few million to invest, with an average of ~£226k per client with SJP.
- **Life stage:** mainly **45 to 70+**. Pre-retirees and retirees, because pensions are 55% of FUM and retirement decisions trigger the need for advice.
- **Occupation:**
  - **business owners** (exit and profit-extraction planning);
  - **professionals** such as doctors, lawyers and senior employees. SJP runs propositions aimed at NHS staff and law society members (REPORTED, SJP website);
  - **families** planning to pass on wealth.
- **How they arrive:** mostly by **referral**, from existing clients, accountants and solicitors. That's cheap, trusted, and suits a relationship business.

### Why these clients and not others [Interpretation]

| Segment | Why SJP does or doesn't target it |
|---|---|
| **Mass market** (under ~£50k) | Too small. A percentage fee on £30k doesn't pay for an adviser's time. These people are served by platforms such as AJ Bell, robo-advisers, and now the FCA's **targeted support** regime (live April 2026). Targeted support lets firms give semi-personalised suggestions without full advice. |
| **Affluent and HNW** (£100k to £5m) | **The sweet spot.** Enough money that fees pay for real advice. Complex enough needs (pensions, tax, IHT, business exit) that advice clearly adds value. And people who value a personal relationship over the cheapest price. |
| **UHNW** (ultra-high net worth, conventionally US$30m+ net worth) | Mostly not SJP's market. These clients want private banking: lending, complex structures, bespoke portfolios, alternatives. They have the bargaining power to negotiate fees down. Coutts, JP Morgan Private Bank and UBS compete here. |

**The economics behind the sweet spot [Interpretation, derived]:**

- **Advice fees per adviser:** the average adviser looks after ~£48.6m. At 0.8% a year, that book generates roughly **£390k a year in ongoing advice charges**. That is before practice costs (staff, offices) and any share SJP takes.
- **Why that's attractive:** it's what makes being a Partner attractive, and why the model can support ~5,000 advisers.
- **The trade-off:** a client with £20k would generate £160 a year, which can't pay for meaningful adviser time.

## A4. What makes the most money

### How SJP's profit is built (2025, PRIMARY)

SJP's own profit measure is the **Underlying cash result**. It strips out accounting items and one-offs such as the refund provision, and from 2026 is called "adjusted IFRS profit after tax".

| Line | £m 2025 | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Net annual management fee | 1,170.9 | What SJP keeps from annual charges on FUM, **after** paying Partners' advice fees, external fund managers and the administrator |
| Less: reduction for "gestation" | (445.7) | Older pension and bond money in its first six years earned little product margin under the old charges |
| **= Net income from FUM** | **725.2** | **The core profit engine** |
| Margin from new business | 99.5 | Old-style initial charges minus new-business costs. **Expected to be ~£0 from 2026**, because the new initial advice charges mostly go to Partners |
| Shareholder interest | 68.5 | Interest earned on SJP's own cash |
| Controllable expenses | (305.8) | People, property, technology |

| **= Underlying cash result** | **462.3** | |

**The biggest revenue generator, by far:** annual charges on FUM. They make up ~81% of the positive items in 2025, and **more than that from 2026**, when new-business margin drops to around zero (derived).

**By product [Interpretation]:** SJP doesn't publish profit by product. Pensions are 55% of FUM and most of the new money, so they are almost certainly the largest single source of profit. They will grow more profitable as the ~£53bn of "gestation" money (mostly pensions and bonds) matures into full charging by around 2031 (PRIMARY: £52.9bn in gestation at end-2025).

### Who keeps what on a typical pound

For a client with £500k in an ISA in Polaris 3, paying **1.59% a year** (~£7,950):

| Layer | Rate | £ a year | Goes mainly to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ongoing advice | 0.80% | £4,000 | **The Partner** (SJP reportedly now keeps a share; the split isn't published) |
| Product charge | 0.27% | £1,350 | **SJP** (it pays the administrator out of this) |
| Fund charge | 0.52% | £2,600 | Split between **SJP** and the **external fund managers** |
| **SJP's net keep** | ~0.43–0.45% of mature FUM (PRIMARY guidance) | ~£2,150–2,250 | **SJP shareholders** (estimate) |

Under the new charges (from 26 Aug 2025), the **initial advice charge** (3%/2%/1%, capped at £30k) mostly funds the Partner. SJP says initial charges "broadly offset" its new-business costs (PRIMARY).

### The profit formula [Interpretation]

> **Profit ≈ FUM × net margin − largely fixed costs**

So four levers drive SJP's profit:

1. **Markets.** In 2025, investment returns added **£23.6bn** to FUM against only £6.2bn of net new money (PRIMARY). SJP's income rises and falls with stock markets.
   - Rough sensitivity: a 10% fall in FUM would cut net income from FUM by roughly £70m a year. That is about 15% of profit, because costs don't fall with markets. This is "operating leverage".
2. **Net flows:** new money in minus money out. Gross inflows were £21.9bn and net only £6.2bn in 2025 (PRIMARY), so retention matters as much as winning new clients.
3. **Margin:** cut by the 2025 charging change. It rebuilds as gestation money matures; management expects roughly +3bps a year to 2031 (REPORTED). A basis point (bp) is 0.01%.
4. **Costs:** a ~£100m-a-year cost reduction programme due by 2027 (PRIMARY).

**Why this matters for your role:** the head-office Wealth Management function mostly works on levers 2 and 4 (and protects the margin by keeping clients happy and compliant). See B4.

### Following one client through the business [Interpretation, based on how SJP describes its model]

1. **Referral.** A 58-year-old business owner is referred to a Partner by their accountant.
2. **Fact-find and advice.** The Partner gathers their finances and goals, builds a cashflow plan, and recommends consolidating three old pensions into an SJP pension invested in Polaris, plus annual ISA top-ups and an inheritance plan. The recommendation goes into a written **suitability report** explaining why it fits the client.
   - **Wealth Management** supplies the tools, templates, research and **advice-standards checks** behind this, because SJP is legally responsible for the advice.
3. **Implementation.** The money moves into an **SJP pension** (product). The **outsourced administrator** processes the paperwork. The money is invested in **Polaris**, which SJP's **Investments** team has handed to external fund managers to run.
4. **Charges.** The client pays the initial advice charge, which mostly goes to the Partner. Then every year they pay ongoing advice + product + fund charges, split as in A4.
5. **Ongoing service.** The Partner holds annual reviews.
   - **Wealth Management** helps make sure these happen *and are evidenced*. That is the lesson of the £426m refund provision.
   - Client service handles queries; data and MI (management information) teams monitor outcomes under **Consumer Duty**, the FCA rules requiring firms to deliver good outcomes and fair value.
6. **Retention and growth.** If the client is happy, they stay (~95% retention), add money, and introduce their children. FUM grows, and so does SJP's income.
7. **Group functions.** **Finance** reports results to shareholders; **Risk and Compliance** oversee conduct; the **Board** sets strategy (now moving from "Strengthen" to "Amplify" in 2027).

### The flywheel [Interpretation]

> Better adviser tools and proposition → more productive, loyal Partners → better client outcomes and retention → more FUM → more income → reinvest in advisers and tools.

The Wealth Management division sits at the **start of the flywheel**. That's why a graduate there can affect the numbers that matter.

### Built-in tensions to understand

- **Restricted advice vs client choice:** SJP's advisers recommend mainly SJP's own products. This is legal, but it's a permanent conflict of interest that has to be managed.
- **Partner independence vs SJP's control:** the Partners are entrepreneurs, but SJP is liable for their advice. Too much control drives Partners away; too little creates conduct risk.
- **Growth vs supervision:** more advisers means more oversight burden.
- **Price vs value:** under Consumer Duty, SJP must *prove* clients get fair value for ~1.6–1.7% a year.

# PART B: THE ROLE

## B1. What "wealth management" actually means

"Wealth management" is used loosely. It helps to separate the activities, because different firms do different combinations.

| Activity | What it is | Example UK firms |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial planning / advice** | Understanding a person's whole financial life and recommending what to do: pensions, tax, protection, inheritance. Regulated; advisers need a **Level 4** qualification. | SJP, Quilter, IFA firms, Evelyn |
| **Investment management (discretionary)** | A manager runs a portfolio and makes the buy/sell decisions for the client. | Rathbones, Evelyn, Quilter Cheviot |
| **Advisory / execution-only investing** | The client makes the decisions, with or without suggestions. | Hargreaves Lansdown, AJ Bell (D2C platforms) |
| **Private banking** | Banking, lending and investment services for very wealthy clients. | Coutts, JP Morgan Private Bank, UBS |
| **Family office** | A dedicated team managing everything for one ultra-wealthy family. | Bespoke |
| **Asset management** | *Makes* funds and sells them to many investors. It serves the fund, not the individual client. | BlackRock, Schroders, M&G |

**The distinction to be crisp on:**

- **Asset management** manufactures investment *products*.
- **Wealth management** serves *people*: it decides what a particular individual should do with their money and looks after them over time.
- **SJP is mainly a wealth manager** (advice-led), with an asset management *arm* that it largely outsources to external managers.
- Your understanding of asset-management fees is useful here: it's the "fund layer" of SJP's model.

### What a financial planner actually does: the advice journey

1. **Discovery / fact-find.** Income, assets, debts, pensions, family, goals, attitude to risk, **capacity for loss** (how much the client could afford to lose without hurting their plans).
2. **Analysis.** **Cashflow modelling** projects their finances year by year to answer questions like "can I retire at 60?" and "how much can I gift?". It also involves tax analysis and reviewing existing pensions and investments.
3. **Recommendation.** A **suitability report** sets out what to do and *why it suits this client*. This is an FCA requirement.
4. **Implementation.** Opening pensions and ISAs, transferring money, choosing funds, arranging protection.
5. **Ongoing service.** Annual reviews: rebalancing, using new tax allowances, reacting to life events (retirement, inheritance, divorce, illness) and to tax changes. Evidence that the service happened is essential under **Consumer Duty**.

### The regulatory basics you should know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **FCA** | Financial Conduct Authority, which regulates how firms treat customers |
| **RDR (2013)** | Retail Distribution Review. Banned commission on investment advice, required advisers to hold Level 4 qualifications, and created the "independent" vs "restricted" labels |
| **Consumer Duty (2023)** | Firms must deliver good outcomes and **fair value**, and be able to prove it |
| **Suitability** | Advice must fit the individual client's needs, objectives and risk profile |
| **FOS** | Financial Ombudsman Service, which settles customer complaints for free |
| **FSCS** | Financial Services Compensation Scheme, which compensates customers if a firm fails. Funded by an industry levy |
| **Targeted support (Apr 2026)** | A new FCA category between generic guidance and full advice, aimed at people who can't afford advice |

  - investment principles and risk;

## B3. How the role connects to the business model

Every head-office project should move one of SJP's profit levers (see A4). If you can say which lever your work moves, you sound commercial.

| Head-office lever | Business metric it moves | Why it matters now |
|---|---|---|
| Better adviser tools, less admin | **Adviser productivity → gross inflows** | SJP is trialling AI tools for Partners (PRIMARY). Investors fear AI disruption (REPORTED, Feb 2026) |

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **AME** | SJP's Asia and Middle East business |
| **Basis point (bp)** | 0.01%. 45bps = 0.45% |
| **Capacity for loss** | How much a client could lose without damaging their plans |
| **Cashflow modelling** | Projecting a client's finances year by year to test plans |
| **D2C** | Direct-to-consumer: investors use a platform themselves, without an adviser |
| **DFM** | Discretionary fund management: a manager makes the investment decisions for the client |
| **Drawdown** | Keeping a pension invested and taking income from it in retirement |
| **FUM** | Funds under management: total client money looked after |
| **Gestation** | Under SJP's old charges, the first six years of pension and bond money, when it earned little product margin |
| **Gross / net inflows** | New money in / new money in minus money out |
| **HNW / UHNW** | High net worth / ultra-high net worth (conventionally US$30m+) |
| **MI** | Management information: regular data reports for decision-makers |
| **Partner** | SJP's self-employed adviser, an appointed representative of SJP |
| **Polaris** | SJP's flagship multi-asset fund range |
| **Restricted advice** | Advice limited to certain products or providers (vs independent, whole-of-market) |
| **Retention** | Share of client money that didn't leave in the period |
| **Suitability report** | The written explanation of why advice fits the client |
| **Wrapper** | The tax structure money sits in: pension, ISA, bond, unit trust |
