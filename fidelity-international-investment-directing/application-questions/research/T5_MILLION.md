## 1.2 UK tax and wrapper rules for 2026/27 (and the known 2027 changes)

This is the part almost no candidate will get exactly right, and getting it exactly right is a cheap way to look serious. Key structural point: with £1m, the candidate cannot shelter everything in tax wrappers in year one — ISA capacity is only £20,000/year and pension capacity is £60,000/year (before any carry-forward) — so wrapper strategy and unwrapped tax treatment both matter.

- **ISA allowance 2026/27**: £20,000 total, splittable across cash, stocks & shares, innovative finance and Lifetime ISA. [T184 / gov.uk][Confirmed]
- **2027 cash ISA reform** (announced Autumn Budget 2025, effective 6 April 2027, i.e. NOT yet in force for the 2026/27 year the candidate is imagining winning the money in): for under-65s, the cash ISA sub-limit falls from £20,000 to £12,000 within the same overall £20,000 ISA envelope (so the other £8,000 must go into stocks & shares/innovative finance ISAs to use the full allowance); the £20,000 cash ISA limit is preserved for those aged 65+. [T184][Confirmed] Anti-circumvention measures accompanying this: a proposed ~22% charge on interest earned on cash held inside non-cash ISAs, a ban on non-cash ISA portfolios consisting entirely of cash-like assets/money market funds, and a ban on under-65s transferring from a non-cash ISA into a cash ISA. [T184][Confirmed] Rationale given by the Chancellor: "The UK has some of the lowest levels of retail investment in the G7… bad for business… bad for savers, too." [Reported, quote via search synthesis, not independently re-verified against Hansard]
- **Pension annual allowance 2026/27**: £60,000 gross per year (subject to having sufficient relevant UK earnings; carry-forward of unused allowance from the previous 3 tax years is available). [T200][Confirmed]
- **Tapered annual allowance**: for "adjusted income" above £260,000 (broadly all income plus employer pension contributions) and "threshold income" above £200,000, the £60,000 allowance tapers down by £1 for every £2 of adjusted income above £260,000, down to a floor of £10,000 at £360,000 adjusted income. [T200][Confirmed] Relevant if the candidate frames the £1m as sitting alongside a high income.
- **Money Purchase Annual Allowance (MPAA)**: £10,000/year once a defined-contribution pension has been flexibly accessed (i.e. taken income beyond the 25% tax-free lump sum). [T200][Confirmed]
- **Capital Gains Tax (CGT), 2026/27**: annual exempt amount £3,000; rates on non-business assets 18% within the basic-rate band and 24% in the higher/additional-rate bands (these are the rates set at the October 2024 Budget and unchanged for 2026/27 in the source read). [T189][Confirmed]
- **Dividend allowance and rates, 2026/27**: £500 tax-free dividend allowance (frozen); dividend tax rates rising 2 percentage points versus 2024/25 levels to 10.75% (basic rate), 35.75% (higher rate) and presumably ~39.35% (additional rate, per HL table). [T189][Confirmed]
- **Personal Savings Allowance (PSA)**: £1,000 for basic-rate taxpayers, £500 for higher-rate taxpayers, £0 for additional-rate taxpayers. [T189][Confirmed]
- **Starting rate for savings**: 0% tax on up to £5,000 of savings income, but this band is reduced £-for-£ by non-savings income above the personal allowance and disappears once non-savings income reaches £17,570; frozen at £5,000 until at least April 2031. Mainly relevant to someone with low/no earned income (a student is actually a plausible beneficiary of this if the £1m is genuinely a windfall and they have little other income) — full stacking (personal allowance £12,570 + starting rate £5,000 + PSA £1,000 = £18,570 of interest tax-free) is a distinctive, non-obvious point for a candidate to raise if realistic for their own tax position. [T201][Confirmed, standard published rule; the £18,570 stacking arithmetic is straightforward addition, not itself sourced separately]
- **Stamp Duty Reserve Tax (SDRT) on UK shares**: 0.5% of consideration on electronic purchases of UK shares (paper stock transfers also 0.5%, over a £1,000 de minimis), collected automatically by the broker. This is a real, quotable frictional cost on entering UK equities that most candidates will ignore. [T202][Confirmed]
- **Gilts and CGT — the standout non-obvious tax point**: gains on individual UK gilts (gilt-edged securities) held directly are exempt from Capital Gains Tax for individuals, under section 115 of the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992. [T183][Confirmed, primary HMRC/gov.uk source] This exemption applies only to individual gilts held directly, not to gilt funds, gilt ETFs or gilt-based OEICs, which remain subject to CGT in the normal way. [T190][Confirmed] Coupon income on gilts is still taxable as savings income at the holder's marginal rate. This creates the well-known "low-coupon gilt" tax trade for higher/additional-rate taxpayers who have used up their ISA and pension allowances: buy a gilt with a low fixed coupon below its £100 redemption value, so most of the return comes as a tax-free capital gain at maturity rather than as taxable income. Example structure cited: Treasury 0.125% 2039, bought around £80 in October 2022, redeeming at £100 — the £20 capital uplift (25% of the purchase price) is entirely CGT-free, while the tiny 0.125% coupon generates almost no taxable income. [T190][Confirmed — concrete example from a secondary source, mechanism itself confirmed by gov.uk]
- **NS&I Premium Bonds**: prize fund rate rising to 4.35% from 3.80% from the September 2026 draw, with odds shortening to 21,000-to-1 (from 22,000-to-1); maximum individual holding £50,000. Prizes are tax-free but the "return" is a lottery-style annual prize rate, not a guaranteed yield — most holders get somewhat less than the headline rate, a minority get more (including the jackpot). [T185][Confirmed]

## 1.3 Frameworks a thoughtful candidate would use

- **Horizon vs risk tolerance vs risk capacity** — three genuinely distinct concepts that weak answers conflate:
  - *Time horizon*: when the money is actually needed.
  - *Risk tolerance*: the investor's subjective, psychological willingness to accept volatility/loss (how they feel about a 20% drawdown).
  - *Risk capacity*: the investor's objective, financial ability to absorb a loss without harming their goals (whether a 20% drawdown actually damages their plan), driven by income, other assets, and how essential the goal is. [T204][Confirmed] A candidate who says "I'm young and have high risk tolerance, so I'll put it all in equities even for the short-term bucket" has made exactly the standard classic error: horizon and capacity, not just appetite, should govern the short-term allocation.
- **Liability-driven thinking — "what is the money actually FOR?"**: rather than starting from asset classes, a more sophisticated answer starts by defining the liabilities/goals the £1m must fund (a house deposit in 18 months, income to live on, a pension in 40 years, a rainy-day buffer) and only then matches assets to each liability's horizon and certainty of size/date. This is standard institutional/wealth-management practice (liability-driven investing, originally a pension-fund concept, applied at personal scale) and is a genuinely more sophisticated frame than "split by time horizon" alone, because two goals with the same horizon can need different assets if one has a fixed, certain cost (e.g. a house deposit) and one doesn't (e.g. general future income).
- **Emergency fund principle**: conventional guidance is 3-6 months of essential expenses in instant-access cash before taking investment risk with the rest, rising to ~9 months for irregular/self-employed income. [T206][Confirmed, standard planning guidance, multiple concordant sources] With a £1m windfall this is almost trivial to satisfy, so a candidate who only mentions "keep an emergency fund" without going further hasn't said anything distinctive — the interesting question is what happens to the other £900k+ of "short-term" money.
- **Sequence-of-returns risk**: the risk that a bad run of returns occurs at the wrong time relative to cash flows (classically, early in retirement when withdrawals begin, or — the relevant case here — early in a short investment horizon when the money will soon be needed and cannot wait out a downturn). Because returns compound path-dependently, being forced to sell into a fall to meet a near-term liability locks in the loss in a way that a long-horizon investor never has to. [T206 general framework — actually sourced at generic retirement-planning pages, applied here to short-horizon logic][Confirmed as a general concept]. This is the rigorous reason "cash for the short term" is correct — not just "equities are risky," but specifically that a forced sale at a low point cannot be undone.
- **Why cash suits short horizons, equities suit long ones**: over short horizons, the probability of loss in equities is materially higher than over long horizons (see 1.4 for exact historical figures); over long horizons, cash's apparent safety of nominal value is offset by a near-certainty of real (inflation-adjusted) erosion, and equities' short-term volatility washes out. The academic/practitioner shorthand: equities are volatile but have positive expected real returns and mean-revert over long periods; cash is "safe" in nominal terms only and has a near-zero (over the very long run modestly positive but historically much lower) real return.
- **Real vs nominal returns — inflation as the long-horizon enemy**: a candidate should explicitly distinguish nominal returns from real (inflation-adjusted) returns. At August 2026 CPI running at 3.1% (above the 2% target), leaving a large sum in cash for decades is not "safe" in the sense that matters for a long-term goal — it just swaps market risk for purchasing-power risk. This is the single clearest way to make the standard "equities for the long run" answer sound reasoned rather than recited.
- **Diversification and correlation**: the benefit of diversification comes specifically from combining assets that are imperfectly correlated, so that portfolio volatility is lower than the volatility of any one holding; a candidate limited to 150 words and told to use "at least one asset class" doesn't need a full efficient-frontier discussion, but should show awareness that diversification is about correlation, not just "not putting all your eggs in one basket" as a slogan.
- **The case for and against bonds in a long-horizon portfolio**: For — bonds/gilts reduce portfolio volatility, provide ballast in equity drawdowns, and (per 1.1) currently pay a historically high yield (30-year gilts near 5.7%-5.9% in September 2026) making the "bonds are pointless at low yields" objection weaker today than in the 2010s-early 2020s ultra-low-rate era. Against — over multi-decade horizons, bonds have historically delivered much lower real returns than equities (see 1.4), so a very long horizon with high risk capacity arguably doesn't need much bond ballast at all; also, long-dated gilts have just suffered a historic sell-off (worst levels since 1998 in September 2026), which is a live illustration that "safe" government bonds are not risk-free in price terms, only in default terms.
- **Cost/fee drag**: fees compound against the investor exactly as returns compound for the investor, because each year's fee is charged on — and permanently removes capital from — the base that would otherwise keep compounding. Illustrative figure: on $100,000 growing at 7% gross for 30 years, a fund charging 1% annually leaves the investor with about $574,000 versus about $761,000 fee-free — a gap of roughly $187,000, i.e. the 1% annual fee costs about 25% of the terminal fee-free wealth over 30 years. [Reported, generic compounding-fee illustration, not UK/GBP-specific but the arithmetic is standard and directly transferable] Over a genuinely long (40+ year) horizon relevant to a £1m windfall for someone in their early 20s, this effect is even larger — a strong reason to at least mention fund charges/OCFs when discussing the long-term allocation, and a natural (if double-edged, see 1.6) segue into the active-vs-passive question.

## 1.5 What makes an answer distinctive (angles, not answers)

- **Start from "what is the money for?" rather than starting from the clock.** The obvious answer buckets by time only (short vs long). A sharper answer buckets by purpose first (liquidity/safety need, a known medium-term goal, long-run growth/legacy) and lets each purpose imply its own horizon, only then choosing the asset class.
- **£1m is well above the FSCS deposit protection limit, so "just put it in cash" is not obviously safe.** The FSCS protects deposits up to £120,000 per person, per authorised institution (raised from £85,000, effective 1 December 2025 — a change the PRA implemented to reflect inflation since the 2017 level was set). [T182][Confirmed] £1m held in a single bank account therefore leaves roughly £880,000 uninsured against institutional failure. A candidate who spots that a "cash/money-market for the short term" answer has a real, quantifiable credit-risk gap — and who mentions either spreading deposits across multiple FSCS-protected institutions or a suitably diversified money market fund/gilt-based structure as the fix — is making a point that "almost no candidate will mention" (per the brief) and that is directly, numerically verifiable. Note also FSCS covers *temporary high balances* (e.g. from a house sale or inheritance, not from a lottery/prize win per se) up to £1.4m for six months — worth being precise that a "winnings" windfall may or may not qualify for that specific temporary protection, so it should not be relied on as the mechanism.
- **Gilts held directly are CGT-exempt — a genuinely under-used, defensible technical point (1.2 above).** This lets a candidate build a "short-term but not pure cash" bucket using short-dated gilts (which are also government-backed, i.e. no FSCS-limit problem, and liquid) while noting the tax efficiency for anyone using up ISA/pension space, especially at higher-rate tax. It is more sophisticated than "cash ISA + easy access", because it solves both the tax problem and the £120k-FSCS-limit problem in the same instrument, and gilt yields are attractive in September 2026 in any case (1.1 above).
- **A liquidity ladder / gilt ladder**: rather than a single "short-term bucket," a laddered structure of short-dated gilts and/or fixed-term deposits maturing in sequence (e.g. 3, 6, 12, 24 months) matches near-term liquidity needs to instrument maturities, reduces reinvestment/interest-rate timing risk relative to one lump fixed-term deposit, and is a recognisable professional technique (used by cash managers, family offices and increasingly retail platforms) rather than a retail-only "savings account" answer.
- **Behavioural risk is the biggest single risk in a windfall, more than any asset-allocation choice.** Money that is not earned tends to be treated differently — research on lottery winners finds roughly 70% dissipate a windfall within about seven years, driven by psychological effects (windfall money mentally coded as "found"/"play" money, social pressure to be generous or to fund others, and the mismatch between the sudden size of the wealth and the individual's prior financial habits/identity) rather than by bad asset selection per se. [T205][Reported — "sudden wealth syndrome" and the ~70%/seven-year figure are widely repeated in secondary/financial-planning sources; treat the exact 70% figure as Reported rather than a single, pinned primary academic citation.] A candidate who names this and argues that discipline/behaviour (avoiding rash decisions, taking time before committing, using rules/wrappers that create friction against impulsive spending) is a bigger determinant of the outcome than the precise split between equities and gilts is making a distinctive, evidence-adjacent behavioural-finance point rather than a purely mechanical asset-allocation one.
- **Phased entry vs lump sum — and being honest about what the evidence actually shows.** The "obvious cautious" instinct is to drip the £1m into markets over months to "average in." Vanguard's own research concludes lump-sum investing wins more often than phased entry historically (roughly 60-70%+ of historical periods, across US/UK/Australian markets), because markets rise more often than they fall, so cash sitting on the sidelines is a real, quantifiable drag, not a free insurance policy. [T187][Confirmed for the conclusion; percentage detail Reported] The nuance a strong candidate can add: phasing is not "wrong," it is a rational trade of some expected return for reduced regret/volatility exposure — but it should be presented as a deliberate psychological trade-off, not dressed up as a purely rational risk-management technique with no cost.
- **The 150-word limit rewards one strong, well-executed idea over a checklist.** Given the constraint, a candidate who tries to namecheck cash, gilts, equities, property, gold, alternatives and tax wrappers in 150 words will read as a superficial list. A distinctive answer picks ONE genuinely sharp idea (e.g., the FSCS-limit point, or the CGT-exempt-gilts point, or the behavioural-risk point) and builds the whole 150 words around demonstrating it is understood, rather than spreading thin across many correct-but-shallow points.

## 1.6 The traps — what would make an answer weak

- **Confusing horizon with risk tolerance/capacity.** Saying "I'm young/adventurous so I'll take more risk with the short-term money too" ignores that the short-term bucket's allocation should be driven by *when the money is needed*, not by the investor's general risk appetite (1.3).
- **Recommending specific products, funds, platforms or individual stocks.** An application-form answer is not the place to name a specific fund, ISA provider, or stock pick (e.g. "buy Nvidia" or "use a Vanguard tracker") — this reads as naive stock-tipping to an asset manager's recruiter, not as demonstrating a framework. Referring to asset classes and, at most, generic instrument types (a global equity index fund, a short-dated gilt) is a safer register.
- **Ignoring tax and wrappers entirely.** With £1m, ignoring ISA/pension capacity constraints (only £20,000/£60,000 a year respectively — 1.2), CGT, dividend tax and the gilts-CGT-exemption point is a missed opportunity to show real UK personal-finance literacy, which is precisely what this question is testing.
- **Ignoring inflation.** Treating "safe" as meaning "nominal capital doesn't fall" rather than "real purchasing power is preserved/grown" is the single most common conceptual gap, especially for the long-term leg, given CPI is currently running at 3.1% versus a 2% target (1.1, 1.3).
- **Being so generic it could be written by anyone** — "diversify," "invest for the long term," "don't put all your eggs in one basket," with no UK-specific number, tax rule, or named framework attached. The brief's own framing ("cash/money market for short term, global equity tracker for long term") is explicitly the obvious answer; reproducing it without a distinctive angle from 1.5 wastes the exercise.
- **Mentioning crypto/alternatives with no framework.** Namechecking bitcoin, crypto or exotic alternatives as a "for the long term, some crypto too" aside, without any risk/volatility/regulatory framing, reads as fashionable rather than analytical. If mentioned at all, it should be inside a clear risk-budget/diversification argument, not as a throwaway.
- **Forgetting the audience: this is an application to an ACTIVE asset manager.** A pure "buy a global passive index tracker and do nothing" long-term answer is defensible on the evidence (low fees compound powerfully in the investor's favour — 1.3; the majority of active funds still underperform passive over 10 years — see below) but sits in some tension with applying to a firm whose business model is active management. This doesn't mean the candidate should abandon the evidence-based passive case — it means they should show awareness of the tension, e.g. by noting where active management has a stronger evidentiary case (categories with more dispersion/inefficiency, such as fixed income, emerging markets or specific niches, rather than large-cap developed equities) or by simply being aware that a reviewer at an active manager may read a flat "passive is always better" claim as slightly tone-deaf, even if factually well-supported. Recent data point for this framing: just over 40% of active funds beat their asset-weighted passive composite over the one year to June 2026 (up from the year before), but roughly 75% of active funds still trail passive over 10 years, and active managers make their strongest case in fixed income, real estate and emerging markets, and their weakest in US large-cap growth. # 6. HOW THIS HELPS THE CANDIDATE ANSWER (analysis angles, not drafted prose)

- **Structuring device**: the candidate has 150 words and must "invest in at least one asset class" and "talk about the asset allocation" for two time horizons. A clean skeleton the research supports: (i) one sentence framing what the money is for at each horizon (liability-driven framing, 1.3); (ii) short-term allocation with a reason rooted in horizon/behavioural risk, not just "safety" (sequence-of-returns logic, 1.3, plus the FSCS £120k point, 1.5, plus optionally the CGT-exempt short gilt idea, 1.2/1.5); (iii) long-term allocation with a reason rooted in real returns and time-diversification of equity risk (1.3, 1.4); (iv) optionally, one line acknowledging the behavioural risk of a windfall or the active-manager tension (1.5, 1.6) to show self-awareness. Given the 150-word cap, the research suggests picking at most one "distinctive" angle from 1.5 rather than trying to use all of them.
- **On the short-term leg**: the researched material supports going beyond "put it in cash" to something that shows genuine technical range — e.g., recognising that £1m sitting in one deposit account exceeds the £120,000 FSCS limit (1.1/1.5) is a specific, checkable, non-obvious fact that very few other candidates are likely to know or mention, and naming it (or its practical fix — spreading deposits, using a diversified money-market fund, or holding short-dated gilts instead of a single deposit) demonstrates attention to a real risk rather than reciting "keep some cash for an emergency fund." The current elevated level of instant-access/fixed-term rates and money-market yields (roughly 4-5%, 1.1) also lets the candidate use a real, current, sourced number rather than a vague "cash pays interest" statement.
- **On the long-term leg**: the research supports grounding "equities for the long term" in two concrete, sourceable facts rather than assertion: (a) the historical probability of equities beating cash/gilts rises sharply with horizon (1.4 — with the caveat in Gaps that the exact percentages vary by source/comparison, so the candidate should pick one clean statistic and attribute it loosely to "long-run UK market data" rather than overclaiming precision); and (b) inflation (currently 3.1%, 1.1) means a long horizon spent entirely in cash is not "safe," it is a near-certain real-terms loss — this reframes "equities are risky" into "cash is risky too, just differently," which is the more sophisticated framing examiners are likely to reward.
- **On "at least one asset class" for each horizon**: the brief only requires one per horizon, but the research shows the strongest candidates would likely differentiate the short-term asset class (cash/money market/short gilts) from the long-term one (global or UK equities, possibly with a bond allocation acknowledged and explicitly reasoned about rather than default), and could use the currently-elevated gilt yield environment (1.1) as a reason a long-term portfolio need not be 100% equities even for a very long horizon, if the candidate wants to show nuance about bonds' role (1.3).
- **On tax**: given the £1m sum, a candidate who shows awareness that not all of it can be sheltered immediately (only £20k/year into an ISA, only £60k/year into a pension, 1.2) — and who is aware of at least one non-obvious tax mechanism (gilts' CGT exemption, 1.2) — signals real UK personal-finance literacy that a generic "diversify and invest for the long run" answer cannot. This is likely to be one of the highest-value, most differentiating facts available from this research, precisely because the brief itself flags it as something "almost no candidate will mention."
- **On behavioural framing**: the research supports a version of the answer that treats "how would you invest it" as inseparable from "how would you behave with it" — i.e., that the biggest risk to a £1m windfall is not asset allocation at all, but behavioural (1.5). This is a genuinely different kind of answer to the two-bucket allocation answer and, if used, should probably be the single organizing idea of the whole 150 words rather than an add-on, given the word limit.
- **On the "active manager" tension**: because this is an application to a house whose business is active management, the research shows there is a live, sourceable debate (1.6) about whether a purely passive long-term recommendation is in some tension with the employer's business model. A candidate does not need to resolve this, but showing they have thought about it (e.g., by nodding to asset classes/geographies where active management has more evidence behind it, per 1.6) is a way to seem aware of context without pretending the passive evidence doesn't exist.
- **Caution flagged by this research**: several of the "if you want the punchiest possible number" statistics (the exact 76%/91% and 79%/88% Barclays probabilities, the 70%-of-lottery-winners figure, the Vanguard 61.6%-73.7% lump-sum win-rate) are Reported rather than Confirmed from primary sources (see Gaps).
