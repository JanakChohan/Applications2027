# 1. FINDINGS

## 1.1 Fidelity International's stated selection approach, in the firm's own words

Fidelity International (FIL) describes its process as **strengths-based**, not competency-based, and says this explicitly: "We've deliberately moved away from a process where we rely on competency-based questions. We're much more interested in the core strengths that motivate someone in the workplace" — a Talent Acquisition Manager quoted on TargetJobs [T232, Confirmed]. The firm frames this as finding "what engages and motivates you" rather than testing pre-formed competencies, and tells candidates to "talk about examples from your work, school, and personal life to demonstrate to us the strengths that you have" [T232, Confirmed].

On technical skill, FIL is explicit and this maps directly onto the brief's stated line: "We do not seek pre-existing technical skills or experience in our industry" but do want "understanding of what we do and what you would need to do in the role" [T231, Confirmed — read directly on the FAQs page]. The same FAQ page adds that strong applicants "will be prepared and have done their research, reading widely (beyond just the 'About Us' page on our website) and show a genuine enthusiasm for this opportunity" [T231, Confirmed]. This is the load-bearing sentence for both Q1 and Q2: FIL is not grading a finance degree, it is grading whether the candidate has actually gone and read about the industry and can show it.

FIL's stated values and behaviours [T231, Confirmed, fetched directly]:
- Two core **values**: **Integrity** ("doing the right thing, every time and putting the client first") and **Trust** ("empowering each other to take the initiative and make good decisions").
- Four **behaviours**: **Brave** ("challenging the status quo, being accountable and speaking up"), **Bold** ("acting with conviction, encouraging diverse thinking and keeping things simple"), **Curious** ("Learning to do new things in better ways and encouraging fresh thinking"), **Compassionate** ("having empathy, caring for colleagues, clients and community").

The named **strengths** FIL screens for, with how to demonstrate them, per TargetJobs' Fidelity-authored graduate profile page (fetched directly) [T233, Confirmed]:
1. **Customer Focus** — "We think constantly about the needs of our customers, and how we can go above and beyond."
2. **Integrity** — "We look to do the right things by our customers, and by our colleagues, every time."
3. **Curiosity** — "We are looking for candidates who are curious about the world around them, financial and otherwise" — evidenced by researching FIL "beyond basic information," "following industry news," and "asking informed questions."
4. **Self-Starter** — "You're motivated to make an impact and actively seek self-improvement."
5. **Adaptability** — "The world of investment management is fast changing, so you need to be ready to adapt."

Note the overlap: "Curiosity" as a named strength is evidenced, in FIL's own words, by "following industry news" — this is almost a direct description of what Q1 asks the candidate to demonstrate (a theme/article/news story learned about recently).

## 1.2 Mapping the four Investment Directing 2027 posting phrases to Q1 vs Q2

The brief supplies four phrases from the 2027 posting. I could not re-fetch that exact live posting (it returns 403 / is behind the application portal at this point in the cycle — see GAPS), but I independently found near-identical wording used by FIL on an early-careers apprenticeship posting fetched directly [T236, Confirmed as FIL house style/language, though that specific page is for a Technology apprenticeship, not Investment Directing — treat the exact wording as [Reported] for the Investment Directing 2027 posting specifically, since the brief is the primary source for that attribution]. Reasoning on the mapping:

- **"Analytical, eager to learn and ready to take on a challenge"** — a general framing statement, not diagnostic of either question specifically. It sets the tone: they expect a candidate without full technical grounding but who thinks in a structured way. Both Q1 and Q2 test "analytical" in different registers (Q1 = analytical about a real-world event; Q2 = analytical about a portfolio-construction problem). [Inferred]
- **"A genuine curiosity and eagerness to learn about investment"** — maps most directly to **Q1**. Curiosity about investment is demonstrated by knowing about a recent theme/story and being able to say why it matters to an investor — that is literally what Q1 asks for. [Inferred]
- **"A lively interest in our work and our industry"** — maps to **both**, but slightly more to **Q1**, because "our work" for Investment Directing specifically means translating markets and portfolios into client-facing output (see 1.4 below); demonstrating live interest in "our industry" (asset management, not just financial markets in general) is best shown through a story that is investment-industry-relevant, not just macro/news-relevant in general. [Inferred]
- **"Creative thinking and attention to detail"** — maps most directly to **Q2**. Q2 is a constrained, structured problem (allocate £1m across two time horizons, at least one asset class each) where "creative thinking" shows up as choice of asset classes/instruments beyond the obvious, and "attention to detail" shows up in correctly linking risk, time horizon, and asset choice (e.g., not putting equities in a "next month" short-term bucket). [Inferred]

## 1.3 How written application answers are actually screened in practice

No firm publishes a scoring rubric for individual application-form questions, and I did not find one for FIL specifically or for asset managers generally — this is consistent across all sources checked. What is well corroborated from multiple independent graduate-recruitment-advice sources:

- **Multi-stage funnel, application form is stage 1 of several.** FIL's own hiring-process page (fetched directly) lists: (1) online application + CV, (2) online testing — "a combination of cognitive assessments and/or personality questionnaire," 20–30 minutes, with "5 days to complete," (3) a video interview ("your first opportunity to meet with a member of our hiring team"), (4) an assessment day with "a mix of technical, commercial, and motivational interviews, and a technical case study exercise" [T230, Confirmed]. This means the written answers are not the only sift point, but they are the first one, and a poor answer means the candidate never reaches the stages where they could otherwise shine.
- **Recruiters, not the business, do the first read.** FIL's FAQ says "skilled recruiters will review your CV, looking for all your relevant skills and experience," and only escalate to the hiring manager if there are questions [T231, Confirmed]. This is standard industry practice: a recruitment/TA team does the first sift against basic criteria, with the hiring business (portfolio managers, investment specialists) typically only seeing applications that clear that first bar. [Inferred from T230/T231 plus general graduate-recruitment literature]
- **Answers are very likely to be revisited at interview.** This is not FIL-specific confirmed language, but it is strongly and repeatedly corroborated across independent graduate-careers advice sources, which the brief itself flags as "very important." TargetJobs' general application-form guidance explicitly tells candidates to "keep a copy of your submitted responses so you can review it before any interview you may be invited to" [T249, Confirmed — general advice, two credible outlets corroborate]. Bright Network's asset-management-specific application guidance repeats the same instruction almost verbatim: "Keep a copy of your submitted responses so you can review it before any interview that you may be invited to" [T251, Confirmed]. The consistent appearance of this exact advice across two independent graduate-careers platforms for asset management specifically is strong corroboration that interviewers commonly probe written answers directly — a candidate should treat every claim in the written answer as something they must be able to expand on, defend, or give a follow-up example for, live, without notes.
- **Time spent per application.** I could not find a disclosed, sourced figure for time-per-application at FIL or at asset managers generally (see GAPS). General graduate-recruitment commentary (TargetJobs, Financial Edge Training, Mergers & Inquisitions) describes high volumes and time pressure on recruiters but does not give a number credible enough to cite as a fact; I have not included an invented figure. [Gap — flagged, not fabricated]
- **AI/plagiarism-style detection.** See 1.4 — FIL has gone further than most employers by publishing an explicit Code of Conduct on AI use in applications (below), which functions as their detection/screening policy statement even without disclosing a specific software tool.
- **STAR/STARR-style structuring is the near-universal implicit rubric for competency-shaped answers.** Bright Network tells FIL applicants directly to use "the STARR model" (Situation, Task, Action, Result, Reflection) for a strengths-based example [T233, Confirmed — from the Curiosity/Adaptability demonstration guidance]. Q1 and Q2 are not classic STAR competency questions (they are analysis/scenario questions, not "tell me about a time"), but the underlying assessor expectation — structure, a clear point, a worked example, a conclusion — is the same discipline. [Inferred]

## 1.4 AI-written applications: Fidelity International's stated policy, and the wider market

This is a major, directly on-point finding. FIL has published a live page titled **"Candidates AI Code of Conduct — Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI)"**, fetched directly [T234, Confirmed]. Key content, verbatim (see section 3 for exact quotes):

- **Permitted, no disclosure needed:** using AI to draft/structure/tailor a CV or cover letter, improve grammar/spelling/tone/formatting, translation, interview preparation, and accessibility tools (e.g. screen readers).
- **Permitted, but must be disclosed:** using AI to prepare "written responses for non-timed tasks where originality, reasoning or reflection is assessed" — this is precisely the category Q1 and Q2 fall into (they are non-timed, written, reasoning/reflection tasks on an application form). Also covers summarising prior work, or code generators in take-home tasks where explicitly allowed.
- **Banned outright:** real-time AI coaching/prompting/answer generation during live interviews or supervised assessments; using AI to cheat or bypass aptitude/psychometric/coding tests; impersonation/deepfakes; and — the core principle — "Submitting AI-generated content as your own where authenticity, judgement or personal capability is being assessed."
- **Stated rationale, verbatim:** "We are interested in you – your judgement, experience, potential and values – not your ability to outsource thinking to technology."
- **Consequence:** "Failure to follow this Code of Conduct, or deliberate misrepresentation, may result in disqualification or withdrawal of an offer."

This directly answers the brief's question: **FIL does have a stated AI policy**, and it does not ban AI outright, but it requires disclosure for exactly the kind of "originality, reasoning or reflection" written answer that Q1 and Q2 are, and explicitly says an AI-generated answer submitted as the candidate's own on such a task is a Code of Conduct breach that can cost an offer. Practically: light editing/grammar assistance is fine and needs no disclosure; if AI is used to help structure or draft substantive reasoning in the answer, FIL's own rules say that should be disclosed, and an answer that is not genuinely the candidate's reasoning is the exact thing the Code targets.

Wider market context (search-only, treat as [Reported]/anecdotal — see confidence notes in the source table):
- Concern about AI misrepresentation in graduate applications rose sharply: the Institute of Student Employers' Student Development Survey found roughly two-thirds of employers (about 67% for graduates, 66% for school/college leavers) now worried that AI use in selection leads candidates to misrepresent their abilities, up from roughly half in 2025 [T244, Reported — picked up via aggregated search summary, not independently fetched at source; treat as directionally reliable but re-verify the exact percentage before quoting it in a final report].
- Estimates that a majority of graduates now use AI somewhere in their applications (with a meaningful minority using it to "produce [answers] from scratch") appear in the same cluster of 2026 graduate-recruitment commentary [T244/T245, Reported, single-source-cluster, unverified primary survey — flagged as a gap].
- Some employers have responded by moving away from take-home/asynchronous written tasks toward live, real-time exercises specifically because AI-generated written answers are now common and hard to tell apart from genuine ones at scale [T245, Reported].
- Multiple recruiter-facing commentary pieces (Forbes Councils, Hirelytica, HuffPost) describe "tells" of AI-written applications: generic buzzwords without concrete examples, no specific/idiosyncratic detail, inflated or vague claims, and formulaic structure [T254, Reported/anecdotal — these are largely SEO/vendor-content pieces rather than primary research, so treat the specific "91% of recruiters can spot it" style statistics as unverified vendor claims, not confirmed facts].
- Net practical implication for the candidate: whatever the precise industry-wide numbers, the direction of travel is unambiguous and FIL's own Code of Conduct confirms it institutionally — a generic, could-be-anyone answer is now actively screened against, not just weak on merit. Specificity (a real theme the candidate can defend), a personal voice, and idiosyncratic detail are now themselves selection criteria, not just "nice to have" polish.

## 1.5 Weighting — reasoning, marked [Inferred]

No firm, including FIL, publishes how it weights individual application questions, and the brief is right not to invent one. What can be reasoned from FIL's own materials:

- **Q1 tests two skills simultaneously**: (a) commercial/industry awareness (can the candidate identify a real, relevant theme and explain why an investor should care), and (b) the ability to translate that into language a client without a financial background could understand. The Investment Directing job description — independently corroborated across three separate listings/snippets for the Equities, Fixed Income and Multi-Asset variants of the role, all using near-identical language — states the team is responsible for "representing the [asset class] group to prospective and existing clients, reporting on portfolios, promoting thought leadership, creating new investment capabilities to meet investors' demands, and generating marketing material to support sales activity" [T240 fetched directly for Fixed Income; T241/T259 corroborating snippets for Equities/Multi-Asset variants, Reported]. The entry-level/intern version of this, per FIL's own early-careers language, is that interns "prepare core marketing presentations and reporting materials to support sales activities" and the team combines "strong knowledge of markets and Fidelity's portfolio strategies" with "a capacity to communicate these in different formats, including through direct client relationships" [T259, Reported — consistent across multiple independent listings of the same posting].
- **This is a near-exact match to Q1's second half.** "Generating marketing material," "reporting on portfolios," and "direct client relationships" are all, functionally, the skill of taking an investment view or a portfolio outcome and explaining it to an audience that is not always technically expert (a client, a distributor, sometimes a trustee board with no investment background) — exactly what Q1's "explain to a client without a financial background" clause asks the candidate to demonstrate. [Inferred: this makes Q1 the more job-specific, higher-signal question for Investment Directing specifically, as opposed to for a portfolio-management or research-analyst intern posting where a pure stock-picking or valuation question would be more job-specific.]
- **Q2 tests structured investment reasoning (risk and time horizon), not client communication.** It is a reasonable proxy for "understanding of what we do" — the FAQ-stated bar FIL sets instead of pre-existing technical skill [T231] — but it is not a duty the Investment Directing team performs itself (setting client asset allocation is typically a portfolio-management/multi-asset function; Investment Directing explains and reports on decisions others make). [Inferred]
- **Conclusion [Inferred]:** Q1 is plausibly the higher-priority question for this specific role because it is the only one of the two that maps directly onto a named duty in the job description ("communicate... through direct client relationships," "generating marketing material," "reporting on portfolios"), and because the "explain to a client without a financial background" clause is close to a direct writing-sample test of the actual day-one work. Q2 functions more as a baseline literacy/curiosity filter — confirming the candidate has basic, correctly-reasoned investment knowledge (which the firm says it wants some minimum "understanding of," even while explicitly not requiring pre-existing technical skill) — than as a role-specific competency test. This does not mean Q2 can be weak; a rubric-based sift almost certainly still scores it, and a bad Q2 answer (e.g., putting long-term money in cash, or short-term money in equities, revealing no grasp of risk/horizon) would likely be a hard fail regardless of a strong Q1. But if the candidate has to choose where marginal effort delivers the most signal about role-fit specifically, that is Q1's client-explanation half.

## 1.6 The "explain to a client without a financial background" skill

This is treated seriously per the brief, since it is judged the single most role-relevant thing being tested.

**Regulatory anchor — FCA Consumer Duty, consumer understanding outcome.** The Consumer Duty's consumer-understanding outcome is set out in the FCA Handbook at PRIN 2A.5, with supporting guidance in Chapter 8 of Finalised Guidance FG22/5 [T237/T238/T253, Confirmed the outcome exists and its location; the granular FG22/5 text itself could not be cleanly extracted from the PDF — see GAPS]. The FCA's **March 2026 good-and-poor-practice review of consumer understanding** (fetched directly) is a genuinely useful, concrete guide to what "good" plain-English financial communication looks like in FCA's own recent assessment of the market [T237, Confirmed]:

Good practice, verbatim from the FCA:
- "plain language, clear structure, visual hierarchy and layered content"
- firms using "short summaries, clear definitions, QR-linked explanations and clear signposting"
- "testing communications in a proportionate way, using methods such as short surveys, comprehension checks, A/B testing"
- placing "essential information upfront," highlighting "risks and exclusions early"
- avoiding jargon and giving "risks equal prominence to benefits"

Poor practice, verbatim from the FCA:
- "testing was often superficial, one-off or poorly documented"
- "cosmetic changes, shorter wording, new icons or colour changes without improving clarity, sequencing or prominence" — i.e., firms sometimes make communications *look* simpler without actually making them clearer
- "overlong documents with limited signposting" where "customers were expected to locate critical information without support"
- firms that "committed to using jargon-free and intelligible language but had limited evidence to show this had been embedded" — i.e., saying you'll be plain-English is not the same as being plain-English

**The FCA's general plain-language rule**: firms must "use plain and intelligible language and, where use of jargon or technical terms is unavoidable, explain the meaning of any jargon or technical terms as simply as possible" [T237/T253, Confirmed via FCA-focused legal/regulatory commentary corroborating the Handbook rule]. The FCA also explicitly weighs *how much* testing/care is proportionate to the stakes of the communication — "the purpose of the communication," "the relative importance of that decision," and "whether the scope for harm... is likely to be significant" [T253, Reported].

**Readability measures.** The Flesch Reading Ease formula scores text 0–100 (higher = easier), driven by average sentence length and average syllables per word; commonly cited bands are roughly 90–100 "very easy" (around a US 5th-grade level), 60–70 "standard"/"plain English" (roughly a US 8th–9th-grade level, i.e. an average adult reader), and below 30 "very difficult"/academic [T247, Reported — aggregated from readability-tool vendor sites, directionally standard and widely cited but not a primary academic source]. The practical takeaway most consistently repeated: **aim for roughly 15–20 words per sentence on average**, since sentence length is described as "the biggest driver" of the score [T247, Reported]. Some regulated industries (insurance is the most commonly cited) have historically been required or strongly encouraged to meet a minimum Flesch score for policy documents [T247, Reported].

**What to avoid.** A specialist investment-writing source (fetched directly) lists commonly over-used jargon that should not appear unexplained in writing for "regular folks": terms like "accommodative monetary policy," "alpha," "basis points," "duration," "drawdown," "leverage," "risk premium," and warns specifically that a word like "correction" "means something different to individuals than to investment professionals" — i.e. even everyday-sounding financial words can silently carry a technical meaning the lay reader will misread [T239, Confirmed]. Its core prescriptions: "replace jargon and long words with shorter, less technical words that pack more punch," "define your terms using plain language," and "use analogies, where possible, because they'll stick in your readers' minds better than dry explanations" [T239, Confirmed]. Separately, general commentary on adviser-client communication reports that clients "trust advisers who explain things in natural language rather than with extensive jargon," and that going deep into technical detail (e.g. citing Monte Carlo simulations or alpha/beta) "risks making clients feel stupid, discounted, or patronized" [T256, Reported — generalist source, directionally consistent with FCA and Plain English Campaign guidance but not a primary/regulatory source].

**Plain English Campaign-style principles**, consistently repeated across general plain-English writing guides: prefer the simpler everyday word over the technical synonym (their own example: don't use "equities" when "shares" will do); use active voice; cut unnecessary words; use headings/short paragraphs to aid scanning [T246, Reported — the Campaign's own dedicated financial-jargon examples page returned a 404 on direct fetch, so this is via a general secondary summary, not the primary page itself — see GAPS].

**Analogy and concrete numbers.** Widely repeated technique across general finance-communication sources: reach for a everyday physical analogy for an abstract mechanism (e.g., diversification ≈ "don't put all your eggs in one basket"; compounding ≈ a "savings snowball" that grows on itself) [T256, Reported — generic/SEO-quality sources, use as illustrative technique only, not as a citable authority]. The more rigorous, FCA-endorsed version of "concrete" is not decorative analogy but **concrete, testable comprehension**: the FCA rewards firms that actually check understanding (comprehension calls, A/B tests) over firms that merely claim clarity [T237, Confirmed]. For a 200-word answer this cannot be replicated, but the implied standard is the same: a good explanation should be one a genuinely non-financial reader could repeat back correctly, not one that merely sounds simple.

**What specifically to avoid, synthesised across all of the above:**
- Unexplained jargon (even "everyday-sounding" jargon like "correction" or "equities").
- False precision — presenting a made-up level of confidence or a specific number/forecast where the underlying reasoning is actually a broad directional judgement (the general communications sources flagged this concern; I did not find an FCA-specific "false precision" ruling to cite directly here — see GAPS).
- Condescension — "dumbing down" content in a way that a reader who is not a fool but simply not a specialist can perceive and resent; the FCA explicitly criticised firms that made "cosmetic" simplification (new icons, shorter wording) without improving actual clarity, sequencing, or the substance of what is being conveyed [T237, Confirmed].
- Long sentences and long words generally — the direct drivers of a poor readability score [T247, Reported].

## 1.7 Word limits: how to use 200 words and 150 words well [Inferred throughout — reasoning, not sourced facts]

No source gives a percentage breakdown for a "theme / relevance / explain to a client" structure inside a 200-word cap, so this is my own reasoning from the material above, clearly marked [Inferred]:

- **Q1 (200 words), suggested rough proportions:** since the "explain to a client without a financial background" clause is (per 1.5) the highest-leverage, most job-specific part of the question, and is also the hardest thing to do well in a small space, it plausibly deserves the largest share of the word budget — roughly the *theme/news* itself should be told tightly (concise: what happened, when, one or two concrete facts — no more than is needed to establish it's real and recent), the *relevance to an investor* should be a clear, short causal link (why this matters to portfolios/asset classes/rates/etc.), and the *client explanation* should get the most space and the most deliberate plain-language construction, since it is simultaneously (a) the part most explicitly asked for by name in the question, (b) the part that FIL's own job description most directly rewards, and (c) the part where a generic/AI-flavoured answer is easiest to spot (a lazy answer skips or rushes this clause). [Inferred]
- **Q2 (150 words):** the question has an explicit two-part structure built into it — "(a) short term and (b) the long term" — so the natural and expected structure is one that visibly answers both parts with an asset class and a stated rationale for each, tied explicitly to risk and time horizon (the brief notes the standard framework: shorter horizons favour capital preservation/liquidity — cash, short-dated bonds/money-market instruments; longer horizons can tolerate volatility for higher expected return — equities and other growth assets) [T255, Reported — this is standard, uncontroversial investment theory, not FIL-specific]. At 150 words there is very little room for hedging or caveats; the better use of space is likely to be one crisp reason per allocation choice rather than several partial ones. [Inferred]
- **Should the full word allowance be used?** General graduate-application advice (not FIL-specific) says candidates are not required to hit the exact maximum, but writing well under the limit "means you've probably missed some key points," and the safer failure mode is to write too little rather than pad [T249, Reported]. Given how tight 200 and 150 words already are for what is being asked (a news story *and* its relevance *and* a client explanation; or two full time-horizon justified allocations), it is unlikely a strong answer would naturally come in far short of the cap — 90–100% use of the limit is the more probable signature of a complete answer. [Inferred]
- **Bullet points or prose?** General job-application-form advice is mixed and largely resume-specific (bullets aid scanning, but many online form text boxes don't render true bullet indentation, so a pasted "•" symbol can look messy) [T258, Reported]. For Q1 specifically, prose is very likely the better choice regardless of formatting mechanics, because Q1 is itself a test of written communication ability — turning the answer into a bulleted list undercuts the very skill being assessed (explaining fluidly to a lay reader). For Q2, a lightly labelled two-part structure that clearly separates "(a) short term" from "(b) long term" (which the question itself explicitly frames as two sub-parts) is reasonable and likely to aid a time-pressed reader without looking like a decorative list — this is different from bullet-pointing decorative sub-claims within each part. [Inferred]

# 6. HOW THIS HELPS THE CANDIDATE ANSWER (analysis angles, not drafted prose)

- **Treat Q1's "explain to a client without a financial background" clause as the highest-value sentence in either question.** It is the one thing in the whole application that maps almost word-for-word onto the Investment Directing job description's actual duties (marketing material, portfolio reporting, direct client relationships). Spend disproportionate drafting effort here, and stress-test it by reading it aloud to someone with zero finance background — if they can't repeat back, in their own words, what the theme means for an investor, the explanation has failed the test FIL itself uses internally (FCA-endorsed "comprehension check," T237).
- **Pick a theme deliberately, not just the first news story that comes to mind.** Given that "Curiosity" is a named FIL strength evidenced specifically by "following industry news" (T233), the choice of theme is itself part of what's being graded — an obviously asset-management-relevant story (something that touches a fund flow, a rate decision, a regulatory shift, a company/portfolio-relevant development) will read as more "curious about the industry" than a purely macro/consumer-news item with no obvious investor angle.
- **Use the FCA's own definition of "good" as a private checklist**, even though the candidate will never cite the FCA in a 200-word answer: plain language, no jargon left unexplained, essential point stated early not buried, risk/uncertainty given honest weight rather than glossed over, and no purely cosmetic simplification (i.e. don't just swap one hard word for another hard-sounding-but-simple word without actually making the underlying idea clear).
- **Run a rough readability sanity check before submitting**: shorter sentences (aim well under 20 words on average), and read for any word from the "avoid" list in 1.6 (alpha, basis points, duration, drawdown, leverage, risk premium, and even "correction," which is deceptively everyday-sounding) — if any appear, either cut them or define them in the same sentence in plain terms.
- **For Q2, lead with the risk/time-horizon logic explicitly, not just the asset classes.** Because "attention to detail" is one of the two behaviours most plausibly mapped onto this question, the differentiator is less "which assets did you pick" and more "did you correctly and explicitly justify each choice by reference to time horizon and risk" — the question is testing whether the candidate understands *why* short and long horizons call for different allocations, not just whether they can name asset classes.
- **Assume both answers will be read back at interview.** Since this is well corroborated as standard practice specifically for asset-management applications (T249, T251, both independently telling candidates to keep a copy for interview review), the candidate should not write anything in either answer they could not immediately expand on, unprompted, with more detail or a follow-up example in a live conversation — including being able to explain, if asked, exactly how the answer was produced (i.e., that it reflects their own reasoning), given FIL's AI Code of Conduct explicitly makes "originality, reasoning or reflection" the standard being assessed on tasks like these.
- **If AI tools are used at all in preparing the answer, treat FIL's own Code of Conduct as the line, not general internet practice.** Grammar/spelling/structure assistance is explicitly fine and needs no disclosure; using AI to generate the actual reasoning or content of the answer is the category FIL says must be disclosed, and passing off AI-generated reasoning as personal capability is the category FIL says can cost an offer. The safest posture, and the one most consistent with what is actually being tested (per 1.4 and 1.5), is for the reasoning and the specific example/theme chosen to be genuinely the candidate's own, checked for grammar/clarity afterward rather than generated from a prompt.
- **On length**: given the tight caps, plan to use close to the full 200/150 words rather than stopping early — the general graduate-advice finding that going well under a limit signals missed content applies with more force here because both questions have multiple explicit sub-asks (theme + relevance + client explanation; short-term allocation + long-term allocation) that are hard to cover with real substance in noticeably fewer words than allowed.
- **On format**: write Q1 as prose — it is a demonstration of communication skill, not just content — and structure Q2 with a light two-part shape that mirrors the question's own "(a)... (b)..." framing, without resorting to decorative bullet lists that could read as under-developed reasoning.
