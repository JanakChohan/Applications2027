# T2_EVERGREEN — Evergreen / semi-liquid funds: what they are and why they can break
Research file. Source IDs T50–T77 (assigned range T50–T99). Accessed 2026-09-18 unless stated.

# 1. FINDINGS

## 1.1 What an evergreen fund is, defined against the two things it sits between

Start with the two poles, because "evergreen" only makes sense as a compromise between them.

**Pole A — the traditional closed-end private markets drawdown fund.** A private equity, private credit or infrastructure fund raised as a limited partnership with a fixed life, usually about ten years. Investors do not hand over cash on day one. They make a "commitment" and the manager "calls" capital in tranches as it finds deals (a capital call). Money comes back only when assets are sold or refinanced (a distribution). There is no redemption mechanism at all: an investor who wants out before the fund winds up must sell their stake to another investor on a secondary market, usually at a discount, with the manager's consent. This structure has no liquidity mismatch because it makes no liquidity promise — the fund is illiquid and says so [Inferred].

**Pole B — the open-ended daily-dealing mutual fund / UK OEIC (open-ended investment company).** Investors buy and sell units every business day at a Net Asset Value (NAV) struck once per day. The fund promises same-day-or-next-day-cash liquidity. This works because the fund's rules (UCITS in the EU, similar retail-fund rules in the UK) restrict what it can hold — mostly listed, exchange-traded securities that can genuinely be sold within the redemption window. The liquidity promise and the liquidity of the assets are supposed to match [Inferred].

**The evergreen / semi-liquid middle ground.** An evergreen fund (industry uses "evergreen" and "semi-liquid" roughly interchangeably) is built to hold the illiquid assets of Pole A — private credit, private equity, infrastructure, real estate — inside a structure that behaves partly like Pole B. Its defining features, consistently described across every source consulted [T56][T57][T58][T59][T60]:
- **Perpetual life.** No fixed 10-year end date. The fund keeps operating indefinitely.
- **Continuous (or periodic) subscriptions.** New investors can typically buy in monthly, sometimes daily, at NAV — unlike a drawdown fund's finite fundraising window.
- **Periodic, capped redemptions, not daily dealing.** Investors can ask for money back only at set points — usually quarterly — and only up to a cap, commonly around 5% of the fund's net asset value in that quarter [T57][T58][T59][T60].
- **NAV-based pricing**, not a market price. There is no stock-exchange listing, so there is no independently observable trading price the way there is for a listed closed-end fund's shares. The fund's own valuation (marked usually monthly or quarterly) is the only price [Inferred from T59][T60].
- **No listing / no secondary market as the primary exit route.** Unlike a listed closed-end fund (which trades on an exchange and can be sold instantly, just at a possible discount to NAV), the evergreen fund's built-in redemption facility is the exit route [Inferred].

This is why an evergreen fund is best explained to a non-financial audience as: **"a fund that lets you put money in almost any time, but only lets you take a limited slice of it out every few months, because what it actually owns — loans to companies, stakes in unlisted businesses, buildings — can't itself be sold quickly or cheaply."**

## 1.2 The mechanics that matter

**Why 5%, and where does it come from?** The number is best understood in two layers.

Layer one — the actual US regulatory anchor. SEC Rule 23c-3 under the Investment Company Act of 1940 governs "interval funds" (a specific, registered fund type — see 1.3). It permits repurchase offers only at fixed periodic intervals of 3, 6 or 12 months, and requires each repurchase offer to cover **no less than 5% and no more than 25%** of the fund's outstanding shares [T57]. In practice, the overwhelming majority of interval funds pick the regulatory floor — 5% every quarter — because offering more invites larger, harder-to-fund redemption events [T57]. This is a real, binding rule for the subset of vehicles legally structured as Rule 23c-3 interval funds.

Layer two — market convention beyond that specific rule. Non-traded BDCs (a different, related-but-distinct US structure — see 1.3) are not governed by Rule 23c-3 at all; their statutory anchor is a different provision (Section 61(a)(2)(D) of the Investment Company Act, on tender offers around fee-structure votes) [T67, SNIPPET]. Yet the dominant contractual practice among non-traded BDCs and among unregistered institutional "evergreen" private credit and private equity vehicles (Delaware LPs, Luxembourg RAIFs, etc., which are not SEC-registered funds at all and so face no federal ceiling on redemptions) is also "5% of NAV per quarter, ~20% per year" [T58][T60]. This convergence on 5% outside the funds that are actually bound by Rule 23c-3 is best read as an **industry norm that borrowed the number from the regulatory floor, not a rule that applies to them directly** [Inferred] — candidates should be precise that "5% is regulatory" is only true for the narrow category of registered interval funds; for BDCs, LTAFs, ELTIFs and private evergreen vehicles it is a voluntarily chosen, prospectus-disclosed contractual term, set by the fund's own board/manager.

**Pro-rata allocation.** Every source describing the mechanism agrees on the same rule: if redemption requests in a quarter exceed the cap, the fund does not pay first-come-first-served. It pays out **pro rata** — each redeeming investor gets the same proportion of what they asked for [T57][T58][T60]. Example logic: if the cap is 5% of NAV and requests total 20% of NAV, each investor gets roughly one-quarter of what they requested (5/20).

**Do unmet requests roll forward, or must they be resubmitted?** This varies by structure and is the detail that most separates products, so candidates should not over-generalise. Descriptions of typical evergreen fund documentation say that unmet amounts commonly **roll forward automatically** to the next redemption window without the investor needing to resubmit [T60, description of "typical" fund documentation — Reported, single describable source, not a specific named fund's legal text]. Some non-traded BDC and interval-fund prospectuses instead require investors to resubmit a fresh redemption request each quarter — this candidate should flag in an interview that the exact treatment is fund-specific and is one of the first things to check in any given prospectus [Inferred].

**The liquidity sleeve.** Evergreen funds do not hold 100% illiquid assets. They keep a buffer — cash, short-dated instruments, undrawn credit lines, sometimes secondary-market (already-traded) positions — specifically to fund the quarterly redemption cap without having to sell the illiquid core [T60]. As a live, sourced example: Partners Group said its Global Value fund holds liquidity of around 15% of NAV plus a further undrawn credit facility of about 15% [T63/T66, Reported — reported in press coverage of the June 2026 gating episode, not a document Claude read directly]. The trade-off is explicit: this buffer is invested more conservatively than the fund's core private-market book, which drags down the blended return [T60].

**Subscription inflows as a redemption-funding source — "new money pays the leavers" — and its fragility.** New investor cash coming into an evergreen fund flows into the same liquidity sleeve used to pay departing investors [T60]. This is efficient when the fund is growing: continuous inflows top up the buffer, and the manager rarely needs to touch the illiquid core to fund redemptions. It is fragile for an obvious reason that generalises far beyond finance: **it depends on new subscriptions continuing to arrive.** If sentiment turns — because of a credit-market scare, a marked-down valuation, bad headlines about a peer fund — subscriptions can slow or stop at exactly the moment redemption requests rise. The fund then has two shrinking numbers (inflows down, sleeve draining) and one growing number (requests), which is precisely the dynamic behind the 2026 wave of private-credit and evergreen-PE fund gatings that recur throughout this research [T58][T63][T64][T66].

**Valuation frequency and NAV striking.** Because there is no daily market price, evergreen funds strike NAV periodically — monthly is common for private credit funds, quarterly is more typical in real assets and private equity vehicles [Inferred from general descriptions in T58/T60, not one specific document]. Between valuation dates, subscriptions and redemptions both transact at the last struck NAV.

## 1.3 Vehicle types by jurisdiction

**US non-traded BDC (Business Development Company).** A BDC is a US closed-end investment company, registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940, whose statutory purpose is to lend to and invest in US small and mid-sized businesses. A "non-traded" BDC raises money continuously (often through wealth-management/broker-dealer channels) but its shares are not listed on an exchange. Liquidity to investors is provided only through a discretionary quarterly share repurchase program, most commonly capped at 5% of NAV per quarter (about 20% per year) [T58]. Crucially, the board is not legally obliged to repurchase any amount at all in a given quarter and can suspend or reduce the programme if it "would place an undue burden on liquidity" [T67, SNIPPET from search of SEC filings language]. This is a key distinction from the Rule 23c-3 interval fund below: the BDC repurchase is discretionary by design, not a fixed regulatory entitlement.

**US interval funds (Rule 23c-3).** Registered closed-end funds under the Investment Company Act of 1940 that commit, in their own fundamental policy, to make periodic repurchase offers at fixed intervals (3, 6 or 12 months) of between 5% and 25% of shares outstanding [T57]. Unlike the BDC's discretionary buyback, this is a binding fund policy filed with the SEC — the fund cannot simply choose to skip a scheduled repurchase offer the way a BDC board can decline one [T57][Inferred distinction].

**US tender offer funds.** Also registered closed-end funds, but instead of committing to a fixed Rule 23c-3 schedule, the board decides at its discretion when and how large a tender offer to make, executed under the general tender-offer rules of the Securities Exchange Act (Rule 13e-4) rather than Rule 23c-3's fixed grid [search result summary, T57-adjacent, Reported].

**European ELTIF (European Long-Term Investment Fund) and ELTIF 2.0.** ELTIF is an EU fund label (not a separate legal entity type) created to channel retail and institutional capital into long-term illiquid assets across the EU single market. Reform "ELTIF 2.0" (Regulation (EU) 2023/606) took effect from January 2024, lowering the minimum share of the portfolio that must be in "eligible" long-term assets, removing a prior retail minimum-investment threshold, and — most relevant here — permitting managers to build in optional periodic redemption windows rather than the original all-or-nothing "closed until maturity" design. Where a redemption facility is offered, the default expectation under the framework is a maximum quarterly dealing frequency and a minimum notice period of around 12 months, with the amount redeemable in a window linked to how much of the portfolio is genuinely liquid [T68, SNIPPET — search-result summary of law-firm commentary, not a primary EU text Claude fetched directly; treat percentages/notice period as Reported, not Confirmed, pending a direct read of the Regulation or ESMA RTS].

**UK LTAF (Long-Term Asset Fund).** See 1.4 below — this is researched to primary-source depth.

**Luxembourg Part II fund / RAIF (Reserved Alternative Investment Fund).** Two different Luxembourg wrappers frequently used for evergreen structures. A "Part II" fund (named for Part II of Luxembourg's 2010 law on undertakings for collective investment) is a fund that can invest in essentially any asset type, qualifies as an Alternative Investment Fund (AIF), and — unlike RAIFs — is directly authorised and supervised by Luxembourg's regulator, the CSSF; it can be marketed to retail as well as institutional investors [T69, SNIPPET]. A RAIF is not itself directly supervised by the CSSF (it is "indirectly" regulated because its manager, the AIFM, must be authorised) which makes RAIFs faster and cheaper to launch, but they are correspondingly restricted to "well-informed" (broadly, professional/sophisticated/qualifying) investors rather than the mass retail market [T69, SNIPPET]. Both wrappers are used to build "evergreen" private equity, private credit and real asset vehicles with contractual redemption gates and notice periods set by the fund documents rather than a single statutory percentage [T69, SNIPPET].

## 1.4 The UK LTAF in detail

**Creation.** The FCA created the LTAF regime via Policy Statement **PS21/14**, "A new authorised fund regime for investing in long term assets," published **25 October 2021**; the Handbook rules came into force **15 November 2021** [T50]. It is an open-ended, UK-authorised fund category purpose-built to hold long-term, illiquid assets — venture capital, private equity, private debt, real estate and infrastructure — inside a regulated wrapper rather than an offshore or unauthorised one [T50][T56].

**Dealing frequency and notice period.** LTAFs cannot offer daily dealing. FCA rules set a **minimum 90-day notice period for redemptions** and cap dealing frequency at **no more frequently than monthly** — both are floors, and many LTAFs choose longer notice periods and less frequent dealing than the minimum [T56]. Managers must set the fund's actual redemption terms "consistent with the fund's liquidity profile" — the regulator sets the floor, not the exact number [T56][Inferred wording].

**Who can invest, and how that changed.** At launch under PS21/14 (2021), the LTAF was aimed at "professional investors... like pension providers and trustees of defined contribution or hybrid pension schemes, and sophisticated or wealthy investors" [T50]. The FCA explicitly flagged an intention to consult on widening this. That consultation produced **PS23/7**, "Broadening retail access to the long-term asset fund," published **29 June 2023**, with the final rules recategorising an LTAF unit from a "Non-Mass Market Investment" (NMMI) to a "Restricted Mass Market Investment" (RMMI) [T51]. That recategorisation is what actually let LTAFs be distributed more broadly — to mass-market retail investors, to self-select defined-contribution (DC) pension schemes, and to Self-Invested Personal Pensions (SIPPs) — subject to RMMI safeguards such as mandatory risk warnings, an appropriateness assessment, and (for unadvised retail investors) a self-certification that they are not putting more than 10% of net assets into this category of higher-risk investment [T51].

**FCA's stated rationale on liquidity mismatch.** The regulator's own language, from the joint FCA/Bank of England review that preceded and informed this whole programme of work (statement of 16 December 2019), frames the core design principle plainly: the liquidity of a fund's assets should be judged "by reference to the price discount needed for a quick sale," redemption prices paid to leavers should reflect "the discount needed to sell the required portion of a fund's assets in the specified redemption notice period," and the redemption notice period itself should be long enough to match "the time needed to sell the required portion of a fund's assets without discounts beyond those captured" in the price [T53]. In plain terms: **the notice period and dealing frequency of a fund should be built backwards from how long it actually takes to sell what the fund owns, not forwards from what would be most attractive to sell to a customer.** The 90-day/monthly floor for the LTAF is a direct regulatory expression of that principle [Inferred link between T53's stated principle and T50/T56's later specific numbers].

## 1.5 The liquidity mismatch problem — the core intellectual content

The mechanism, stated simply: an evergreen (or any open-ended) fund promises to convert an investor's holding into cash faster than the fund itself can convert its underlying assets into cash, at the price the fund is claiming those assets are worth. Every serious regulator addressing this over the past decade converges on the same three linked ideas.

**1. First-mover advantage.** The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee states it directly: "there is an advantage to investors who redeem ahead of others, particularly in a stress" [T52]. If a fund pays leavers at a NAV that has not yet been marked down for the true cost of selling illiquid assets quickly, the investor who gets out first is paid too much relative to the fund's real liquidation value, and the cost of that overpayment falls on whoever is still in the fund when the truth catches up. This creates a rational incentive to redeem early — not because you think the manager is dishonest, but because you know other investors are thinking the same thing, and being second or third makes you worse off than being first. The IMF describes the same mechanism from the systemic-risk angle: open-end funds "that offer daily redemptions while holding illiquid assets can amplify the effects of adverse shocks by raising the likelihood of investor runs and asset fire sales," because "the price paid to investors may not fully reflect all trading costs associated with the assets they sold" [T54-adjacent language via IMF sourcing, note: primary IMF blog page returned 403 on direct fetch, so this is Reported via search-engine summary of the IMF's own text, not a page Claude read in full — treat as Reported not Confirmed].

**2. Why the gate is both protective and self-fulfilling.** A cap on redemptions exists to stop the fund being forced to dump its most saleable assets at fire-sale prices just to pay leavers, which would harm everyone who stays [T60][T65]. But the very existence — or announcement — of a gate can itself trigger more redemption requests: investors who were on the fence rush to submit a request the moment they hear a gate might bind, precisely because they know that once it binds, pro-rata allocation means anyone who waits gets less of what they ask for. This is the self-fulfilling part: the protective mechanism and the panic trigger are the same event, seen from two sides [Inferred, consistent with first-mover-advantage literature in T52/T54].

**3. The valuation question.** If a fund's illiquid assets are marked to a model (an internal estimate) rather than an observable trading price, and that model is slow to reflect deteriorating conditions, redeeming investors can cash out at a NAV that is more generous than the assets would actually fetch if sold — at the expense of investors who remain and eventually absorb the write-down [T58, drawing on the reported BlackRock example of a private loan written from 100 cents to zero within three months, illustrating how fast a "stale" mark can unravel; Reported]. Multiple sources make the same point from the demand side: once investors suspect marks are stale, the rational move is to submit a redemption request today, before the NAV catches down tomorrow — which itself accelerates the redemption wave the marks were supposedly protecting against [T58].

**Regulatory and central-bank record on this.** The FSB's December 2023 revised policy recommendations state the structural vulnerability squarely: "A key structural vulnerability from asset management activities is the potential mismatch between the liquidity of fund investments and daily redemption of fund units in open-ended funds," and calls for "the redemption terms that open-ended funds offer to investors" to "be based on the liquidity of their asset holdings in normal and stressed market conditions" [T54]. IOSCO's parallel guidance on "anti-dilution liquidity management tools" (swing pricing, dual pricing, anti-dilution levies, redemption fees) is designed explicitly "to mitigate material investor dilution and potential first-mover advantage arising from structural liquidity mismatch," by passing the actual cost of trading onto the investor who is transacting rather than spreading it across everyone who stays [T62, SNIPPET from search summary of IOSCO's own guidance language].

**The UK precedent everyone in this interview should know: Woodford.** The LF Woodford Equity Income Fund was a daily-dealing, FCA-authorised retail fund (not technically an "evergreen" vehicle, but the textbook UK case of the liquidity mismatch problem) that suspended dealing on **3 June 2019**. The FCA's investigation (fines announced **5 August 2025**, nearly £46m combined against Neil Woodford personally and Woodford Investment Management) found that between July 2018 and June 2019 the manager "made unreasonable and inappropriate investment decisions," systematically selling the fund's more liquid holdings and buying less liquid ones — so that by the time of suspension **only 8% of the fund's investments could be sold within seven days**, against a daily-redemption promise [T55]. Fund value had fallen from over £10.1bn (May 2017) to £3.6bn immediately before suspension [T55]. The redress scheme is expected to provide up to approximately £235m in compensation [search summary]. The lesson the FCA drew, and has since built into rulemaking, is exactly the mismatch principle above: a fund cannot honestly promise liquidity terms that are faster than its actual portfolio supports.

**The property fund precedents (2016 and 2020).** Following the June 2016 Brexit referendum, several of the UK's largest open-ended commercial property funds — holding roughly £15–18bn in aggregate across the affected funds — suspended dealing within days because they held physical buildings that cannot be sold in the time frame their daily/near-daily dealing promised, while investors rushed to redeem on fears of falling property values [search summary]. The FCA's own account of the resulting rule change (Policy Statement PS19/24, published **30 September 2019**, rules effective **30 September 2020**) states the causal link explicitly: "Funds which hold less liquid assets can encounter liquidity difficulties if significant numbers of investors simultaneously try to withdraw their money at short notice, as seen shortly after the EU referendum in 2016 when a number of property funds had to suspend dealing," adding that "in stressed market conditions, assets that are less liquid can also suffer from a high degree of valuation uncertainty" [T61]. The same structural weakness recurred in March 2020: at the start of the Covid-19 pandemic, property fund valuers invoked "material valuation uncertainty" clauses, and the FCA's own rule (introduced after 2016) required funds to suspend dealing once valuation was materially uncertain for 20% or more of fund assets — which several major UK property funds (including Aegon/Kames Property Income and Janus Henderson UK Property) did from **16 March 2020** [search summary].

## 1.6 Why the gate is arguably working as designed — the counter-argument, sourced

This is a live, on-the-record debate, not a hypothetical. In **June 2026**, Partners Group — a large Swiss listed private-markets manager — capped redemptions on its Global Value evergreen private-equity fund at the standard 5% of NAV per quarter after redemption requests reportedly reached around 9.8% of NAV, and signalled it might do the same on other evergreen vehicles; Partners Group shares fell sharply (reported around 17% intraday) on the news [T63/T64, Reported via press coverage — Bloomberg/CNBC reporting, not directly fetched in full; treat headline facts as Reported]. This episode produced two clear on-the-record defences of the gate as designed behaviour rather than distress:

- **Eric J. Pan, President & CEO of the Investment Company Institute** (the main US fund-industry trade body), writing in an opinion piece dated 27 April 2026 ("Private Credit Funds Are Working Precisely As Designed To"), argued: "These funds are meant for long-term investing, and they limit redemptions accordingly. Those limits are not a sign of stress — they are a tool to protect investors," and that if such a fund "were structured to offer broad, on-demand liquidity, it would need to hold a much larger share of liquid, publicly-traded securities... reducing its allocation to private credit" — i.e., the whole point of the product would be undone [T65].
- **Nicolas Moura, PitchBook senior analyst for EMEA private capital**, quoted in PitchBook/Yahoo Finance coverage dated around 5 June 2026 on the Partners Group episode: "Evergreen fund gates are often misread as a red flag, but they are precisely the mechanism these structures were designed for: protecting long-term investors when short-term redemption demand temporarily outpaces liquidity" [T66].

The substance of the "working as designed" case, drawn from these sources: (i) the cap exists precisely to stop the manager being forced into fire-sales that would destroy value for everyone who remains, not just the person trying to leave [T60][T65]; (ii) the terms — the 5% cap, the notice period, pro-rata allocation — are set out in the fund's own prospectus/offering documents before an investor ever commits money, so nobody who redeems late is being surprised by a rule that was hidden [T58 background material; T65]; (iii) pro-rata allocation is itself an equity mechanism — it treats a redemption event as a shared, proportionate cost across everyone asking to leave in that window, rather than paying whoever asks first in full and leaving the rest with nothing [T57][T60]. The honest counter-counter-argument, also drawn from research above, is that formal disclosure in a legal prospectus is not the same as an ordinary retail investor genuinely understanding, in advance, what a 5%-cap-with-pro-rata-and-a-90-day-notice actually feels like when they need cash and can't get it — a gap flagged directly in coverage of the private-credit redemption episodes [search summary, T58-adjacent].

# 6. HOW THIS HELPS THE CANDIDATE ANSWER

**For Q1 (theme/article relevance, explain to a non-financial client):**
- This whole topic is a strong Q1 candidate because it is genuinely current (Partners Group gating its evergreen fund made headlines in June 2026, and the "gate cap vs redemption requests" story is recurring across the private-credit sector in 2026 per T58) and it is directly on-thesis for Fidelity International, whose FCA-authorised Diversified Private Assets LTAF and FutureWise default strategy (per the brief) are exactly this kind of vehicle.
- The strongest "explain to a client with no financial background" version leans on the plain analogy already built above: a fund that lets you put money in whenever you like, but only lets you take a slice out every few months, because what it owns takes time to sell. A second, complementary analogy worth developing (not drafted here): a shared taxi versus a private car — daily-dealing funds are like a bus that runs on a fixed, generous timetable because buses are quick to reroute; an evergreen private-assets fund is like a delivery of custom-built furniture — you can order any time, but you can't get a same-day refund because the item has to actually be resold to someone else first.
- A genuinely differentiated answer would go beyond "illiquid assets are risky" and name the specific mechanism: first-mover advantage (T52), the reason pro-rata allocation exists (T57/T60), and why a gate is not automatically bad news (T65/T66) — while also being honest that disclosure in a prospectus is not the same as an ordinary saver understanding what it means in practice.
- Relevance-to-an-investor framing options: (a) an investor who needs to be told, before they buy, exactly what "quarterly, capped, pro-rata" liquidity really means in a stressed market, not just in a calm one; (b) an investor evaluating whether a fund's disclosed liquidity sleeve (e.g., ~15%+15% reported for Partners Group, T63/T66) is genuinely sized for its redemption profile; (c) the industry-wide question of whether pushing more illiquid, semi-liquid product into DC pension defaults (the Mansion House Accord context already in the brief) is appropriate given that DC savers are, by definition, unsophisticated retail investors who may not read a 90-day-notice clause closely.

**For Q2 (£1m allocation, short vs long term):**
- This research is most useful for the long-term bucket. It gives concrete, sourced vocabulary for discussing an allocation to private markets/illiquid assets within a long-horizon portfolio: why an evergreen/LTAF-type vehicle might feature (perpetual exposure, no need to re-commit every 10 years, but real liquidity constraints that must match the investor's own need for cash) versus a traditional 10-year drawdown fund (truer illiquidity, but potentially cheaper/purer exposure and no gate risk because there is no redemption promise to break).
- It also supplies the risk-management vocabulary an examiner would want to see used correctly: NAV-based pricing versus market pricing, notice periods, redemption caps, and liquidity sleeves — useful when explaining why a large sum should not be entirely allocated to illiquid/semi-liquid vehicles even for a long-term horizon (matching an investor's own genuine liquidity needs to the fund's liquidity terms is the same principle regulators apply to funds themselves, per T53).
- For the short-term bucket, this file is not the primary source (short-term allocation should draw on cash/money-market/short-duration fixed income research elsewhere), but the liquidity-mismatch logic is useful as a contrast: whatever is allocated to "cash-like" short-term needs should have genuinely matched — not merely nominal — liquidity, which is the exact test this file shows evergreen funds sometimes fail under stress.
- Caution for Q2: the word-limit (150 words) is very tight, so this material is best used to sharpen precision in a sentence or two on the long-term bucket (e.g., correctly naming why a semi-liquid private-markets allocation carries a liquidity mismatch risk that a public-market allocation does not) rather than to justify a lengthy structural discussion.
