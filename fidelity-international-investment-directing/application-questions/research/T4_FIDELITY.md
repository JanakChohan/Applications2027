### 1.2 The fund itself: asset mix, structure, redemption terms
The Fidelity Diversified Private Assets LTAF invests across **private equity, private credit, infrastructure, real estate and natural resources**, plus a **sleeve of public/listed assets held specifically for liquidity purposes** [T140][T141][T143][Confirmed]. As an LTAF it is legally barred from daily dealing: FCA rules require LTAFs to deal no more frequently than monthly and to give investors a **minimum 90-day redemption notice period**, with many LTAFs choosing notice periods longer than the 90-day floor [T141][T152][T172][Confirmed]. Trustnet specifically describes Fidelity's structure as "up to 90 days for payout," contrasted with daily dealing in conventional open-ended funds, and frames this explicitly as a mechanism designed to **prevent the fund having to "gate" (temporarily suspend) redemptions** the way open-ended property funds have had to in the past [T141][Confirmed]. I could not locate the fund's own factsheet, prospectus or Key Information Document (KID) with the specific fee schedule or minimum investment size — fidelityinternational.com and most of professionals.fidelity.co.uk returned 403 errors to automated fetches, consistent with the brief's warning that the domain is Akamai-blocked [GAP — see Section 5].

### 1.3 The FutureWise integration: how big, how fast

FutureWise's own scale has grown fast: it stood at £16.9bn when the LTAF story first broke, and by May 2026 Fidelity announced it had **passed £25bn in assets**, with a stated ambition to exceed £40bn by 2030 [T144][T145][Reported]. ### 1.4 Manufacture vs. distribute: what the "cease manufacturing" line actually means
I located and directly read the primary document: **FIL Holdings (UK) Limited's Strategic Report for the year ended 31 December 2024**, a group-accounts filing made at UK Companies House on 26 June 2025 (company number 06737476, auditor PricewaterhouseCoopers LLP) [T147][Confirmed — primary source, read in full via OCR of the filed PDF]. This is a UK holding-company entity within the Fidelity International group (it holds FIL Investment Advisors (UK), FIL Pensions Management, Financial Administration Services and the UK platform businesses), not the single global "Fidelity International" annual report — an important distinction to keep straight given the brief's warning about firm identity. The exact text reads:

> "Strategic decisions were made over the course of the year to cease manufacturing of non-core private asset classes in order to pivot resources to areas of unique strength within the ISS business. These areas of priority include active ETFs and the distribution of third-party capabilities for both private assets and select public market investments."

**What this means, worked through carefully:** Fidelity is not saying it is exiting private assets. It is saying it decided to stop *building its own investment teams and products* ("manufacturing") in private-asset categories where it judges it lacks genuine competitive edge, and instead to (a) double down on active ETFs, where it does think it has an edge, and (b) **distribute other managers' ("third-party") private-asset products** rather than run the underlying strategies itself. The Private Markets Partner Network is the direct, visible expression of this: of the ten names in the network, **Fidelity International itself appears only once, and its own contribution is understood to sit in real estate/property** (the one private-asset category the firm has historically managed directly) [T145][Inferred — the article lists Fidelity as one of ten but a search-tool paraphrase attributed the manufacturing split by asset class; treat the exact real-estate attribution as reported, not verified against a primary document]. Private credit, private equity and infrastructure exposure inside the LTAF is sourced from specialist third parties: Ares, Crescent, Churchill (private credit); CVC, Hg, Haveli (private equity); Stonepeak, DigitalBridge (infrastructure) [T145][Confirmed]. **This is the single most important nuance for the candidate's argument**: Fidelity is functioning as an **allocator, packager and distributor** of private credit into the pension default, not as a manufacturer originating and underwriting the loans itself. That is structurally different from a US private-credit manager like Blackstone or Ares running a **non-traded BDC that they themselves originate, price and gate** — Fidelity in this story is one layer removed, more like a fund-of-funds/multi-manager platform. It does not, however, remove all risk transmission: if any of Ares, Crescent or Churchill's underlying private-credit funds face valuation stress or liquidity strain (the exact dynamic hitting the US market in 2025–26), that stress still flows through to FutureWise members via the LTAF, just via an extra intermediation layer.

### 1.5 The DC pension liquidity mechanics — how redemptions actually work inside a default
This is the sharpest and most technical part of the story, and it is genuinely subtle.
- **Members do not "sell" the LTAF the way a wealth client redeems from a fund.** In a DC default, a member's individual "switch" (moving between the scheme's fund options), a full transfer to another pension provider, or drawing benefits at retirement are the three events that could, in principle, require cash to be raised. Members do **not** place a redemption order against the LTAF directly — they interact with the *blended default fund* (FutureWise), which itself holds the LTAF as one sleeve among many liquid public-market sleeves [T140][T143][Inferred from standard LTAF-in-DC-default architecture, corroborated generically by industry commentary, not confirmed against Fidelity's own scheme documentation].
- **The liquidity mismatch is managed at the platform/fund level, not passed through to the member on demand.** Industry commentary on LTAFs-in-DC generally confirms: LTAFs "provide a limited degree of liquidity, usually at quarterly intervals," and typically hold **cash, fixed income and public equity alongside private holdings specifically so they have liquidity on hand to meet redemption requests** without forced-selling illiquid assets [T171][T172][Reported — generic industry description, not Fidelity-specific]. The FCA's original design brief for the LTAF explicitly aimed to solve the "square peg in a round hole" problem that daily-dealt property funds hit in 2016 and 2020, when they had to suspend ("gate") dealing because they could not sell buildings fast enough to meet redemptions [T141][T171][Confirmed].
- **Fiduciary/liquidity risk formally sits with the scheme trustee (for a trust-based scheme) or the platform/product governance body (for a contract-based scheme/master trust), not with the individual member.** The Pensions Regulator (TPR) requires DC trustees to "disclose and explain" their illiquid-asset policy in the Statement of Investment Principles (from 1 October 2023) and published dedicated guidance on private markets investment (24 January 2024) explicitly telling trustees to understand the scheme's own cash-flow and liquidity dynamics, including in stressed markets [T155][T173][Confirmed]. This is the regulatory answer to "who bears the liquidity risk": in the first instance, the governance body managing the default is responsible for making sure the scheme's overall cash flow (inbound contributions, outbound benefit payments and transfers) can absorb the illiquid sleeve without ever needing the LTAF itself to gate.
- **Why DC default cash flows behave differently from wealth-channel money.** A workplace pension default receives a continuous, largely automatic stream of new contributions every payroll cycle from active members (auto-enrolment defaults) — this is "sticky," programmatic inflow, unlike a wealth client's discretionary lump-sum invested in a BDC who can decide en masse to ask for money back. Outflows (retirements, transfers) are also more demographically predictable and can be forecast and pre-funded by the scheme's liquid sleeve. This is the strongest **structural argument that defuses the scary parallel**: a BDC's non-traded-fund clientele can all get nervous about credit conditions at the same moment and submit correlated redemption requests (which is exactly what happened to US BDCs in 2025-26 — see 1.6), whereas most DC-default money is not "redemption-prone" in the same way because most of it isn't there by an active, revisable choice in the first place. This also cuts the other way, though: **because members never chose the LTAF, they cannot vote with their feet even if they wanted to** — that is the "never opted in" framing the brief flags, and it is fair as far as it goes (see Section 6).

### 1.6 The US private-credit redemption stress the story is being compared to
This is well documented and current as of September 2026, and gives the candidate hard numbers for the "requests received vs requests honoured" distinction the brief insists on (Rule 6).
- Across the **12 largest non-traded BDCs**, redemption requests in **Q1 2026** averaged **12.1% of net assets (median 10.1%)** — well above the 5%-of-NAV-per-quarter threshold most of these funds use to trigger gating. Only **53.4% of total requested redemptions were actually honoured** across the group; the rest was deferred/pro-rated [T149][Confirmed — With Intelligence analysis of SEC filings]. Individual fund request rates in Q1 2026 ranged as high as the low-20s to low-40s percent for some Blue Owl vehicles [T149][Reported].
- **Blackstone's flagship non-traded BDC, BCRED (~$77–79bn AUM)**, capped quarterly share repurchases at its standard **5% of NAV** limit after investors requested roughly **10% of shares** in Q2 2026 (announced ~June 2026) and again after a similar ~10% request level in Q3 2026 (announced ~September 2026) — i.e., investors got roughly half of what they asked for, twice in a row [T150][Confirmed — reported by Bloomberg, Reuters-sourced trade press and CNBC; I could not open the Bloomberg or CNBC articles directly (403 errors) so this is PAYWALLED-NOT-READ on the primary outlet, corroborated by Seeking Alpha's news wire summary and multiple secondary trade-press pickups that agree on the figures].
- Fitch Ratings' own assessment (reported June 2026) is more reassuring: the non-traded BDCs it rates have "sufficient liquidity to continue meeting quarterly tenders of up to 5%... under a downside scenario assuming no new equity inflows," with average leverage of 0.85x [T151][Reported].
- The proximate trigger cited by trade press: nervousness following the **First Brands, Tricolor and MFS bankruptcies**, plus fears of an AI-driven "SaaS-pocalypse" hitting software loan valuations [T149][Reported].
- **Important distinguishing detail for the candidate to hold onto:** these are US **non-traded BDCs**, sold mainly through the **US wealth/broker-dealer channel** to individual investors who chose, voluntarily, to buy a specific evergreen private-credit product. Redemption caps here are a contractual feature of the BDC structure (a quarterly tender offer capped at 5%/20% annually) — not a "gate" imposed in an emergency, but a standing structural limit that is now visibly binding because demand to exit exceeds it. This is mechanically similar in spirit to what an LTAF's notice period does (slow outflows to protect the fund and remaining investors) but the *trigger* is different: BDC caps are being hit because sophisticated, opted-in wealth investors are trying to get out en masse; nothing structurally equivalent has happened, or can as easily happen, to a DC-default LTAF sleeve, because DC members are not making an active, correlated decision to exit private credit — see 1.5 and Section 6.

### 1.7 Regulatory backdrop: Consumer Duty, LTAF risk disclosure, and the FCA's valuation review
- LTAFs sold to retail investors are classified as **Restricted Mass Market Investments (RMMIs)**, which under the FCA's 2023 rules (PS23/7) require **prominent risk warnings**, a **mandatory appropriateness assessment** before an unadvised retail client can invest, and confirmation the client will not put more than **10% of net assets** into RMMIs generally [T153][Confirmed]. The FCA specifically revised the warning wording after consultation because it judged the original draft **"overemphasised investment risk"** relative to **liquidity risk**, concluding liquidity — not just volatility — is the thing retail investors most need to understand about an LTAF [T153][Reported].
- The **Consumer Duty's "consumer understanding" outcome** (FCA Handbook PRIN 2A.5) requires firms to use plain, jargon-free language, make key information prominent, and — crucially for a complex, illiquid product — not assume the audience has sophisticated financial knowledge; the FCA's own guidance states the "average consumer has quite poor knowledge of financial services" and complex, long-term products with non-standard features (which an LTAF-in-a-default squarely is) require **extra care**, not standard-form disclosure [T154][Confirmed].
- The **FCA's multi-firm review of private-market valuation practices** was published **5 March 2025**. It surveyed 36 firms managing roughly £3tn in global private assets (about £1tn UK-based) across private equity, venture capital, private debt and infrastructure. It found "many examples of good practice" but also material gaps: **conflicts of interest around valuation were only partly identified** (including conflicts tied to marketing, fund borrowing, asset transfers between funds and redemptions), governance committees were sometimes dominated by the same senior investment staff whose performance the valuations reflect, and **only a minority of firms had defined quantitative triggers for interim/ad hoc revaluations** — meaning valuations can go stale between scheduled marks [T148][Confirmed]. This is directly relevant to the "DC members are being sold private credit whose true value nobody can price cleanly on a given day" strand of the story, and it long predates and is broader than the specific AI/private-credit stress of 2026 — it is a structural, sector-wide finding, not an accusation against Fidelity specifically. There is no evidence I found that Fidelity was named or flagged adversely in this review.

### 1.8 Other UK LTAF players and market size
The UK LTAF market has grown quickly but off a small base and remains dominated by institutional (mainly DC) rather than retail money.
- **AUM**: approximately **£5bn in June 2025**, rising to **£7.3bn by April 2026**, with a further **£3.1bn of committed-but-not-yet-called capital** on top [T146][Confirmed]. This matches and slightly refines the figures the brief already had.
- **Number of funds**: roughly **25 LTAF strategies** live as of the £7.3bn figure (April 2026); a slightly earlier snapshot (March 2025) counted **12 umbrella LTAFs and 22 authorised sub-funds** [T146][T152][Confirmed — consistent across two independent sources].
- **Main providers found**: Fidelity International (Diversified Private Assets LTAF), Schroders Capital (at least three LTAFs — Global Private Equity, Global Energy Infrastructure, and a UK Venture and Growth LTAF backed by the British Business Bank and Phoenix Group), Aviva Investors (reported to have four LTAFs live), M&G (Diversified Private Credit Feeder LTAF, launched March 2025, an evergreen private-credit/structured-credit/ABS vehicle), Legal & General/LGIM (Private Markets Access Fund for DC schemes, July 2024), Scottish Widows (three LTAFs approved August 2025, run with Columbia Threadneille/"CG"), BlackRock (a DC-focused private-markets LTAF reported launched in May 2026), and Carne Group/Willis Towers Watson (one LTAF) [T140][T146][T152][T164][T165][T166][Reported — aggregated from multiple trade-press hits; individual AUM by provider not independently verified for each]. Roughly **82% of UK asset managers with a European presence say they are considering launching an LTAF within three years**, according to research cited by Norton Rose Fulbright — i.e., this is a land grab, and Fidelity is an early but not unique mover [T152][Reported].
- **Retail access is still nascent.** A Morningstar analyst is quoted noting that allowing LTAFs into stocks & shares ISAs is "an important signal of the direction of travel," implying that as of April 2026 this retail unlock is recent/emerging rather than mature [T146][Reported].

### 1.9 The Mansion House Accord — the political scaffolding behind all of this
Signed 13 May 2025 by 17 workplace-pension providers (matches the brief), the Accord is a **voluntary** commitment to invest **at least 10% of DC default assets in private markets by 2030**, with **at least half of that (5% of the total) earmarked for UK assets** — aiming to mobilise roughly **£50bn** of pension capital into private markets over five years [T156][Confirmed]. It explicitly defines "private markets" as unlisted equities, property, infrastructure and debt/credit — i.e., private credit is inside the scope of the Accord, not incidental to it. Industry commentary (IPE, Pensions Expert, Professional Pensions) flags a real tension: trustees have a **fiduciary duty to act in members' best interests first**, and some commentators worry that a voluntary industry-wide target, agreed partly to serve a government growth agenda, risks **"mandation" creeping in** if providers don't hit the numbers organically, and that a scarce pipeline of good UK private assets could mean the extra money simply **bids up prices** rather than improving member outcomes [T157][T167][Reported]. This is useful, fair-minded context: Fidelity's LTAF push is not a rogue commercial decision, it is happening inside — and partly because of — a coordinated, government-encouraged industry commitment that itself has open critics on fiduciary-duty grounds.

### 1.10 What Investment Directing at Fidelity actually does, and where this story bites
The role description (multiple internship listings, consistent across cycles) says the Investment Directing team's job is to **"commercialise Fidelity's investment capabilities,"** representing the **Equities, Fixed Income, Multi Asset and Real Estate** groups to clients, and covers: **reporting on portfolios; promoting thought leadership; working with product development to create new products that meet investor demand; and generating marketing material to support sales activity** [T158][Confirmed — verbatim language consistent across Bright Network/targetjobs/Prosple postings]. Two things follow directly from the research above:
1. **This team would be one of the people actually writing the client-facing material that explains the LTAF/FutureWise story** — to trustees, to consultants, and potentially (given Consumer Duty's reach) indirectly to members. Getting the liquidity story right in plain English is literally in-scope for the job, not an abstract public-policy question.
2. **The FCA's own findings (the 90-day-notice risk-warning redesign, the Consumer Duty consumer-understanding outcome, and the private-market valuation review) collectively describe exactly the communication failure modes Investment Directing must avoid**: overstating investment risk while underplaying liquidity risk; using jargon without explanation; and describing a valuation as more precise/current than it really is. A candidate can credibly argue that explaining an LTAF honestly to a non-specialist means leading with **"you may not be able to get your money out quickly, and here is exactly how long you might have to wait and why,"** before getting to expected returns — the reverse of a typical sales pitch.

## 6. HOW THIS HELPS THE CANDIDATE ANSWER (analysis angles, not drafted prose)

**For Q1 (200 words, "a theme/article/news story... relevant to an investor... how would you explain to a client without a financial background"):**

- The strongest version of this answer is a **two-step structure**: (1) state the parallel plainly — private credit sold in liquid-looking wrappers is under visible strain in the US (BDC gates, ~half of redemption requests unmet in Q1 2026), and Fidelity is putting a related asset class into millions of UK workers' pension defaults; then (2) **immediately and precisely qualify it** using the structural differences established above. A candidate who does only step (1) sounds alarmist; a candidate who does both demonstrates judgement — which is explicitly what the brief says separates a good answer from an outstanding one.
- The qualifying facts to draw on, in order of strength: (a) Fidelity is largely **distributing third-party managers' private credit, not manufacturing/originating it itself** — a genuinely different risk position from Blackstone/Ares running their own BDC; (b) the **LTAF wrapper has a built-in 90-day-plus notice period and a public-asset liquidity sleeve specifically engineered to avoid the BDC-style gating problem**; (c) DC-default cash flow is **contribution-driven and demographically smooth**, not "hot money" that can all try to leave at once the way a wealth client's discretionary BDC allocation can; (d) the exposure is **capped at ~15% of a diversified default**, not the client's whole position; (e) there is **regulatory scaffolding** (FCA RMMI rules, Consumer Duty, TPR disclose-and-explain) specifically designed around this exact risk.
- The honest counterweight to keep the answer credible rather than let Fidelity off the hook entirely: **members never chose this** (it's a default, not an active decision), the **FCA's own valuation review found sector-wide gaps in how private assets are priced** (so "the LTAF is valued conservatively and safely" is not something anyone can fully verify day to day), and **redemption caps/notice periods do not eliminate liquidity risk, they just relocate the pain** — someone still has to absorb the mismatch if enough people want out at once (in FutureWise's case, that would fall on the scheme/trustee/platform's liquidity management, not on the member directly and not on Fidelity's balance sheet in the way it would for a manufacturer).
- Explaining it to "a client without a financial background": the plain-English version is something like — "the fund holds things like private loans and buildings that can't be sold in a day; because pension money mostly comes in and goes out slowly and predictably, only a limited slice is invested this way, and the wrapper is built to make people wait up to 90+ days if too many want their money at once, instead of freezing the whole fund like some property funds have had to in the past." That is essentially the FCA's own reasoning for redesigning the retail risk warning to foreground liquidity over volatility — useful to cite as evidence the industry itself sees liquidity, not day-to-day price swings, as the layperson's real risk here.

**For Q2 (150 words, £1m windfall, short-term vs long-term asset allocation):**

- This research is less directly about Q2, but the LTAF/private-markets story is a legitimate, current, well-evidenced example the candidate could use **if they want a private-markets sleeve in the "long term" bucket**, provided they handle liquidity correctly: private credit/private assets are properly a **long-horizon-only allocation precisely because of the notice-period/illiquidity mechanics documented here** — that is a defensible, well-informed reason to include a small private-markets allocation in the (b) long-term portion and explicitly exclude it from (a) short-term, where liquidity is paramount. The 90-day-plus notice period and Q1 2026 BDC gating data are concrete, real numbers a well-prepared candidate could cite (accurately, briefly) to justify why an individual's own emergency/short-term money should never sit in anything LTAF-like or BDC-like, while a small, patient slice of long-term money reasonably could.
- Do not overreach: £1m held personally is **wealth money**, structurally exactly like the US BDC/wealth-channel investor this research shows is currently getting gated — not like DC-default contribution money. If the candidate uses the private-credit theme in Q2, the honest framing is closer to "this is precisely the kind of money that behaves like the redemption-prone wealth capital in the US story, so size and access terms must be chosen very carefully" — the DC-default reassurances from Q1 do **not** transfer to a personal lump sum, and a sharp answer would not blur that line.

**General judgement point for both answers**: the research above shows this is a live, unresolved policy question — the FCA, TPR, and the industry itself (via the Mansion House Accord critics) are actively debating exactly the tension the candidate is being asked to describe. An answer that treats it as settled either way ("obviously fine" or "obviously reckless") is weaker than one that shows awareness the debate is ongoing and names the actual mechanisms (notice periods, valuation governance, contribution-driven cash flow, fiduciary duty) rather than gesturing vaguely at "illiquidity risk."
