# 1. FINDINGS

## 1.1 The stated cause: what Blue Owl actually said, and what it means

Blue Owl Capital, the roughly $315bn alternative asset manager, disclosed in early April 2026 that two of its non-traded, semi-liquid business development companies (BDCs) — Blue Owl Credit Income Corp (OCIC, its ~$36bn flagship direct-lending vehicle) and Blue Owl Technology Income Corp (OTIC, its ~$6bn tech-focused vehicle) — received extraordinarily high shareholder redemption requests in Q1 2026: 21.9% of OCIC's shares outstanding and 40.7% of OTIC's [Confirmed, T101][T102][T103]. Both funds capped redemptions at their standard 5% quarterly limit (a "gate"), meaning the great majority of investors who asked for their money back did not get it [Confirmed, T101][T102]. Combined, investors sought to redeem roughly $5.4bn across the two funds, partly offset by about $1bn of new inflows [Reported, T102]. Blue Owl attributed the surge to "heightened market concerns around AI-related disruption to software companies" [Confirmed, T102] — this is the brief's core phrase and it is a real, dated (2 April 2026) company statement, not a paraphrase.

**Why software, and why does AI threaten it specifically?** Private credit — direct lending by non-bank funds instead of banks or public bond markets — grew explosively over the last decade largely by financing leveraged buyouts (LBOs) of software companies. Software was considered close to an ideal credit for lenders because: (i) revenue is recurring (subscription/SaaS billing) and therefore predictable; (ii) gross margins are very high (little cost of goods sold) so cash flow available to service debt is large relative to revenue; (iii) capital intensity is low (no factories or inventory) so free cash flow converts well; and (iv) customers are "sticky" — switching core business software is costly and slow, which underpins low customer churn and high net revenue retention (NRR), the metric that justified premium valuations. Blue Owl itself is unusually exposed to this thesis: one of its BDCs (Blue Owl Technology Finance Corp) holds roughly 70% of its portfolio in software, and Blue Owl Capital Corp had earlier trimmed software exposure from 19% to 16% of the book via natural loan repayments, a sign management itself was de-risking [Reported, T109].

The AI thesis threatens exactly the four pillars above. If AI agents can perform the tasks that seat-licensed software used to require a human to operate, then (a) the number of paid "seats" a customer needs falls even as usage of the underlying task rises, breaking the per-seat pricing model; (b) NRR — expansion of the same customer's spend over time — turns negative as seats are cut rather than added; and (c) the "stickiness" that justified low churn assumptions is undermined if an AI-native competitor can replicate core functionality cheaply. Andreessen Horowitz argued in a widely cited January 2025 analysis (referenced secondhand via commentary, primary post not directly verified in this research — [Reported, low confidence, T114]) that the two-decade "golden rule" of SaaS — "streamline human tasks into software and charge per user" — was "no longer valid." Bain & Company's 2025 technology report, "Will Agentic AI Disrupt SaaS?", is the most rigorous mainstream articulation: it maps five scenarios from "no AI impact" through "AI cannibalizes SaaS," argues customers will increasingly "expect to pay based on outcomes, not log-ons," and cites incumbents (Intercom, Salesforce) already moving toward outcome-based pricing in response [Confirmed, T110]. IDC has forecast that by 2028 pure seat-based pricing will effectively be obsolete, with the large majority of vendors refactoring pricing around consumption or outcomes [Reported, T115]. Trade press coined the term "SaaSpocalypse" in a Forbes piece by Peter Cohan on 6 February 2026 [Reported, T111], and SaaStr (industry commentary site run by investor/founder Jason Lemkin) published data in 2026 arguing this was "the SaaS rout" and, for the first time, software as a group traded at a discount to the S&P 500 [Reported, T112].

## 1.2 The evidence: what actually happened to valuations, spreads and defaults

**Public equity valuations.** The evidence for a genuine repricing is strong and dated. Software forward price/earnings multiples fell in a clear staircase: roughly 84x (May 2020–May 2022, pandemic-era peak), 43x (mid-2022 to mid-2024), 33.6x (July 2024–June 2025), 31.2x (July–December 2025), and 22.7x in Q1 2026 — a level that put the sector, for the first time in modern history, below the broader S&P 500's multiple [Reported, T112]. The iShares Expanded Tech-Software ETF (IGV) was down roughly 21% year-to-date and about 30% from its September 2025 peak by the time of that SaaStr analysis, with roughly $2 trillion of software market capitalisation lost since the correction began in late January 2026 [Reported, T112]. This is a real, measurable de-rating that predates and coincides with Blue Owl's redemption episode — the software correction began in earnest in late January 2026, and Blue Owl's own software-lending concerns became a story on 20 February 2026 [Reported, T104], with the redemption disclosure following on 2 April 2026.

**Credit-side evidence is much thinner and more ambiguous — this is the intellectually important nuance.** Despite the equity de-rating, one CNBC report from 9 February 2026 on private credit "worries" resurfacing specifically noted that default rates in the software/technology lending book had "remained relatively stable" [Reported, T122] even as investor sentiment soured. Economy-wide private credit default measures were rising only gradually, not collapsing: Proskauer's Private Credit Default Index (a widely used industry benchmark compiled from lender data) moved from 1.84% in Q3 2025 to 2.46% in Q4 2025 to 2.73% in Q1 2026, before easing slightly to 2.51% in Q2 2026 [Reported, T121]. Using a broader definition, Fitch put the U.S. private credit default rate at a record 6.0% in April 2026 [Reported, T122]. Moody's April 2026 outlook described credit risk as "fragmented and fragile" rather than in outright crisis, and separately estimated the annualised probability of default for all U.S. listed companies at 7.9% and for U.S. high-yield issuers at 3.2% as of March 2026 (note: this is a general corporate metric, not a software-specific or pure private-credit figure) [Confirmed, T120]. A significant complication in reading any of these numbers: distressed exchanges (negotiated restructurings that avoid a formal bankruptcy filing) accounted for roughly 65% of all 2025 defaults and 94% of downgrades to default/selective-default status in the twelve months to February 2026 [Reported, T121] — meaning headline default rates likely understate true stress, because troubled borrowers are being quietly restructured rather than formally defaulting.

**PIK income — the "canary in the coal mine."** Payment-in-kind (PIK) interest lets a borrower pay its lender in more debt instead of cash, usually because the borrower cannot generate enough cash to service the loan. A rising PIK share of a BDC's income is one of the most closely watched leading indicators of credit stress, because it signals lenders are already accommodating struggling borrowers before a formal default. The data show a clear upward trend: PIK income as a share of total BDC interest income rose from 5.9% in 2023 to over 7% by Q4 2025 [Reported, T117], and public BDCs were averaging around 8% PIK income in 2026 [Reported, T116]. Separately, the share of BDC loans carrying a PIK component (as opposed to income share) rose from roughly 6% to roughly 10% of loans by early 2026, a 67% increase in three years [Reported, T116]. One boutique research shop's threshold framework classes 0–6% as "normal," 6–8% as "watch," 8–10% as "warning" (associated historically with a default surge within 2–4 quarters) and above 10% as "critical" (associated with a hard default wave 1–2 quarters later); by that framework the industry moved from "watch" toward "warning" through 2025–26 [Reported, low-confidence boutique source, T116]. Separately, aggregate BDC unrealised losses reached 2.35% of net asset value in Q1 2026, described as the steepest quarterly decline since mid-2022 [Reported, T116]. The Federal Reserve's May 2026 Financial Stability Report is reported to have flagged the elevated and rising use of PIK provisions as a specific area of concern [Reported — primary Fed document not directly fetched in this research, T137], alongside a reported (but attribution-uncertain) statistic that roughly 40% of private credit borrowers now carry negative free cash flow, up from about 25% in 2021 [Reported, attribution uncertain, T137].

**Bottom line on the evidence** [Inferred]: the equity-market repricing of software is large, fast and well documented. The credit-side deterioration is real but far more moderate and gradual, and default rates specifically in software lending are reported as "stable" even as the redemption panic was unfolding. This gap — a violent narrative/valuation shock against a much smaller move in realised credit losses — is itself the most interesting analytical fact in the whole episode, and Blue Owl's own CEO leaned on it: Marc Lipschultz (Blue Owl co-CEO) pushed back publicly, noting that the software companies in Blue Owl's portfolio had grown revenue by 40% and EBITDA by 50% since ChatGPT's launch in November 2022 — i.e., AI so far looks like a portfolio tailwind, not the threat the redeeming investors feared [Reported, T101][T103]. Separately, Blue Owl's president of credit, Craig Packer, gave a more circumspect explanation, saying "tender activity was elevated across the non-traded BDC industry in the first quarter of 2026, reflecting a period of heightened negative sentiment toward the asset class" [Confirmed, T102] — note this frames it as an industry-wide sentiment event, not a Blue Owl-specific credit problem.

## 1.3 The other AI angle: private credit is also over-financing AI infrastructure

This is the angle the brief specifically wants surfaced, because it is nearly the mirror image of point 1.2: instead of AI destroying a borrower's business model, here AI borrowers (datacentre and AI-infrastructure operators) may be receiving too much cheap private credit, financed on assumptions of AI demand that may not materialise.

**Scale.** Morgan Stanley's July 2025 research ("Bridging a $1.5tr Data Center Financing Gap") estimated global datacentre capital expenditure would reach about $2.9 trillion through 2028, of which Big Tech's own operating cash flow would cover only about $1.4 trillion — leaving a $1.5 trillion financing gap to be filled by debt markets. Of that gap, Morgan Stanley projected private credit would supply the single largest share, roughly $800bn, versus $200bn from corporate bonds and $150bn from securitised products [Reported, T125]. UBS separately estimated private-credit AI-related loans may have nearly doubled in the twelve months to early 2025 [Reported, T126]. Total AI-related corporate debt outstanding was reported to be on track to approach $570bn in 2026 [Reported, T126].

**Concentration risk.** The lending is concentrated among a small number of large borrowers. CoreWeave — a specialist AI-cloud/GPU-compute company — had secured more than $30bn in debt and equity capital in 2026 alone by August, including a single $3.1bn facility that reportedly drew $19bn of investor demand [Reported, T126]. Moody's flagged, in a report titled "Power without delivery," that datacentre debt exposure is concentrated to a handful of borrowers and tenants, and is secured against hardware (GPUs) that Nvidia's own product cycle is racing to make obsolete — a specific and unusual collateral risk: the security backing the loan depreciates technologically far faster than a conventional building or piece of infrastructure would [Reported, T126].

**Circular / vendor financing.** Regulators and commentators have converged on a specific structural worry: AI chipmakers and cloud "hyperscalers" are simultaneously customers, suppliers and investors in the same AI labs, and cash can circulate between the same handful of counterparties in ways that inflate apparent demand. Documented examples include: OpenAI's committed purchase of $250bn of cloud services from Microsoft; OpenAI deploying AMD chips while becoming one of AMD's largest shareholders; Nvidia's reported willingness to invest up to $100bn in OpenAI partly to help fund OpenAI's purchases of Nvidia hardware; a $300bn OpenAI–Oracle partnership for Stargate datacentre capacity; and a roughly $15bn combined Microsoft/Nvidia investment in Anthropic, which in turn is committed to spending roughly $30bn on Microsoft's cloud and Nvidia's chips [Reported, moderate-to-low confidence on the aggregate framing, T131][T132][T138][T139]. Axios reported on 27 July 2026 that Nvidia was again drawing "circular" financing scrutiny over a potential financing guarantee tied to OpenAI [Reported, not directly fetched — 403, T131]. The mechanism critics worry about: cash leaves a chipmaker's balance sheet as an "investment" and returns to its income statement as "revenue" after passing through one or two AI labs and cloud providers, which can manufacture the appearance of end-user demand that does not actually exist yet.

**Regulatory flags.** The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) used its Annual Economic Report, published 28 June 2026, to name an "AI capex bust," "circular financing," and "sovereign debt fragility" as the three most likely interlocking pressure points that could crack global financial stability; it estimated the five largest hyperscalers would spend over $1 trillion on AI-related capex in 2026 alone, and separately noted that circular deals mixing equity, debt and supplier contracts create assets that may effectively be "pledged multiple times" [Reported, T130]. The IMF's April 2026 Global Financial Stability Report stated that "firms within the AI ecosystem are increasingly reliant on circular financing arrangements," creating vulnerability "if investment slows," and separately warned that stretched valuations and concentration in AI-related firms raise downside risks to equity markets [Reported, T129]. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee, in its July 2026 Financial Stability Report, explicitly named private credit markets and "corrections to artificial intelligence-related equity valuations" — alongside sovereign debt markets — as the three main sources of UK financial stability risk, and separately flagged that AI-related companies' use of credit markets, "including in public markets, private credit, leveraged and structured finance," had "accelerated rapidly" [Confirmed, T127]. It further warned that rapid advances in frontier AI capability were themselves increasing cyber and operational risk to the financial system, because "frontier AI models are increasingly capable of identifying and exploiting software vulnerabilities at greater scale" [Confirmed, T127] — a genuinely distinct, third channel by which AI intersects financial stability (beyond credit destruction and credit over-supply).

[Inferred] Points 1.2 and 1.3 together show private credit is exposed to AI from both directions simultaneously: it has lent heavily against software borrowers whose business model AI may be eroding, and it has lent heavily to build the AI infrastructure itself, on the assumption that AI demand will justify the buildout. If the AI capex cycle disappoints, both exposures could deteriorate together rather than offsetting each other, because a slowdown in enterprise AI adoption would simultaneously validate the software-disruption fear (less need to replace software) and undercut the revenue case for the datacentre borrowers (less compute demand) — though this specific "both fail together" scenario is my own inference, not something any single source states directly.

## 1.4 The 2026 market backdrop: why investors wanted liquidity

**Rates.** Central banks were not easing into the episode; if anything the reverse. The U.S. Federal Reserve raised its target range by 25bp to 3.75%–4.00% at its 15–16 September 2026 meeting, with the "dot plot" showing officials split on whether to raise again by year-end 2026/2027 [Confirmed, T134]. The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 16 September 2026 by a 6–3 vote (three members wanted to raise to 4.00%), with UK CPI inflation having risen to 3.1% in August 2026 and the Committee citing the war in the Middle East as pushing up energy costs [Confirmed, T133]. This matters for two reasons: higher policy rates make cash and liquid short-duration credit more attractive relative to locked-up private credit paying a fixed illiquidity premium that may no longer look generous; and higher discount rates mechanically hit the valuation of long-duration growth assets — like software companies priced on distant future cash flows — hardest, compounding the AI-driven de-rating.

**Equities.** 2026 was a volatile year for AI-exposed equities specifically. By mid-2026, AI-related companies were estimated to account for roughly 80% of the gains in the U.S. stock market, with the five largest technology companies representing about 30% of S&P 500 value — the highest concentration in roughly fifty years [Reported, moderate confidence, T136]. The market experienced repeated AI-driven drawdowns: a sharp one-day fall in the Nasdaq Composite (down 2.2%) on 24 June 2026 amid AI stock selling, and by 31 July 2026 the Nasdaq had entered its "second correction of 2026," described elsewhere as its sixth double-digit drawdown since November 2022 [Reported, T135]. The Bank of England's July 2026 Financial Stability Report separately noted a "significant rise in hedge fund leverage in equity markets" and increasing concentration in global indices driven by AI names [Confirmed, T127] — meaning the market structure itself had become more fragile heading into the second half of the year.

**Credit.** Private credit spreads had compressed materially by the time this all played out: reported figures put spreads at roughly 450–500 basis points for the majority of loans by year-end 2025, after multiple waves of repricing as competition among lenders intensified [Reported, T115]. Banks were also reported to be re-entering mid-market direct lending as the regulatory environment stabilised, competing away some of the yield premium BDCs had offered, and BDC equities as a group underperformed the broader market by roughly 20% from early 2025 to mid-2026 [Reported, T115].

**The simpler, behavioural explanation.** Evergreen (semi-liquid, continuously-offered) private credit vehicles sold heavily through the wealth-management/retail channel had grown enormously: the U.S. evergreen fund market reached $607bn across 567 funds by 31 March 2026, up from $590.8bn and 552 funds in 2025 — and this growth continued even as headline redemption stress at Blue Owl was making news [Reported, T137]. This is an important, easily overlooked fact: fundraising into evergreen structures did not stop; a subset of investors redeemed hard while the category as a whole kept growing. A large, relatively new pool of retail/wealth-channel money had never lived through a private-credit downturn or a gate before, having mostly bought in during a multi-year run of strong reported returns; a wave of that money testing its "liquidity" for the first time when headlines turned negative was, on this reading, close to inevitable regardless of the specific trigger.

## 1.5 Competing explanations for the redemption surge, weighed

**(a) Genuine AI-driven credit fear.** For: Blue Owl's own stated reason; the well-documented, large, dated equity de-rating in software (T112); Bain's and IDC's structural arguments about seat-based pricing (T110, T115); the fact that Blue Owl's own tech-focused fund (OTIC) saw nearly double the redemption rate of its generalist fund (OCIC) — 40.7% vs 21.9% — which is exactly what you would expect if the fear were specifically about software/tech exposure [Confirmed, T102][T103]. Against: software-specific default rates were reported as "relatively stable" even as this played out (T122); Blue Owl's CEO cited strong portfolio revenue/EBITDA growth since ChatGPT's launch (T101); PIK and default data show gradual deterioration across private credit generally, not a software-specific credit collapse.

**(b) Disappointing returns versus liquid credit alternatives.** For: spread compression to ~450–500bps, banks re-entering the market, BDC equities underperforming by ~20% (T115) — all consistent with investors simply finding better risk-adjusted returns elsewhere once the illiquidity premium shrank. Against: this is a slower-moving, more structural story that doesn't obviously explain why redemption requests spiked so sharply and specifically in Q1 2026 rather than rising steadily for a year.

**(c) Wealth-channel investors who never understood the illiquidity.** For: the huge and continuing growth of evergreen fund AUM overall even as individual vehicles gated (T137) is consistent with a subset of unsophisticated or newly-onboarded retail money panicking at the first bad headline, while more sophisticated capital stayed; gates exist precisely because evergreen structures promise more liquidity than the underlying loans can actually support, and this is the first real test of that promise for many buyers. Against: Craig Packer's own comment that "tender activity was elevated across the non-traded BDC industry" (T102) suggests this was a broad, somewhat rational repricing of sentiment, not simply naive investors panicking in isolation.

**(d) Rebalancing after public market gains.** For: with AI-related equities driving an outsized share of 2026 market gains (T136), some investors plausibly wanted to trim illiquid private allocations to fund purchases of, or rebalance toward, liquid AI-exposed equities, or simply to rebalance a portfolio whose public equity sleeve had run up. Against: no source directly ties Blue Owl's redeeming investors to this motive; it is a plausible but unverified inference.

**(e) Contagion and headline effect.** For: this is well evidenced. Jamie Dimon's "cockroach" warning came on 15 October 2025 — five and a half months before Blue Owl's disclosure — after JPMorgan's exposure to the Tricolor auto-lender collapse and the unrelated bankruptcy of auto-parts supplier First Brands; Dimon's exact words were "when you see one cockroach, there are probably more. And so we should — everyone should be forewarned on this one" [Confirmed, T135]. This was about auto lending, not software, but it primed general suspicion of private credit months before the software-specific fear crystallised. Mohamed El-Erian then explicitly linked new private-credit stress (in early March 2026, around when BlackRock also reportedly joined other managers in curbing withdrawals from a fund) back to Dimon's cockroach framing, writing "The big question for markets and the real economy is whether we're just dealing with cockroaches… or are these termites posing systemic risks?" [Reported, T136]. KKR also gated a fund at close to the same time as Blue Owl [Reported, T107]. This pattern — one firm's bad headline triggering redemption tests at unrelated firms — is a textbook contagion/headline effect. Against: it doesn't fully explain why the fear specifically fixed on AI and software by Q1 2026, rather than remaining focused on auto lending or generic underwriting quality.

**(f) Higher-for-longer rates making liquid alternatives attractive.** For: the Fed hiked (not cut) to 3.75–4.00% in September 2026 and the BoE held at 3.75% with a hawkish dissent (T133, T134), both driven by inflation from Middle East-conflict energy prices; this raises the opportunity cost of being locked into an illiquid private credit vehicle instead of holding cash or short-dated liquid credit at an attractive yield. It also compounds explanation (a) mechanically, since higher discount rates hit long-duration software valuations hardest. Against: rates had actually been cut in late 2025 (part of what compressed BDC yield advantage per T115) before this later 2026 hiking cycle — so the "higher-for-longer" story is not a clean, single-direction narrative across the full period; it reversed direction more than once.

**Most persuasive [Inferred]:** the combination of (a) and (e) — genuine AI-driven fear as the proximate trigger, amplified by contagion/headline dynamics and a structurally oversized, newly-onboarded wealth-channel investor base testing gates for the first time (c). The reasoning: the size and speed of the jump (redemption requests reaching 22–41% of AUM within a single quarter) is far larger than the moderate, gradual deterioration visible in actual credit metrics (Proskauer index in the low-to-mid single digits, software defaults reported stable, PIK income rising but only into "watch/warning" territory rather than "critical"). That mismatch between the size of the reaction and the size of the underlying credit deterioration is the signature of a sentiment/liquidity-driven run triggered by a real but not-yet-fully-realised structural fear, rather than evidence of an already-materialised credit crisis. Reasonable people could instead weight (b) or (f) more heavily; I flag this explicitly as my own inference and not something any cited source states outright.

## 1.6 Bear and bull case on private credit, September 2026

**Bear case, named proponents and dates:** Jamie Dimon (JPMorgan CEO), 15 October 2025, "cockroach" warning after Tricolor/First Brands [Confirmed, T135]. Mohamed El-Erian (Allianz chief economic adviser), 6 March 2026, "cockroaches or termites" framing amid fresh redemption pressure and BlackRock reportedly curbing withdrawals [Reported, T136]. Mayra Rodriguez Valladares (Forbes contributor, financial-risk consultant), a sustained bear voice through spring 2026 with pieces on 11 May ("Trying to Count the Cockroaches") and 24 May ("Rising Private Credit Defaults Are Testing Banks and Insurers") [Reported, T124][T123]. S&P Global Ratings, 17 February 2026, named private credit, AI-fuelled tech issuance and rising leverage as the three key factors it judged would drive 2026 credit-market liquidity risk [Confirmed, T114]. The Federal Reserve Bank of Boston published a 2026 paper on early warning signals visible in BDC portfolios [Reported, T118]. The Financial Stability Board issued a "Report on Vulnerabilities in Private Credit" (dated 6 May 2026 per its title; document metadata suggested a later creation date of 24 July 2026 — flagged as a discrepancy I could not resolve from a garbled fetch) [Confirmed primary source, content partially unreadable, T119]. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee (July 2026 Financial Stability Report) named private credit, sovereign debt and AI-equity-valuation corrections as the three main UK financial stability risks [Confirmed, T127]. The IMF's April 2026 Global Financial Stability Report flagged AI-ecosystem circular financing and concentration as risks to watch [Reported, T129]. The BIS's 28 June 2026 Annual Economic Report named an AI capex bust and circular financing among its top three global risks [Reported, T130].

**Bull case, named proponents and dates:** Moody's 2026 Private Credit Outlook forecast industry AUM exceeding $2 trillion in 2026 and approaching $4 trillion by 2030, alongside its April 2026 assessment that default risk, while "fragmented and fragile," was not collapsing [Confirmed, T120]. Marc Lipschultz (Blue Owl co-CEO) argued in April 2026 that AI had so far been a tailwind, not a threat, for his firm's software borrowers, citing 40% revenue and 50% EBITDA growth since ChatGPT's late-2022 launch [Reported, T101][T103]. Dechert LLP (law firm) published a piece in June 2026 titled "Don't Believe the Headlines: A Defense of BDCs and Private Credit" [Reported, T138]. The Chartered Alternative Investment Analyst Association (CAIA) published a more measured industry-insider take on 20 April 2026, "Private Credit Redemptions, Defaults, and Wrappers, Oh My!" [Reported, T139]. The continued growth of the evergreen fund universe to $607bn by end-Q1 2026 despite the headline redemption stress (T137) is itself bull-case evidence that underlying investor demand for the asset class had not broken. Morgan Stanley's research (T125) makes a structurally bullish case for private credit's growing role financing AI datacentre buildout, framing the ~$800bn opportunity as a durable growth driver for the industry rather than a risk.

# 6. HOW THIS HELPS THE CANDIDATE ANSWER (analysis angles, not drafted prose)

**For Q1 (200 words, theme/article relevance + explain to a non-financial client).** This story is unusually strong for Q1 because it has three layers a strong answer can move between: (i) a concrete, dated news event (Blue Owl's April 2026 gating); (ii) a mechanism (AI threatening seat-based software pricing, which the candidate can explain in plain terms — "software used to charge per employee who logged in; if an AI assistant now does that employee's job, the software company has one fewer customer to bill, even though the same amount of work is getting done"); and (iii) a second, opposite mechanism (private credit also over-financing AI datacentres) that shows genuine sophistication if the candidate can gesture at it in one sentence — the strongest possible answer notes that private credit is exposed to AI risk from both directions, which is a distinctive, non-obvious point almost no other candidate is likely to make. For explaining to a client without a financial background, the redemption-gate mechanism itself is a good hook: "imagine a savings account that promises you can take out cash each quarter, but if everyone tries to withdraw at once, the bank can only let out a small fraction" — this makes "gating" and "illiquidity" tangible without jargon. A weak answer would just say "AI is disrupting jobs" or "AI is a bubble" without connecting it to the specific credit/redemption mechanism; a strong answer explains why software was considered safe lending collateral in the first place (recurring revenue, sticky customers) and why AI specifically undermines that logic, then is honest that the actual credit losses so far are much smaller than the fear (the PIK/default data), which is the kind of nuance that separates analysis from headline-repeating.

**Distinguishing "requests received" from "requests honoured"** is a genuinely important test of whether the candidate read past the headline: 21.9%/40.7% were requested, only 5% was paid out. A client-facing explanation should make clear that most people who wanted their money back did not get it that quarter — this is the entire point of a gate, and conflating the two numbers is the single most common mistake an unprepared candidate would make on this topic.

**For Q2 (150 words, £1m investment, short vs long term, asset allocation).** This research is not directly about Q2, but it is highly relevant background if the candidate wants to reference illiquidity risk or private markets when discussing long-term asset allocation: private credit/BDC vehicles are a real-world illustration of the liquidity-premium trade-off (higher yield in exchange for accepting you might not get your capital back on demand), which is exactly the kind of concept a strong long-term allocation answer should implicitly understand even without naming Blue Owl specifically. The candidate could use the general lesson — "illiquid, higher-yielding assets suit money you won't need for years, and 2026's private credit gating shows what happens when investors misjudge that" — as a one-line justification for why a short-term allocation should stay liquid (cash, short-dated bonds) while a long-term allocation can rationally include less liquid, potentially higher-returning assets, provided the investor's own time horizon and risk tolerance genuinely support it. This should stay as a brief, general point in Q2, not a Blue Owl case study — Q1 is where the specific story belongs.

**Where to be careful.** The candidate should not overstate the AI-credit-destruction thesis as settled fact — the evidence in section 1.2 shows software defaults have been reported as stable even as sentiment collapsed, so a well-calibrated answer should present AI disruption as a genuine, well-evidenced *fear* that has already repriced equities and triggered a liquidity event, while being honest that it has not (yet) shown up as a wave of realised credit losses. That distinction — fear/repricing versus realised loss — is exactly the kind of intellectually honest nuance the brief is asking for, and is likely to be rare among other candidates' answers.
