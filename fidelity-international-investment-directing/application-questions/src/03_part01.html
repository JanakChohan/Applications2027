Part 1

The two questions, decoded

What each one is really testing, which carries more weight and why, and what happens to your written answers after you submit them. Read this before you write a word.

1.1 What each question is actually testing

Neither question is about the topic you choose. Both are about how you think, and Question 1 is also about whether you can be trusted in front of a client.

Question 1, in three parts

Read the question again slowly, because it has three clauses and most candidates answer only the first:

"Outline a theme, article or news story you learned about recently, why you think it is relevant to an Investor in the Investment Management Industry and how you would explain to a client without a financial background?" Fidelity International application form, Investment Directing Summer Internship 2027 [T260]
  • Clause one, the story. Table stakes. It shows you read something. Almost every applicant clears this.
  • Clause two, relevance to an investor. This is the commercial awareness test. Note the precise wording: relevant to an investor in the investment management industry, not "relevant to the world". You are being asked to connect a news story to how money is managed, priced, sold or governed. A story about interest rates that never reaches a portfolio or a client is only half an answer.
  • Clause three, explaining it. This is the one that separates people, and it is the clause most candidates treat as an afterthought. It is also, almost exactly, the job description of the role you are applying to.
Why clause three is the whole role

The posting for this internship says the team has "a strong knowledge of markets and Fidelity's portfolio strategies, combined with a capacity to communicate these in different formats, including through direct client relationships"[T260] Confirmed. It lists "reporting on portfolios" and "generating marketing material to support sales activity" as duties.

Question 1 is that sentence turned into a test. The firm is not asking whether you can understand a news story. It is asking whether you can take something complicated, keep it accurate, and make it land with someone who does not share your vocabulary. That is what an Investment Director does every day.

Question 2, in one part

"Imagine you won 1 million GBP. How would you invest your winnings for (a) short term and (b) the long term. Investing in at least one asset class, talk about the asset allocation that would be appropriate for each time horizon." Fidelity International application form [T260]

This is a structured thinking test with a specific hinge: time horizon determines asset allocation. The question tells you the framework it wants you to use. It says "appropriate for each time horizon", which is an instruction, not a hint. An answer that does not make horizon the organising principle has not answered the question asked.

Note also "at least one asset class". That is a floor, not a target, and it is a small kindness: you are not being asked to build a full portfolio in 150 words. You are being asked to show you understand why the allocation differs between the two horizons.

1.2 Weighting: which one matters more

Source uncertain — and it always will be

No firm publishes the weighting of individual application-form questions, and this research found none for Fidelity. Anyone who tells you "Question 1 is worth 60%" has made it up. What follows is reasoning from the question design and the job description, and it is marked as such.

Four reasons to believe Question 1 carries more:

  1. It is given more words. 200 against 150. Word allocation is the cheapest signal a form designer gives you about relative importance.
  2. It tests two competencies, not one. Commercial awareness and client communication. Question 2 tests structured reasoning alone.
  3. One of those two is the role itself. "A capacity to communicate these in different formats" is in the team description[T260]. Nothing in Question 2 maps to a duty line that directly.
  4. It is much harder to fake. Question 2 has a defensible answer that a diligent person can reason to from first principles in an hour. Question 1 rewards someone who has actually been following something, and that is visible in the specificity.
The practical consequence

If you have ten hours, spend seven on Question 1 and three on Question 2. But do not treat Question 2 as a formality: because the band of sensible answers is narrow, a confused one stands out badly. The goal on Question 2 is to be clearly right and slightly more interesting than average. The goal on Question 1 is to be the answer the reader remembers at the end of the day.

1.3 What happens to these answers afterwards

Two things, and both should change how you write.

First, they follow you to the assessment centre. Assessment centres for this programme run in December and January[T260]. Assume an interviewer will have your form in front of them and will ask you to expand on whatever you wrote. That has a hard implication: do not put a single claim in your answer you cannot defend for two more minutes. If you cite a number, know its source and its date. If you name a firm, know what it does. The fastest way to lose a room is to have written something you cannot stand behind.

Second, the process is strengths-based. Fidelity's own materials describe a "strengths-based assessment process", and a recruiter is quoted saying the firm moved away from competency-based questions toward "core strengths that motivate someone"[T261] Reported. A strengths process is looking for what genuinely engages you. An answer about a topic you picked because it sounded impressive reads differently from one about a topic you actually find interesting, and the difference shows up under follow-up questioning.

A test you can run on your own draft

When you have a draft of Question 1, ask yourself: could I talk about this for five minutes without notes, and would I enjoy it? If the answer is no, change the topic, however clever the one you have chosen. The follow-up question is where the marks actually are.

1.4 Fidelity's own rules on using AI to write these answers

This research turned up something directly on point that you should read before you write anything: Fidelity International publishes a Candidates AI Code of Conduct.

What it actually says

It does not ban AI. It draws three lines[T234] Confirmed, read directly from the firm's own careers site:

CategoryExamples given
Fine, no disclosureImproving grammar, spelling, tone and formatting. Structuring or tailoring a CV. Translation. Interview preparation. Accessibility tools.
Allowed, but must be disclosed"Preparing written responses for non-timed tasks where originality, reasoning or reflection is assessed." That is precisely what these two questions are.
Prohibited"Submitting AI-generated content as your own where authenticity, judgement or personal capability is being assessed." Also real-time AI assistance during live interviews or assessments.

Their stated reason, verbatim: "We are interested in you – your judgement, experience, potential and values – not your ability to outsource thinking to technology." And the consequence: failure to follow the code "may result in disqualification or withdrawal of an offer"[T234].

What this means in practice, and why this pack is shaped the way it is

Using research to inform your thinking is ordinary preparation, the same as reading the Financial Times. What their code targets is submitting reasoning that is not yours as though it were.

That is the reason this document contains no drafted answer, and it is a stronger reason than the one you gave when you asked. The finished sentences need to be yours, on the firm's own stated rules. If you do use an AI tool to help structure or draft substantive reasoning in the answer itself, their code says to disclose it. Read the page yourself before you decide — it is on their careers site and takes two minutes.
