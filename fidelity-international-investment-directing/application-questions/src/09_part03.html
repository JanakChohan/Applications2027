3.1 What it is really testing, and the answer everyone else will give

You said you did not know how to approach this one. Here is the reframe: the question is not testing whether you can allocate a portfolio. It is testing whether you understand why horizon changes the answer.

The obvious answer, which a large share of applicants will give in some form, is:

Short term: cash, a savings account or a money market fund. Long term: a diversified global equity fund, because equities beat cash over long periods. The baseline. It is not wrong. That is the problem with it.

That answer is correct, defensible and completely unmemorable. Since nearly everyone lands within a narrow band of sensible responses, the marginal value of being right is low and the marginal value of being right and interesting is high.

What the question's own wording tells you

"Talk about the asset allocation that would be appropriate for each time horizon." The word "appropriate" is doing the work. You are being asked to justify a match between an asset and a horizon. The mechanism behind that match, not the allocation itself, is the answer.

"Investing in at least one asset class" is a floor, not a target. You are not expected to build a full portfolio in 150 words, and trying to will make you look superficial.

The observation this environment hands you free

It also sets up the long-term leg: a roughly 1 percentage point real return will not do anything meaningful over thirty years, which is precisely why the long horizon needs a different asset.

3.3 The frameworks worth using

Three things that are not the same, and are routinely confused

ConceptWhat it meansWhy the distinction matters here
Time horizonWhen you need the money.The question's organising principle. It is an objective fact about the money.
Risk toleranceHow much volatility you can stomach emotionally.A fact about the person. It does not change when the money is needed.
Risk capacityHow much loss you could absorb without damaging your plans.The one most candidates never mention. With £1m and no liabilities, capacity is high even if tolerance is low.

The trap is answering "I am young so I would take more risk" and applying that to the short-term money too. Youth does not change when you need cash next year.

The rigorous reason short horizons need safe assets

It is not "shares are risky". It is sequence-of-returns risk: over a short period, the order in which returns arrive dominates the average. If you need the money in eighteen months and the market falls 20% in month fourteen, the long-run average return is irrelevant to you. You have crystallised a loss because the clock, not the market, made the decision.

3.5 Seven angles, ranked by how few applicants will use them

Pick one. In 150 words, one idea executed properly beats five name-checked.

#The angleWhat it saysRisk
1The FSCS gap £1m in cash is not risk-free, because only £120,000 per institution is protected[T182]. Solve it with spread deposits, a money market fund, or short-dated gilts. Almost none. It is verifiable, numerical and correct.
2CGT-exempt gilts Short-dated gilts solve the safety, tax and FSCS problems in one instrument[T186]. Must get the mechanism right: the exemption is on the capital gain, not the coupon.
3Behaviour is the real risk A windfall is psychologically different from earned money, and the biggest determinant of the outcome is the investor's own conduct, not the equity/bond split. The "roughly 70% dissipated within seven years" figure is widely repeated but weakly sourced[T205]. Use the idea; be careful with the statistic.
4Purpose before horizon Ask what the money is for, and let each purpose imply its own horizon. This is how an institution thinks: liability first, allocation second. Must still answer the question as asked. Do not reframe so hard you dodge it.
5A gilt or liquidity ladder Rather than one short-term bucket, stagger maturities at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months so cash arrives when needed and reinvestment risk is spread. Needs a sentence on why, or it reads as jargon.
6Lump sum versus phasing in Vanguard's research concludes investing a lump sum immediately beats phasing in, in roughly 60-70% of historical periods, because markets rise more often than they fall[T187]. Phasing is a deliberate trade of expected return for reduced regret. Only works if you name it as a psychological trade-off rather than pure risk management.

3.6 The traps

  • Confusing horizon with risk tolerance. "I am young so I would be aggressive" applied to money needed next year.
  • Naming specific products or stocks. Asset classes and generic instrument types are the right register. Naming a fund or a share reads as tipping, not thinking.
  • Ignoring tax entirely when the sum is large enough that tax is unavoidable.
  • Treating "safe" as nominal. The single most common conceptual gap.
  • Crypto as a throwaway. If it appears at all it needs a risk-budget framing, not a shrug.
  • Being so generic anyone could have written it. The default failure mode.
The tension nobody tells you about — and what to do with it

You are applying to an active asset manager. The evidence-based answer to "where do I put money for thirty years" is often a low-cost global index fund, and roughly 75% of active funds still trail passive over ten years[T207]. A flat "buy a tracker and do nothing" can read as slightly tone-deaf to the reader, even though it is well supported.

Two honest ways through, and you should choose deliberately rather than stumble into one. Either be precise about where active has the stronger evidential case, which is areas with more dispersion such as fixed income, emerging markets and smaller companies, rather than US large-cap. Or state the low-cost case plainly and show you know it is in tension with the firm you are writing to. Demonstrating that you have noticed the tension is worth more than resolving it either way. Pretending it does not exist is the only losing option.

3.7 Three ways to build the answer

Shapes, not sentences. Word counts are indicative and total 150.

A · The flaw-and-fix strongest, highest risk

  1. 25 words. Name the obvious answer and the problem with it: £1m of cash is not risk-free because of the FSCS limit.
  2. 45 words. Your short-term solution and the mechanism: spread deposits, money market fund, or short-dated gilts, with the reason.
  3. 55 words. The long-term leg, anchored on real returns: why 0.8% real does not compound and 5.1% does.
  4. 25 words. The principle connecting them: the horizon decides the asset, and "safe" means different things at each end.

Why it works: opens with something the reader has not seen that day. Why it is risky: you must get the FSCS numbers exactly right.

B · Purpose first safest of the three

  1. 30 words. Reframe: the horizon follows from what the money is for. Name two purposes.
  2. 50 words. Short-horizon money: capital certainty, because sequence-of-returns risk dominates over short periods. Name the asset class and the current rate.
  3. 50 words. Long-horizon money: real growth, because inflation is the binding risk. Name the asset class and the real-return evidence.
  4. 20 words. One sentence on tax or wrapper capacity to show you noticed £20,000 does not cover £1m.

Why it works: it is how an institution actually thinks, and it answers the question as asked.

C · One idea, fully developed best if you have a genuine view

  1. 20 words. State the allocation quickly, almost as a given.
  2. 110 words. Spend nearly everything on one idea from 3.5, developed properly, with the mechanism and a number.
  3. 20 words. Close on what it implies for how you think about risk.

Why it works: depth is rarer than coverage and reads as conviction. Watch: you must still visibly answer both (a) and (b), or it looks like you dodged half the question.

Before you submit this one

Re-check Bank Rate, CPI and gilt yields on the day. The Bank of England's next decision after 16 September 2026 will land before your 15 November deadline, and an answer quoting a stale rate is exactly the kind of avoidable error this question is designed to surface.
