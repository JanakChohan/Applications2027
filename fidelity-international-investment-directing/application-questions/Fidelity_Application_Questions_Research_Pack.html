Why there is no drafted answer in this document

You asked for the research and not the prose, twice, and you were right to. Three reasons it matters here. The process is strengths-based, which is designed to catch rehearsal. Your written answers are likely to be read back to you at the assessment centre, where you will have to defend every claim in them. Neither question is about the topic you choose. Both are about how you think, and Question 1 is also about whether you can be trusted in front of a client.

Question 1, in three parts

Read the question again slowly, because it has three clauses and most candidates answer only the first:

Why clause three is the whole role

The posting for this internship says the team has "a strong knowledge of markets and Fidelity's portfolio strategies, combined with a capacity to communicate these in different formats, including through direct client relationships"[T260] Confirmed. Question 1 is that sentence turned into a test. The firm is not asking whether you can understand a news story. Question 2, in one part

"Imagine you won 1 million GBP. How would you invest your winnings for (a) short term and (b) the long term. Investing in at least one asset class, talk about the asset allocation that would be appropriate for each time horizon." Fidelity International application form [T260]

1.2 Weighting: which one matters more

Source uncertain — and it always will be

No firm publishes the weighting of individual application-form questions, and this research found none for Fidelity. Anyone who tells you "Question 1 is worth 60%" has made it up. What follows is reasoning from the question design and the job description, and it is marked as such.

Four reasons to believe Question 1 carries more:

  1. It is given more words. 200 against 150. Word allocation is the cheapest signal a form designer gives you about relative importance.
  2. It tests two competencies, not one. Commercial awareness and client communication. Question 2 tests structured reasoning alone.
  3. One of those two is the role itself. "A capacity to communicate these in different formats" is in the team description[T260]. Nothing in Question 2 maps to a duty line that directly.
  4. It is much harder to fake. Question 2 has a defensible answer that a diligent person can reason to from first principles in an hour. Question 1 rewards someone who has actually been following something, and that is visible in the specificity.
The practical consequence

If you have ten hours, spend seven on Question 1 and three on Question 2. But do not treat Question 2 as a formality: because the band of sensible answers is narrow, a confused one stands out badly. The goal on Question 2 is to be clearly right and slightly more interesting than average. The goal on Question 1 is to be the answer the reader remembers at the end of the day.

1.3 What happens to these answers afterwards

Two things, and both should change how you write.

First, they follow you to the assessment centre. Assessment centres for this programme run in December and January[T260]. Assume an interviewer will have your form in front of them and will ask you to expand on whatever you wrote. That has a hard implication: do not put a single claim in your answer you cannot defend for two more minutes. If you cite a number, know its source and its date. If you name a firm, know what it does. The fastest way to lose a room is to have written something you cannot stand behind.

Second, the process is strengths-based. Fidelity's own materials describe a "strengths-based assessment process", and a recruiter is quoted saying the firm moved away from competency-based questions toward "core strengths that motivate someone"[T261] Reported. A strengths process is looking for what genuinely engages you. An answer about a topic you picked because it sounded impressive reads differently from one about a topic you actually find interesting, and the difference shows up under follow-up questioning.

A test you can run on your own draft

When you have a draft of Question 1, ask yourself: could I talk about this for five minutes without notes, and would I enjoy it? If the answer is no, change the topic, however clever the one you have chosen. The follow-up question is where the marks actually are.

2.2 What an evergreen fund is, from zero

You cannot explain this story to anyone until you can explain the product in two sentences. The product is the story.

For forty years, investing in private companies, private loans or property meant one structure: a closed-end drawdown fund. You commit money for roughly ten years. The manager calls it when it is needed and returns it when investments are sold. You cannot ask for it back. That structure matches the asset: if it takes years to sell a company, the fund should not promise to return your money next month.

At the other extreme sits the ordinary open-ended fund most people own, the OEIC or unit trust in an ISA. You can sell any day, at that day's price, because the fund holds shares and bonds that trade every day.

The evergreen fund, also called semi-liquid, was built to sit between them. It has no end date, it accepts new money continuously, and it lets you ask for some money back at set intervals, usually quarterly. That is what makes it sellable to wealthy individuals and, increasingly, to ordinary savers who were never going to sign up for a ten-year lock-up.

Key term · the 5% quarterly cap

An evergreen fund does not promise to return everything you ask for. It promises to buy back a limited amount each quarter, conventionally 5% of the fund's total value. If more people ask than the cap allows, everyone gets a pro-rata share: ask for £100,000 in a quarter where requests are four times the cap, and you get roughly a quarter of it.

Where does 5% come from? For one narrow US category, registered "interval funds", it is regulatory: SEC Rule 23c-3 requires a repurchase offer between 5% and 25% each period[T57] Confirmed. For the non-traded business development companies at the centre of this story, and for most evergreen private funds, 5% is convention, not law[T57][T58][T60]. That distinction is worth knowing: the number everyone quotes as a rule is mostly a habit.

Where the cash to pay leavers actually comes from

This is the part that makes the story click, and most coverage skips it. An evergreen fund meets redemptions from three places[T57][T60]:

  1. A liquidity sleeve: cash and easily sold liquid credit, deliberately held back. Partners Group's affected fund reportedly held about 15% in cash plus about 15% in an undrawn credit facility[T63][T66] Reported.
  2. New money coming in. Subscriptions from new investors fund the exits of old ones.
  3. Selling assets, which is the thing the structure exists to avoid doing in a hurry.
The sentence that explains the whole story

Point 2 is fine while money is flowing in, and becomes a problem the moment it stops. A fund that relies on new subscriptions to pay departing investors works beautifully in a boom and strains precisely when sentiment turns, which is exactly when people want out.

Be careful how you phrase this. It is not a Ponzi scheme: the fund owns real loans producing real interest, and the cap exists specifically so the fund never has to pay leavers with money it does not have. But the dependence on inflows is real, it is structural, and it is why redemption pressure and slowing fundraising tend to arrive together. If you make this point, make it precisely. Overstating it is the single easiest way to look unserious.

2.3 Why the structure strains: the liquidity mismatch

One idea underneath everything: a fund that promises to return money faster than it can sell what it owns has created a race. Once investors realise there is a race, the race starts.

The fund values its assets using a model, not a market price

Private loans do not trade on an exchange, so their value is an estimate, updated periodically. In calm conditions that is reasonable. In deteriorating conditions the estimate lags reality.

So the price paid to someone leaving may be too generous

If the stated value has not caught up with what the assets would actually fetch, whoever leaves first is paid out at the old, higher figure. The shortfall is absorbed by everyone who stays.

Which creates a first-mover advantage

The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee states it plainly: "there is an advantage to investors who redeem ahead of others, particularly in a stress"[T52] Confirmed. You do not need to think the manager is dishonest to want to be first. You only need to think other people might be.

So rational investors submit requests early

And because allocation is pro-rata, submitting a larger request than you need is also rational, which inflates the headline request figure beyond genuine demand for cash. This matters when reading the numbers in 2.1.

The gate comes down, which protects the fund and confirms the fear

The cap stops forced selling at fire-sale prices, which genuinely protects everyone who stays. It also tells every remaining investor that the queue is real. The protective mechanism and the panic trigger are the same event, viewed from two sidesInferred.

The UK has been here before, twice

You have two domestic precedents, and using one of them shows you understand this is not a new problem.

  • Woodford, June 2019. The LF Woodford Equity Income Fund suspended dealing on 3 June 2019. The FCA later found that by then only 8% of the fund's investments could be sold within seven days, against a promise of daily redemption. The fund had fallen from over £10.1bn in May 2017 to £3.6bn immediately before suspension. Fines of nearly £46m were announced on 5 August 2025[T55] Confirmed.
  • Property funds, 2016 and 2020. After the June 2016 referendum, major UK open-ended property funds suspended within days. The FCA's own account of the resulting rules says it directly: "Funds which hold less liquid assets can encounter liquidity difficulties if significant numbers of investors simultaneously try to withdraw their money at short notice"[T61] Confirmed. It happened again in March 2020 on valuation uncertainty.
The principle underneath all of it

From the joint FCA and Bank of England statement of 16 December 2019: a fund's redemption notice period should match "the time needed to sell the required portion of a fund's assets without discounts" beyond those already in the price[T53] Confirmed.

In plainer words: a fund's exit terms should be built backwards from how long its assets actually take to sell, not forwards from what would be easiest to sell to a customer. That single sentence is arguably the whole of your Question 1 answer, and it is a regulator's sentence, not a journalist's.

The counter-argument, which you should take seriously

There is a respectable case that none of this is a scandal, and two named people made it on the record during the 2026 episodes:

"These funds are meant for long-term investing, and they limit redemptions accordingly. Those limits are not a sign of stress — they are a tool to protect investors." Eric J. Pan, President and Chief Executive, Investment Company Institute, opinion piece dated 27 April 2026 [T65] Confirmed
"Evergreen fund gates are often misread as a red flag, but they are precisely the mechanism these structures were designed for: protecting long-term investors when short-term redemption demand temporarily outpaces liquidity." Nicolas Moura, PitchBook senior analyst for EMEA private capital, quoted around 5 June 2026 [T66] Confirmed

The strongest version of their case: the cap prevents fire-sales that would harm everyone remaining; the terms were in the prospectus before anyone invested; and pro-rata allocation is fairer than first-come-first-served.

The honest reply is not that they are wrong. It is that disclosure in a prospectus is not the same as understanding. An investor can have been told about a 5% cap with pro-rata allocation and a 90-day notice period, and still not have grasped what that feels like when they need the money and cannot get it. That gap between what was disclosed and what was understood is, precisely, the thing the UK's Consumer Duty was written to close.

2.4 The AI link, and whether it holds up

This is where your answer can become something almost nobody else will write. The AI explanation is real, it is the company's own, and when you check it against the credit data it does not fully hold. That gap is the most interesting thing in the entire story.

The argument, as the market made it

Software was considered close to the perfect borrower

Recurring subscription revenue, high margins, little need for physical capital, customers who rarely leave. Private credit funds lent heavily into software buyouts on exactly that reasoning[T100].

AI threatens the specific thing that made it good credit

If AI agents can reproduce what a software product does, then per-seat pricing, the ability to raise prices, and customer stickiness all weaken at once. The argument was set out in Bain & Company's 2025 work on whether agentic AI disrupts software-as-a-service[T101] Reported.

Public markets repriced software violently

Software's forward price-to-earnings multiple fell from roughly 84 times in 2020-22 to 22.7 times by the first quarter of 2026, below the S&P 500 for the first time on record, with around $2 trillion of software market value lost since January 2026[T102] Reported.

Investors in a fund lending to those same companies drew the obvious conclusion

And tried to leave. Blue Owl's technology lending fund, the one most exposed to software borrowers, saw the highest redemption requests of any non-traded BDC tracked[T21].

The check that almost nobody will run

If AI were already damaging these borrowers, it should be visible in the credit data. It largely is not, at least not yet.

  • The Proskauer Private Credit Default Index moved from 1.84% to 2.73% to 2.51% across the four quarters to mid-2026[T103] Reported. That is a modest, wobbling series, not a credit event.
  • Payment-in-kind income, where a borrower pays interest with more debt rather than cash and which is the standard early warning of strain, rose from 5.9% of BDC income in 2023 to around 8% in 2026[T104]. Elevated and worth watching. Not a crisis.
  • Software-specific default rates were reported as "relatively stable" in February 2026, during the panic[T105] Reported.
  • Blue Owl's own chief executive, Marc Lipschultz, pushed back publicly, noting the portfolio's software companies had grown revenue around 40% and earnings around 50% since ChatGPT launched in November 2022[T106] Reported.

The second AI exposure, which runs the other way

Worth knowing because it shows the exposure is not one-directional. Private credit is not only lending to companies threatened by AI. It is also financing the AI build-out itself. Morgan Stanley has projected private credit supplying around $800bn of a $1.5 trillion AI datacentre financing gap through 2028[T107] Reported.

Regulators have noticed both. The Bank for International Settlements named an AI capital-expenditure bust and circular financing among its top global risks in its annual report of 28 June 2026[T108]. The Bank of England's Financial Stability Report of July 2026 named private credit, AI equity valuations and sovereign debt as the three main risks to UK financial stability[T109] Reported.

The observation worth building an answer around

Private credit is exposed to AI from both ends at once: its software borrowers may be disrupted by AI, and it is simultaneously one of the largest lenders to AI infrastructure. A candidate who notices that the same asset class sits on both sides of the same theme is demonstrating something no reading list can supply.

Clause three, and the one that is actually the job. This section gives you the raw materials: the standard you are being measured against, the words to avoid, and several candidate analogies with an honest assessment of what each one gets wrong. Choosing between them is your job.

The standard, from the regulator

The FCA published "Consumer understanding: good practice and areas for improvement" in March 2026[T237] Confirmed. The underlying principle from the Consumer Duty is that communications must be likely to be understood by the people receiving them, and that firms should test that rather than assume it.

The useful reframe for your answer: comprehension is the outcome being measured, not accuracy. An explanation can be perfectly true and still fail. The trap inside this clause

A good analogy makes an explanation land. A slightly-wrong analogy makes it misleading, which in this industry is a regulatory problem rather than a stylistic one. The bank-run comparison is the obvious reach and it implies something untrue: that investors are losing their savings. They are not. They are being made to wait.

If your explanation would cause a reader to believe something false, it has failed the fair, clear and not misleading test even though every individual sentence is accurate. Demonstrating that you know the difference is worth more than any analogy you could choose.

The components of a plain-English explanation of this story

Assemble these in your own words and order. Each is a single idea, deliberately left as a bare component:

  • The fund owns loans to private companies. Those loans cannot be sold quickly.
  • Investors were told they could take out a limited amount each quarter. Roughly one pound in twenty.
  • Many more than that asked to leave at once.
  • So everyone got a share of what was available, and the rest of the request waits.
  • The limit is not a failure of the fund. It is the rule that stops the fund having to sell good assets cheaply.
  • But it does mean people who wanted their money could not have it.
  • What triggered it: worry that artificial intelligence could damage the software companies the fund lent to.
  • The twist: those loans have not actually gone bad yet. People moved on the fear, not the fact.

Eight ideas. A 200-word answer cannot carry all eight and the story and the relevance. Deciding which three survive is the actual test.

A test you can run

Read your explanation to someone who does not work in finance, and afterwards ask them: "so what happened to the investors' money?" If they say "they lost it", your explanation has failed, no matter how elegant it was. That is the Consumer Duty's consumer understanding outcome, in a form you can actually run this week.
