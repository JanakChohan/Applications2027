How to use this pack

You are applying, not interviewing tomorrow. That is the best possible position to be in, because the material that wins this application is the material nobody else bothers to find. Here is the order to read it in.

What this pack is, and what it is not

This is a research pack built only from public sources. It is written to take someone with a finance degree and one internship from knowing roughly what an asset manager does to being able to hold a technical conversation about Fidelity International's business model, the Investment Directing function, and the rules that govern what that function is allowed to say to a client.

It is not a script. Every scaffold here is a structure to think inside, not a sentence to recite. Interviewers at asset managers listen to hundreds of these. A memorised answer is audible, and it is worse than a rougher answer that is obviously yours.

Three things this pack does that a normal preparation document does not. It decodes the job description line by line against real, live Fidelity International job postings, so you are working from what the firm actually asks people to do rather than from marketing copy. It gets the regulation right, with rule numbers and dates, because for this specific seat the regulation is the job. And it tells you where the evidence is thin, so you never walk into a room repeating something this pack invented.

The honesty rule this pack follows

Fidelity International is a private company. It does not publish quarterly results, hold earnings calls, or file the kind of disclosure a listed rival does. A large share of what is written about it is estimate or single-source reporting. Where that is true, this pack says so and gives a range rather than inventing a precise number. If you quote an estimate as a fact and an interviewer knows the real figure, you have damaged yourself for the rest of the conversation. Saying "reported at around X, though they do not publish it" is stronger, not weaker.

Fidelity International began in 1969 as the overseas arm of the Boston business and became independent in 1980[S402] Confirmed. It is headquartered in Pembroke, Bermuda, and its largest investment hub is London[S402][S44]. Almost every confusing "Fidelity" story you will read online, and a large share of the internship advice, is about the American firm. Public descriptions place the "Client Propositions team comprised of product, solutions and investment directing teams" under the Global Head of Asset Management Distribution[S41] Reported. 1.1 What an Investment Director actually is

An Investment Director sits between the people who manage the money and the people who sell it. That is the whole job in one sentence, and almost every other fact about the seat follows from it.

The industry's generic name for this seat is Client Portfolio Manager. Fidelity International calls it Investment Director, and runs a junior grade called Associate Investment Director[S1] Confirmed. Schroders and Janus Henderson use Client Portfolio Manager directly; M&G says Investment Specialist; BlackRock says Product Strategist; Baillie Gifford splits the job in two, pairing a Client Manager who owns the commercial relationship with an Investment Specialist who owns the investment content[S2][S3][S4][S5][S6] Reported.

That the same function exists at every serious firm under six different names tells you it is structural, not a Fidelity quirk. It exists for an economic reason. A fund manager who is excellent at picking stocks is not necessarily good at spending two hours being cross-examined by a pension scheme's investment consultant, and the firm does not want its scarcest and most expensive investment talent doing that all day. Equally, a salesperson who cannot answer a probing question about duration positioning will lose the room. The Investment Director is built to do both: technically credible enough that the portfolio manager trusts them to speak for the strategy, commercially fluent enough to read a client Inferred.

Key term · Client Portfolio Manager (CPM)

A client-facing investment professional who represents a strategy to clients but does not make the buy and sell decisions. They explain the portfolio, they do not run it. One live Fidelity International advert describes the role, in the firm's own words, as a "Portfolio Manager (PM) proxy responsible for client facing activities including investment process, strategy updates, product updates, client pitches, client servicing, prospecting, due-diligence, RFPs and product development"[S47] Confirmed. "PM proxy" is the most precise two-word definition of this job you will find anywhere, and it came from Fidelity, not from this pack.

Say this in the interview "The way I understand it, Investment Directing is the translation layer. The portfolio managers own the decisions, Distribution owns the relationship, and the Investment Director is the person who can stand in front of a client and explain the portfolio at the manager's level of detail without being the manager. One of your own adverts calls it a PM proxy, which I thought was a good description."

Why it works: it shows you found a real job posting, it uses the firm's own phrase, and it gets the reporting line right, which most candidates do not.

1.2 Every duty line, decoded

The internship advert describes the team's job and then the intern's job. Below, each line is taken apart: what it means, who you deal with, what good looks like, and what it quietly tells you about how the firm is built.

Source uncertain — the job description

The 2026 posting's text is reproduced here from search-engine summaries, because the job board hosting it blocks automated access[S21]. The wording is consistent across multiple summaries and matches the language on Fidelity's own careers pages, so it is reliable as substance. Treat it as Reported, not verbatim, and re-read the real 2027 advert when it opens. If you send me the live text I will rebuild this section against it line by line.

Team line 1 · "Representing the Equities, Fixed Income, Multi Asset, Systematic Investing and Real Estate groups to prospective and existing clients"

What it means. The investment engine is organised into five asset-class groups, and an Investment Director is assigned to one of them, not to all five. A live advert was scoped specifically to Systematic Investing[S1]. "Representing" means being the person in the room for a prospect meeting, a client review, a consultant call or a conference panel, carrying that group's house view.

Counterparties. Inside: the portfolio managers and analysts in that group, whose views you must reflect rather than invent. Outside: pension schemes, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, wholesale fund selectors, platforms, private banks and wealth managers[S1][S10].

What good looks like. Technical accuracy that would survive the portfolio manager reading it, plus the ability to translate for a non-specialist without flattening it into something misleading. Those two things pull against each other, and managing that tension is the craft of the job.

What it reveals. Real Estate is the odd one out. It is physical and illiquid, valued by appraisal rather than by a daily market price, so it reports to clients on a different rhythm from a daily-priced equity fund[S10]. If you are asked which of the five interests you and you pick Real Estate, be ready to say why the illiquidity changes the communication problem. That is a strong answer.

Team line 2 · "Reporting on portfolios"

What it means. The production line of monthly and quarterly factsheets, commentary, client review packs, and performance attribution. Attribution answers: did we beat the benchmark, by how much, and from what — stock selection, sector allocation, duration, currency.

Counterparties. A performance and attribution team supplies the numbers (Fidelity has such roles; a live Dublin vacancy is titled "Performance & Attribution Lead - Associate Director"[S45]), portfolio managers approve the narrative, and Compliance signs off the wording before it leaves the building.

What good looks like. The numbers tie exactly to the official performance record. You cannot round favourably or quietly drop an ugly period, partly because it is against the rules and partly because a consultant such as Mercer is very likely re-running the same numbers independently[S8][S11].

Team line 3 · "Promoting thought leadership on topics"

What it means. White papers, "viewpoints", webinars, outlook pieces, conference panels. This is not about one fund's last quarter. It keeps the house view visible so that when a client eventually runs a search, the firm is already in mind.

The trap. Candidates assume thought leadership is the fun, unregulated part. It is not. A white paper is a financial promotion in FCA terms just as much as a factsheet is, and clears the same "fair, clear and not misleading" bar[S14].

Team line 4 · "Working with product development to create new products to meet investors' demands"

What it means. There is a distinct Product function, separate from both the investment teams and Distribution. Live Fidelity vacancies include "Product Owner - Equity" and "Product Owner - Multi-Asset"[S43][S44] Confirmed. So the firm splits manage, manufacture and sell three ways. Investment Directing is the channel through which structured client demand — the same RFP question asked eleven times, a consultant's repeated objection, a platform's request for a different share class — flows back into what gets built. One live advert lists "Content for Product launches, reshapes, DDQ, RFPs, perspectives" as a deliverable[S1].

Team line 5 · "Generating marketing material to support sales activity"

What it means. Pitchbooks, RFP and DDQ answers, one-pagers, case studies. Highest volume, most junior-accessible, and almost certainly where most of your ten weeks will go, because drafting a first version needs accuracy and structure rather than years of judgement.

Key terms · RFP and DDQ

A Request for Proposal is a formal document a prospective client or its consultant sends out when it wants to hire a manager, containing dozens to hundreds of questions. A Due Diligence Questionnaire is the same idea aimed at how the firm is run: its controls, its people, its risk processes. Both are answered largely from a maintained library of approved answers, which someone has to keep current. That someone is often the most junior person in the team. It sounds dull and it is genuinely important: the answers are contractual in tone and wrong ones are expensive.

1.4 What the team is judged on

No firm publishes its internal targets, and none were found for Fidelity[S1]. The structural logic points to three things Inferred:

  • Net flows and asset retention for the strategies covered. Money kept is worth as much as money won, and is cheaper to get.
  • Win rate on RFPs and pitches. The industry treats this as a measurable, improvable skill; vendors sell software on precisely that claim[S13] Reported.
  • Consultant ratings and buy-list status. A manager usually has to be rated by a gatekeeper such as Mercer, WTW or Aon before most institutional searches will consider it[S11]. The UK investment consultants' market was significant enough that the Competition and Markets Authority ran a full market investigation into it[S12].

The sharpest version of the third point, and a good thing to say out loud: a downgrade by one consultant can shut off more new business than any marketing campaign can replace. That is why this function exists at a senior level at all.

Don't say this

"I'd be judged on how persuasive the materials are." No. Persuasion that outruns the evidence is the specific failure mode the rules in 1.5 exist to prevent. The honest version is that you are judged on whether clients stay invested through a bad quarter because the explanation was good and true, not because it was flattering.

1.7 The team, reconstructed from public evidence

Fidelity International publishes no org chart for this function. What follows was rebuilt from live job adverts, press coverage and public professional profiles. The method is shown so you can judge the evidence yourself, and so you can describe how you did it, which is itself a good answer.

The method · job adverts are an org chart in disguise

A company will not publish its structure, but it will publish a hundred job adverts that each name a reporting line and a set of partner teams. Collect enough of them and the shape appears. Two adverts did most of the work here, and both are quoted below.

Where the function sits — the single most useful structural finding

Public descriptions place "the Client Propositions team comprised of product, solutions and investment directing teams" under the Global Head of Asset Management Distribution, from March 2024[S41][S53] Reported, corroborated across two independent write-ups.

So Investment Directing is owned by Distribution, not by the Chief Investment Officer organisation. Most candidates assume the opposite. It reframes the role as a commercial function that must be technically credible, rather than an investment function that happens to face clients.

But the picture is not clean, and saying so is better than pretending otherwise. Two live adverts point different ways:

An "Investment Director, Fixed Income" role in Hong Kong reports to the "Head of Investment Directing, Asia Pacific", and describes the job as a "Portfolio Manager (PM) proxy responsible for client facing activities including investment process, strategy updates, product updates, client pitches, client servicing, prospecting, due-diligence, RFPs and product development". Live Fidelity International job advert [S47] Confirmed. The same advert states the fixed income business it supports manages "over $US80 billion".
An "Associate Investment Director – Systematic Investing" role in London reports instead to the "Head of Systematic Investing", sits in an "ISS Client Propositions team", and names its key partners as "Multi Asset, Solutions, ETFs, Investment Management, Distribution (PMs, PSG, Stats, PCR, Sales & Marketing, RFP teams)". Live Fidelity International job advert [S48] Confirmed. The same advert says Systematic Investing has grown from roughly $3bn to roughly $30bn of assets in about five years.

The honest reading: Investment Directing is matrixed. It is owned centrally by Distribution, and individual post-holders can be line-managed by the asset-class head they represent. That is normal in asset management, and the public record does not fully resolve it Inferred.

2.7 The fulcrum fee: the best story in the firm's recent history

This is the single most useful episode to know, because it shows the firm doing something genuinely unusual, and then honestly abandoning it.

In July 2018 Fidelity International launched a variable management fee share class on five UK funds, explicitly in response to the FCA's 2016 Asset Management Market Study, which had criticised the lack of fee innovation and clustering around a flat 0.75%[S72] Confirmed. The base charge was cut to 0.65%, then moved symmetrically up or down by up to 0.20 percentage points on rolling three-year performance against benchmark: a maximum of 0.85% if the fund beat the benchmark, a minimum of 0.45% if it lagged[S72].

On 25 May 2021 Fidelity scrapped it on the open-ended funds, citing limited client demand. A spokesperson said: "We've seen limited client demand in our open-ended funds, and as a result we decided the UK-domiciled VMF share classes were too small to manage cost-effectively... Adoption of our model was always a client choice and we are pleased to see that it opened the doors to wider discussions on pricing structures"[S72] Confirmed. It survived on three investment trusts holding about £2.9bn, because a trust has one share class and an independent board to negotiate with[S72].

AJ Bell's Ryan Hughes named the flaw: "The challenge with fulcrum fees is always that they need explaining... There is also a natural difficulty in explaining to customers that if the fund does well, they will pay higher fees"[S72] Confirmed.

Key terms · the fee vocabulary

AMC is the annual management charge, the manager's own fee. The key behavioural point: a fee charged on assets rewards gathering assets; a fee charged on performance rewards taking risk. Firms choose which incentive they want, and regulators watch both.

The role's own industry

Where the investment-specialist function came from, the regulation that shaped it, the systems it runs on, and what its internal clients actually value in 2026. If you learn one technical area properly, make it this one.

6.1 Why this job exists at all

The function grew because two things happened at once. Institutional clients and their consultants began demanding far more access and far more evidence, and portfolio managers' time became the scarcest resource in the firm. Someone had to absorb the load without diluting the technical answer. Hence a dedicated seat, at every major firm[S1]–[S6] Inferred.

Part 8

Forming your own view

Six arguments where a thoughtful person could land on either side, with the evidence for each. The point of this section is not to tell you what to think. It is to make sure that whatever you think, you know the strongest version of the opposing case.

Interviewers at this level are not testing whether you agree with them. They are testing whether your view survives contact with a counter-argument. A candidate who holds a position under mild pressure and concedes a genuine point gracefully beats a candidate with a more fashionable opinion every time.

8.1 Is active management worth paying for?

The case for
  • Underperformance statistics are dominated by large, efficient markets, especially US large-cap. They are much weaker evidence about Asian smaller companies, credit or property.
  • Most savers do not want a benchmark return. They want an outcome: income in retirement, capital preserved, volatility they can tolerate. An index cannot be asked for that.
  • Someone must set prices. If everyone indexes, price discovery degrades. Active management is the mechanism, whoever pays for it.
  • Just over 40% of active funds beat their average passive peer in the twelve months to June 2026[S218]. A minority, but a rising one.
The case against
  • 94.1% of US active funds underperformed over the twenty years to 2024[S307]. Two decades is not noise.
  • Morningstar's ten-year success rate was 21%[S308]. Picking the winner in advance is a second, harder problem on top.
  • Fees compound relentlessly. Roughly 60 bps against roughly 10 bps is a 50 bp annual handicap before any skill is demonstrated[S217].
  • Clients are voting: 70.4% of UK platform net inflows went passive in Q2 2026[S232].
Where to land

The defensible position is narrower than "active is worth it". It is: active earns its fee in specific, less efficient places and for specific client outcomes, and an honest firm should concede the passive case in large, liquid, heavily researched markets. Note that this argument has a consequence you should be willing to follow: it implies a firm should be smaller in some markets than it would like to be. Being willing to say that is what makes it sound like a view rather than a script.

8.2 Is private ownership an advantage or an excuse?

Clients get continuity. Two close comparators are currently being acquired[S129][S132]; this one cannot easily be. No listed equity means no acquisition currency and a harder time paying people in shares, which matters in a talent business.

Your opinion goes here

A reasonable one: private ownership does not remove commercial pressure, it removes the obligation to time decisions around a reporting calendar. The structural fact that changes everything: this is strengths-based, not competency-based

22What is a basis point, and why does the industry use them?

One hundredth of one per cent. Fees are charged on asset value, so revenue moves with markets without an invoice. Hence a market fall is immediately a revenue event.

23What is performance attribution?

Decomposing return against a benchmark into its causes: stock selection, sector or asset allocation, duration, currency. It is the core analytical content of a client review.

24Explain active share and tracking error.

Active share is how much the portfolio differs from the benchmark by holdings. Tracking error is how much the return has differed, annualised. High fees with low active share is the "closet tracker" problem regulators care about.

25What is an OEIC? A unit trust? An investment trust?

See Part 5.1. The key distinction: investment trusts are closed-ended and listed, so the price can differ from the value of the assets, which is why they can use structures open-ended funds cannot — including the surviving fulcrum fee[S72].

26What is an LTAF and what is hard about it?

A UK structure for illiquid private assets in long-term savings[S224]. Hard because notice periods, valuation uncertainty and explaining illiquidity honestly to default-fund members who did not choose it.

27What is a benchmark and why does the choice matter?

The yardstick. It matters because it defines outperformance, and because under COBS 4.6.2R past performance must be shown against an appropriate reference[S400]. Choosing a flattering benchmark is a conduct issue, not a marketing choice.

28What is GIPS?

Global Investment Performance Standards. Composite construction rules that stop a firm showing only its best account[S18]. Frequently a precondition in institutional RFPs.

29What is an RFP and who reads it?

See Part 1.2. Written by you, read by a consultant or a scheme's investment team, scored against competitors, and effectively contractual in tone.

30What does an investment consultant do, and why should you care?

They gatekeep institutional searches through ratings and buy lists[S11]. A downgrade closes more business than any campaign replaces. The CMA ran a full market investigation into their power[S12].

31What is the difference between DB and DC pensions?

Defined benefit promises an income and the employer carries the risk; defined contribution builds a pot and the member carries it. DB is closing and de-risking; DC is where growth and policy attention are.

32Why can't you just show the best five-year period?

The single best technical answer in this pack. COBS 4.6.2R fixes the window: the preceding five years in complete twelve-month periods, not the most prominent feature, with the reference period and source stated and a prominent warning that past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results[S400].

33What is the anti-greenwashing rule?

In force 31 May 2024. Every sustainability claim must be fair, clear and not misleading. Separately the fund naming and marketing rules applied from 2 December 2024[S19].

34What is Consumer Duty, in one sentence?

A requirement to act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers, published 27 July 2022 as PS22/9 and in force for open products from 31 July 2023, with four outcomes including consumer understanding[S401].

35What is inside information, and why does it matter in this seat?

Price-sensitive non-public information. It matters because you sit near people who hold it. Reference the FCA's 2015 thematic review on market abuse in asset management and its wall-crossing good practice[S17].

11.1 The four-step protocol

Every scenario question is the same question underneath: when this gets hard, what kind of person are you? The protocol below buys you three seconds of thinking time, imposes a structure, and ends with you in control of the conversation rather than waiting to be rescued.

Why the close matters more than the rest

Most candidates produce a reasonable middle and then evaporate: "...so yeah, I think that's what I'd do." The close does two things. It converts your answer from an anecdote into a principle, which is what gets remembered. And handing a question back turns an interrogation into a conversation, which is the position you want to be in for the next forty minutes.

11.2 The priority order when good things collide

Almost every scenario in this job is a collision between four legitimate goods. They are not equal, and knowing the order is most of the answer.

That closing sentence is the one line to hold every frame. It works because it concedes that speed matters, rather than pretending it does not, and then explains why it still loses.

The conduct rules underneath it

You do not need to recite these, but knowing they exist changes how you answer. The FCA's Code of Conduct sets out Individual Conduct Rules applying to almost everyone at a regulated firm[S351] Confirmed:

  1. You must act with integrity.
  2. You must act with due skill, care and diligence.
  3. You must be open and cooperative with the FCA, the PRA and other regulators.
  4. You must pay due regard to the interests of customers and treat them fairly.
  5. You must observe proper standards of market conduct.
  6. You must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers.

The point for you: a mis-stated performance number in a pitchbook is not a marketing slip. It is potentially a Rule 1 or Rule 2 matter for whoever signed it off. That is why the people around you will seem disproportionately careful, and why matching their care is how you earn trust as an intern.

11.6 The composure set

Five situations designed to make you uncomfortable. For each there is a decision tree simple enough to redraw from memory in the thirty seconds before you answer.

Why these have real cases behind them

Two of these scenarios are grounded in actual FCA enforcement, so you can reference a real consequence rather than a hypothetical one. The Woodford Equity Income Fund was suspended on 3 June 2019 holding about £3.5bn. On 11 April 2024 the FCA issued a Final Notice against Link Fund Solutions, the fund's authorised corporate director, ordering restitution of £298,403,919 and stating it would otherwise have proposed a penalty of £50m[S355] Confirmed. On 5 August 2025 the FCA announced fines of £5,888,800 against Neil Woodford personally and £40m against Woodford Investment Management[S356] Confirmed. Separately, on 14 September 2026 the Upper Tribunal upheld a lifetime ban on Crispin Odey[S358] Confirmed — a pure integrity case rather than a marketing one.

You do not need to cite these unprompted. But if an interviewer asks whether the controls really matter, having one precise number is worth more than any amount of principle.

Composure 1 · Two senior people want the same scarce resource

Composure 2 · A senior person asks you to skip a control

Say this in the interview "I'd separate the person from the request. I'm not refusing them, I'm declining one specific action, and I'd try hard to solve the underlying problem — send the approved slides now, flag what's outstanding, chase compliance with the real deadline. If they went ahead anyway I'd tell my manager the same day. In a regulated firm that's not informing on someone, it's the control working."

Composure 3 · You hear something that might be inside information

The tree: Stop → say so immediately, in the room if possible → leave if appropriate → tell compliance the same day → write down what you heard, from whom and when → do not repeat it to anyone, including colleagues.

Why the write-down matters: the FCA's 2015 thematic review identified, as poor practice, fund managers being wall-crossed, deciding not to trade, and never logging that it happened[S17] Confirmed. The record is the control. Saying that shows you understand why the paperwork exists.

The line: "I'd rather ask an awkward question in the moment than be someone who has to be untangled from a list three months later."

Composure 4 · A mistake you made

The tree: Confirm it is real → tell your manager before you fix it → establish the blast radius (who received it, is it material) → fix → propose the change that stops it recurring.

The structure for the answer: spend 20% on what happened, 20% on how fast you disclosed, 60% on what changed afterwards. Most candidates invert this and spend the whole answer on mitigation, which reads as defensiveness.

Don't say this

"I can't think of a significant mistake." This reads as either dishonest or inexperienced, and in a regulated firm it is close to a disqualifying answer. The willingness to report your own errors is a core competence of the job, and you are being asked to demonstrate it right now.

Composure 5 · Acting on incomplete information against a deadline

The tree: Separate what you know from what you are assuming → decide whether the gap is material to the decision → if not, proceed and state the assumption in writing → if yes, deliver the part that is solid, name the gap, give a time for the rest.

The principle: "Incomplete and labelled is fine. Incomplete and unlabelled is the problem." This is the answer to the false-choice tactic in Part 11.3, and it works for almost any case-study question too.

11.7 The CV grill

A good interviewer takes one line of your CV and goes three levels down. Level one is what you did. Level two is how you did it. Level three is whether you actually understood it. Most candidates are solid at level one, fine at level two, and fall apart at level three.

Eight attacks, and how to hold

The attackHow to hold your frame
1 "That sounds like it was mostly your team, not you." Claim your part precisely and give the others theirs. "The analysis was mine, the client relationship wasn't. What I owned specifically was..." Over-claiming here is fatal; under-claiming is nearly as bad.
2 "Why did you choose that approach?" Have a reason that is not "that's what we were told to do." If it was, say so and add what you would do differently now.
3 "What would you do differently?" Have a real answer, and make it a judgement change, not a resource complaint. "More time" is not an answer.
4 "What was the actual outcome?" If you do not know, say so: "I left before it concluded, so I genuinely don't know — what I can tell you is what we'd expected and why." Never invent a result.
5 "That doesn't sound very impressive." Do not inflate in response. "It was a small piece of work. What made it useful to me was..." Re-anchoring on the learning beats defending the scale.
6 "Explain that to me as if I don't know the sector." This is the job interview inside the job interview. Take it seriously. No jargon, one concrete analogy, sixty seconds. Then check: "Does that land, or shall I take a different angle?"
7 "Walk me through the numbers again — that doesn't add up." Slow down. Go back to the source. If you cannot reconcile it live: "I can't square that from memory and I don't want to guess at it. I'll send you the workings." That answer is fine here for exactly the reasons it is fine in the job.
8 "Why did you leave?" / "Why this gap?" One clean sentence, no defensiveness, no criticism of anyone, then forward. Length signals discomfort more than content does.
The four-line drill — do this for every bullet on your CV
  1. What I did, in one sentence, with my part clearly separated from the team's.
  2. One number attached to it, that I can defend and explain the source of.
  3. The hardest judgement call I made, and why I made it that way.
  4. What I'd do differently, phrased as a change in judgement rather than a change in circumstances.

Four lines per bullet. If your CV has ten bullets that is forty lines, which is about an hour of work and covers perhaps 70% of everything you will be asked. There is no higher-return preparation available to you.

"Why Fidelity International?"

Three lengths, a table of what everyone else will say against what you can say, and an honest list of reasons you might not want this job. Built from mechanism, not adjectives. Test: if you could swap in "Schroders" and the answer still worked, it is not finished.

12.1 Thirty seconds

"Fidelity International is one of very few active managers that both makes the funds and owns the distribution around them — its own retail platform, its own adviser platform, its own workplace pensions business — and it has been privately owned since splitting from the US Fidelity in 1980. That means it can invest through a cycle without a quarterly earnings call, and it also means it has to genuinely manage the conflict of selling its own funds on its own shelf. Investment Directing sits exactly on that seam: turning what the analysts and portfolio managers are doing into something a client can actually act on. That's the part of this industry I find most interesting — not picking the stock, but explaining and defending the decision to the people whose money it is." Roughly 90 words. Say it in about 30 seconds. Do not speed up.

12.2 Two minutes

Build outward from the same spine. Four beats, roughly 30 seconds each.

  1. The structure, with a number. The 30-second version, plus the audited UK figures: £189.5bn managed and £104.4bn administered[S78]. Add that you had to get those from Companies House filings because the parent is private and publishes no group accounts — which tells them how you research.

12.5 The counter-case: honest reasons not to want this

Think these through. If one of them genuinely bothers you, better to know now than in March 2027.

  • It is not an investing job. The duties are support and translation: material, reporting, sourcing other people's views, project work. If what you actually want is to generate ideas, this is adjacent to that, not inside it. Interviewers can tell, and they will ask.
  • The output is constrained by design. You will spend real time on wording, sign-off and version control. If that reads to you as bureaucracy rather than craft, ten weeks will feel long.
  • You are defending a proposition under pressure. Most active funds underperform their benchmark over most horizons[S307][S308]. If you find the passive argument more convincing than the active one, you should reckon with that honestly rather than spend a career arguing against your own view.
  • Less transparency than a listed firm. The clearest ownership figure available appears to be from 2012[S79]. If you want an employer whose numbers you can fully see, this is not that.
  • It has been cutting. Around 1,000 roles globally in March 2024[S142], UK headcount down about 13%[S78], and senior investment turnover. A stable private firm is not the same as a firm that is not under pressure.

Part 14

Glossary

80 terms. Every one of these appears somewhere in this pack, defined in plain English. If you can define thirty of them without looking, you are ahead of almost every other applicant.

Active management
Choosing investments in an attempt to beat a benchmark, rather than tracking it. Costs more; most funds do not beat their benchmark over long horizons.
Active share
How much a portfolio's holdings differ from its benchmark's, as a percentage. Low active share plus a high fee is the "closet tracker" problem.
Adviser platform
Technology through which financial advisers hold and administer client investments. Fidelity Adviser Solutions is one.
Alpha
Return above what the benchmark explains. The thing active management claims to produce.
AMC
Annual management charge. The manager's own fee, distinct from the fund's total costs.
Anti-greenwashing rule
FCA rule in force from 31 May 2024 requiring any sustainability claim about a product or service to be fair, clear and not misleading.
Assessment of Value
An annual public report a UK fund manager must publish judging whether each of its funds delivers value. In force since September 2019.
Attribution
Breaking a portfolio's return against its benchmark into causes: stock selection, sector or asset allocation, duration, currency.
AUA
Assets under administration. Money held on a platform that the firm may not manage. Earns a thinner service fee than AUM.
AUM
Assets under management. Money the firm actually invests, and charges a management fee on.
AUMA
Assets under management and administration combined. A wider measure; check which one a figure refers to.
Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of one per cent. The unit fees are quoted in. 35 bps on £1,000 is £3.50 a year.
Benchmark
The index or yardstick a fund is measured against. Choosing a flattering one is a conduct issue, not a presentational choice.
Beta
Sensitivity to the market as a whole. Broadly, the return you get for simply being invested.
Buy list
The approved list of funds a wealth manager, platform or consultant will recommend. Getting on it is the commercial prize.
CASS
The FCA's Client Assets sourcebook, governing client money and custody assets. Fidelity's UK board runs a dedicated CASS Oversight Forum.
CCI
Consumer Composite Investments. The UK regime replacing PRIIPs, commenced 6 April 2026 with full implementation by 8 June 2027.
Client Portfolio Manager (CPM)
The industry-standard name for the seat Fidelity calls Investment Director. A client-facing investment professional who represents a strategy but does not run it.
Closed-ended
A fund with a fixed number of shares, such as an investment trust. The share price can differ from the value of the underlying assets.
COBS
The FCA's Conduct of Business sourcebook. COBS 4 governs financial promotions and is the rulebook this role lives inside.
COCON
The FCA's Code of Conduct sourcebook, containing the Individual Conduct Rules that apply to almost everyone at a regulated firm.
Composite
A grouping of similar portfolios whose combined performance is presented to prospective clients, constructed to GIPS rules so a firm cannot show only its best account.
Consultant
An adviser to institutional investors, such as Mercer, WTW or Aon, that rates managers and effectively gatekeeps institutional searches.
Consumer Duty
FCA regime published 27 July 2022 as PS22/9, in force for open products 31 July 2023. Four outcomes: products and services, price and value, consumer understanding, consumer support.
Custodian
The institution that physically holds a fund's assets, separately from the manager who decides what to buy.
DB pension
Defined benefit. Promises a set income; the employer carries the investment risk. Largely closed and de-risking in the UK.
DC pension
Defined contribution. Builds a pot; the member carries the risk. Where UK savings growth and policy attention sit.
DDQ
Due diligence questionnaire. A structured set of questions about how a firm is run, answered largely from an approved library.
Default fund
Where a workplace pension member's money goes if they make no choice. Most members never choose, so the default carries enormous responsibility.
Depositary
An independent body that oversees a fund and polices the manager's compliance with the rules.
Drawdown
Two meanings. The peak-to-trough fall in a portfolio's value; and, in pensions, taking income from a pot rather than buying an annuity.
Duration
A bond portfolio's sensitivity to interest rate changes. Longer duration means bigger moves for a given rate change.
ETF
Exchange-traded fund. A fund traded on an exchange like a share. Historically index-tracking; increasingly also actively managed.
Factsheet
The standard monthly or quarterly one- or two-page fund summary. A core output of this role and a regulated communication.
Fee compression
The long-run fall in what managers can charge. Active funds now average under 60 bps against roughly 10 bps for passive.
Financial promotion
In FCA terms, an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity. Most of what this team produces is one.
Flows
Money coming in (inflows) and going out (outflows). Net flows is the difference, and is the number the function is ultimately judged on.
Four-eyes principle
Nothing client-facing goes out on one person's say-so. At least one preparer and one independent reviewer.
Fulcrum fee
A fee that moves with performance. Fidelity's version ran from July 2018 with a 0.65% base flexing between 0.45% and 0.85%, withdrawn from open-ended funds in May 2021.
GIPS
Global Investment Performance Standards. Rules for calculating and presenting performance, widely required in institutional RFPs.
Greenwashing
Overstating the sustainability characteristics of a product. Now specifically prohibited by an FCA rule.
Illiquid
Hard to sell quickly at a fair price. Property and private assets are illiquid, which changes how they must be explained and valued.
Information barrier
Also "Chinese wall". A control that stops sensitive information passing between parts of a firm. Governed by FCA SYSC 10.
Institutional
Large professional investors: pension schemes, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, endowments.
Investment Director
Fidelity International's house title for the client-facing investment specialist seat. Junior grade: Associate Investment Director.
Investment trust
A closed-ended, listed company whose business is holding investments. Has an independent board and one share class.
ISA
Individual Savings Account. A UK tax wrapper, not an investment in itself.
KID / KIID
Key information document. The short standardised disclosure a retail investor receives. Being replaced under the CCI regime.
LTAF
Long-Term Asset Fund. A UK structure letting long-term savers hold illiquid private-market assets. Fidelity was authorised for its first in August 2025.
Mandate
An instruction to manage money to agreed rules. "Winning a mandate" means being hired.
Mansion House Accord
Signed 13 May 2025. Seventeen DC providers committed to 10% of default funds in private markets by 2030, with 5% ringfenced for UK assets.
MiFID II
EU and UK regime in force from January 2018. Unbundled research from dealing commission and introduced product governance and target markets.
MNPI
Material non-public information. Price-sensitive information not yet public. Acting or dealing on it is market abuse.
Multi asset
Portfolios holding several asset classes at once. One of Fidelity's five investment groups.
NAV
Net asset value. What a fund's assets are worth per unit or share, usually struck daily.
OCF
Ongoing charges figure. The total annual cost of running a fund, including the AMC. The number an investor should actually compare.
OEIC
Open-ended investment company. A standard UK pooled fund structure; grows and shrinks as investors buy and sell.
Open-ended
A fund that issues and cancels units as money arrives and leaves, so the price tracks the underlying assets.
Passive
Tracking an index rather than trying to beat it. Cheap, and taking the large majority of new money.
Performance fee
An additional charge levied if a fund beats a hurdle. Rewards risk-taking rather than asset-gathering.
PM proxy
A portfolio manager's stand-in for client-facing work. Fidelity's own advert uses this phrase for the Investment Director role.
PRIIPs
The disclosure regime behind the KID, now being replaced in the UK by the CCI regime.
Product governance
The MiFID II requirement to define a fund's target market and review it over time, and to equip distributors to sell only into that market.
RFP
Request for proposal. The formal document a prospective client or consultant issues when hiring a manager. Scored against competitors.
SDR
Sustainability Disclosure Requirements. The FCA regime from PS23/16, published 28 November 2023, including labels and fund naming rules.
Segregated mandate
A single client's own portfolio, run to its own rules, rather than a pooled fund.
Share class
Different versions of the same fund with different fees, currencies or minimums. A frequent source of performance figures that do not match.
Sharpe ratio
Return earned per unit of volatility taken. A measure of risk-adjusted performance.
SICAV
A common continental European pooled fund structure, broadly the equivalent of a UK OEIC.
SIPP
Self-invested personal pension. A UK pension wrapper allowing the holder to choose investments.
SM&CR
Senior Managers and Certification Regime. Makes named individuals accountable. Extended to asset managers from December 2019.
SYSC 10
The FCA rules on conflicts of interest and information barriers. The framework governing selling your own funds on your own platform.
Target market
The defined group of investors a product is designed for. Marketing must be consistent with it.
Tracking error
How much a fund's returns have deviated from its benchmark, annualised. Not the same as active share.
UCITS
An EU regulatory standard for retail funds, widely trusted and used well beyond Europe.
Unit trust
A UK pooled fund structure holding assets in trust for investors. Practically similar to an OEIC for the investor.
Value chain
The full sequence from saver to security: adviser, platform, manager, broker, custodian, depositary, administrator.
Wall-crossing
Being formally brought inside a wall and given inside information, with the obligation to log it and not deal. The FCA's 2015 thematic review set out good and poor practice.
Wholesale
The intermediary market: fund selectors, wealth managers, private banks. Distinct from both retail and institutional.
Workplace pensions
Pensions provided through an employer. One of Fidelity International's three UK distribution channels.

Part 16

What could not be found

Every research pack has holes. Most hide them. These are this one's, listed so that you never state something confidently that rests on nothing, and so you know which questions are genuinely worth asking on the day.

16.1 The blocking constraint

Fidelity International's corporate website, fidelityinternational.com, returned access-denied responses to every automated request made during this research. Its annual report and its MIFIDPRU Pillar 3 disclosure could not be retrieved at all. The careers subdomain was reachable and was used extensively.

The workaround, and it is a good one: the UK subsidiaries are UK companies and must file audited accounts at Companies House. That is where the hard financial numbers in Part 2 come from. But it means this pack has excellent UK data and weak global data, and you should know which is which when you speak.

A closing thought on honesty

The instinct this section is training is the same one the job itself requires. An Investment Director who says "I don't know, I'll check, here's what I can tell you now" is trusted. One who fills gaps with confident approximations is eventually found out, usually by a client with a spreadsheet. The habit that makes you good in the interview is the same habit that makes you good in the seat. That is not a coincidence, and it is worth saying out loud if you get the chance.
