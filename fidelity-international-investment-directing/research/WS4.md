# WORKSTREAM 4 — THE ASSET MANAGEMENT INDUSTRY FROM ZERO, AND THE INVESTMENT-SPECIALIST SUB-INDUSTRY

# 1. FINDINGS

## 4A.1 What the product actually is

An asset manager takes other people's money and invests it. The "product" is the legal wrapper the money sits in plus the investment decisions made inside it. In plain English:

- **Pooled fund**: many investors' money is mixed into one vehicle. Everyone owns a slice (a unit or share) of the whole pool, priced daily off the value of the underlying assets. This is the retail default because it is cheap to run and diversified even for a small saver.
- **Segregated mandate**: one client (usually a pension scheme, insurer or sovereign wealth fund) has its own portfolio managed to its own rules, not pooled with anyone else. Used when the client is big enough (often nine or ten figures) to want bespoke restrictions (e.g. excluding certain sectors) and its own custody arrangements.
- **Unit trust**: the classic UK open-ended pooled fund, structured as a trust. Investors hold "units"; a trustee holds the assets for their benefit. Quotes a bid price (sell) and offer price (buy) with a spread built in. [S208][S209]
- **OEIC (Open-Ended Investment Company)**: the modern UK equivalent of a unit trust, structured as a company rather than a trust, so investors hold shares, not units. First appeared in the UK in 1997 and has largely superseded new unit trust launches because it has a single price rather than a bid/offer spread. [S208][S209]
- **SICAV (Société d'Investissement à Capital Variable)**: the continental European (chiefly Luxembourg-domiciled) equivalent — an open-ended investment company with variable capital, used heavily for cross-border European fund ranges. [S209]
- **UCITS (Undertakings for Collective Investment in Transferable Securities)**: not a product in itself but an EU/UK regulatory passport. A fund structured as a UCITS (whether a unit trust, OEIC or SICAV) can be sold to retail investors across Europe under one authorisation, subject to strict diversification and liquidity rules. Most mainstream UK and European retail funds are UCITS. [S209][S210]
- **Investment trust**: a closed-ended, listed public limited company. It issues a fixed number of shares at launch (occasionally more via further issuance) and does not create or cancel shares to meet demand. Its shares trade on the stock exchange and can sit at a premium or discount to the net asset value (NAV) of the underlying portfolio, because supply and demand for the shares is separate from the value of the assets. It can also borrow (gearing) to invest, which open-ended funds generally cannot do to the same extent. [S208][S209]
- **ETF (exchange-traded fund)**: legally similar to an OEIC (open-ended, company-like) but its shares trade continuously on an exchange all day, priced by the market (with an arbitrage mechanism keeping it close to NAV), rather than being bought and sold once a day at a manager-calculated price. Historically almost all passive/index-tracking; increasingly also used as a wrapper for active strategies (see 4A.5 below on ETF share classes). [S208][S209]
- **Investment trust vs ETF vs OEIC, summarised**: OEICs and unit trusts are priced once a day and are open-ended (the manager creates/cancels units to match demand); ETFs are open-ended but trade all day on an exchange; investment trusts are closed-ended and trade on an exchange at a price set by supply and demand, which can diverge from NAV.
- **Model portfolio (Model Portfolio Service, MPS)**: a ready-made basket of funds or securities built by a discretionary manager for financial advisers to put their retail clients into, rather than the adviser choosing single funds. It sits at the boundary between fund management and wealth management, and is one of the fastest-growing distribution channels in UK retail because it lets advisers outsource investment selection at scale.

## 4A.2 Who the customers are, and how they buy differently

- **Retail savers**: buy directly (via an investment platform such as Hargreaves Lansdown or an employer pension) or through an adviser. Decisions are driven by past performance, brand, cost (increasingly visible since cost-disclosure reforms) and platform "buy lists." Very price and story sensitive; typically buy OEICs, unit trusts, ETFs and increasingly model portfolios.
- **Financial advisers and wealth managers**: buy on behalf of clients, so they weight process, risk-rating fit, and increasingly total cost of ownership including their own platform and advice fee stacked on top of the fund's fee. They are gatekeepers — getting onto an adviser's "recommended" or "buy" list is a distribution win in itself.
- **Platforms** (e.g. wrap platforms, workplace pension platforms): are not end-buyers but distribution chokepoints. A fund has to be made available on a platform (often paying a listing/rebate arrangement or accepting a lower share class fee) before an adviser or retail investor can access it there.
- **DC (defined contribution) pension schemes**: the member bears the investment risk; the scheme trustee or provider selects a default fund most members never change. Because it is one decision affecting thousands of members' contributions, DC scheme selection is high-volume, fee-sensitive, and increasingly directed at consolidated "master trusts."
- **DB (defined benefit) pension schemes**: the employer/scheme bears the risk of meeting promised pensions, so mandates are chosen for liability-matching (bonds, LDI strategies) and increasingly for buy-out/buy-in with insurers as schemes close and de-risk. Many DB schemes in the UK are now shrinking or being bought out, a structural driver behind the DC consolidation push described in 4A.6.
- **Insurers**: buy asset management as an input to their own balance sheets (matching insurance liabilities) and also distribute funds through insurance wrappers; often run their own in-house asset manager (e.g. an insurance group with a captive asset management arm).
- **Sovereign wealth funds and endowments**: sophisticated, long-horizon institutional buyers who typically use segregated mandates or funds-of-one, run extensive due diligence (DDQs, on-site visits) and negotiate fees directly, often paying less per basis point than a retail investor for the same strategy.

## 4A.3 The value chain from saver to security

Money moves through several distinct firms, each doing one job:

1. **Distributor** (adviser, platform, bank, workplace pension provider) — brings the saver's money in and chooses which products to offer.
2. **Platform** — administers the wrapper (ISA, SIPP, GIA) the saver holds the investment in, and executes the saver's fund switches.
3. **Fund manager / asset manager** — makes the investment decisions inside the fund or mandate.
4. **Market maker / broker** — executes the actual buy and sell orders for securities in the market on the fund manager's instruction.
5. **Custodian** — holds the underlying securities safely, separate from the fund manager, so the manager can never simply run off with the assets.
6. **Depositary** — for UK/EU authorised funds, a regulated entity (often part of a custodian bank) with a statutory oversight duty over the fund manager, monitoring cash flows and safekeeping and required to challenge the manager if something looks wrong. Legally distinct from a custodian even though often provided by the same banking group.
7. **Transfer agent** — keeps the register of who owns which units/shares in the fund, processes subscriptions and redemptions, and pays out dividends/income.
8. **Fund administrator** — calculates the daily NAV, handles fund accounting, and produces the numbers everyone else relies on.
9. **Index provider** (e.g. a benchmark index house) — defines the benchmark the fund is measured against or, for a passive fund, the index it tracks; charges licensing fees for that.

## 4A.4 How firms differ: active vs passive, and the fee models

- **Active management**: the manager tries to beat a benchmark by picking securities, charging a fee (often 50-100+ basis points a year for equities) regardless of whether they succeed, sometimes topped up with a performance fee.
- **Passive/index management**: the manager simply replicates a benchmark as cheaply as possible; fees have fallen to roughly 10 basis points on average across passive funds as competition has intensified. [S217]
- **Hedge fund**: typically a segregated, less liquid structure aimed at absolute return (positive return in any market condition) rather than beating a benchmark, historically charging both a management fee and a performance fee ("2 and 20" being the old shorthand, now often lower).
- **Private equity / private markets manager**: takes committed capital, locks it up for years, invests directly in companies or assets, and is paid a management fee on committed/invested capital plus **carried interest** — typically around a fifth of the profit above a minimum return hurdle, paid to the manager once investors have got their capital and hurdle return back. This "carry" model means the manager is paid mostly on realised outperformance over a multi-year horizon, unlike an asset manager paid an ongoing fee whether or not the fund does well.
- **Wealth manager**: manages money directly for individuals (often via model portfolios or bespoke discretionary mandates) and typically charges an all-in percentage fee for both advice/relationship and investment management combined.
- **Investment bank**: does not manage other people's money as its core business; it originates and trades securities, advises on M&A and capital raising, and often distributes (sells) third-party or in-house funds to its wealth or institutional client base — a different economic model (transaction and advisory fees, not ongoing AUM fees).
- Why this matters: a fee charged as a percentage of assets under management (AUM) rewards a manager for growing assets and staying invested, even in flat markets, and its revenue automatically rises and falls with markets. A performance fee or carried interest rewards absolute outperformance but is far lumpier and harder to predict, and it changes what a firm is willing to risk to earn its pay.

## 4A.5 The economics

A **basis point (bp)** is one hundredth of one percent (0.01%). A fee of "50bps" on a £1 billion fund is £5 million a year. Typical 2025-2026 fee levels: average asset-weighted expense ratios for active funds have fallen to below 60bps, while passive fund fees average around 10bps. [S217] Product mix matters more than headline AUM for profitability: in one 2026 industry analysis, passive strategies made up around 30% of one large sample of managers' AUM but only about 7% of their revenue, while alternatives (private markets, hedge-fund style strategies) made up about 18% of AUM but around 57% of revenue. [S195] Scale matters because most of an asset manager's costs (technology, compliance, research, distribution headcount) are largely fixed regardless of how many assets sit on the platform, so a manager that doubles AUM without doubling headcount improves its margin; this is one of the structural drivers of industry consolidation. Fee compression is described by industry analysts as "existential" because passive and low-cost products keep pushing headline prices down while operating costs (technology, data, regulation, personalisation) keep rising, squeezing margin from both sides. [S195][S217]

## 4A.6 Core vocabulary (plain English)

- **AUM** (assets under management): the total value of everything a manager runs on behalf of clients. **AUA** (assets under administration): assets a firm administers or services (e.g. platform or custodian) without making investment decisions on them — a much lower-margin, higher-volume business than AUM.
- **Net flows**: money coming in (gross sales) minus money going out (redemptions) over a period; the single most-watched health metric for a fund range because it shows whether new money believes in the product, independent of market movements.
- **Benchmark**: the index or peer average a fund is measured against.
- **Tracking error**: how much a fund's returns deviate from its benchmark over time — low for a passive fund by design, higher for an active fund taking bigger bets.
- **Active share**: the percentage of a fund's holdings that differ from its benchmark's holdings; used to show whether an "active" fund is genuinely different from an index or is a disguised "closet tracker."
- **Alpha**: the return a manager generates above what the benchmark/market would explain. **Beta**: the return simply explained by moving with the market. **Information ratio**: alpha per unit of the risk taken to generate it (tracking error). **Sharpe ratio**: return per unit of total risk (volatility), independent of any benchmark.
- **Attribution**: breaking down why a portfolio did or didn't beat its benchmark (which sectors, stocks, currency calls etc. contributed).
- **Drawdown**: the peak-to-trough fall in a portfolio's value before it recovers — a key risk statistic clients ask about.
- **TER / OCF** (Total Expense Ratio / Ongoing Charges Figure): the all-in annual cost of running the fund as a percentage of assets, including the manager's fee and other running costs, excluding trading costs and performance fees. **AMC** (Annual Management Charge): just the manager's own fee, a subset of the TER/OCF.
- **Share classes**: the same underlying fund can be split into different classes (e.g. retail vs institutional, income vs accumulation, hedged vs unhedged, or now ETF vs mutual fund) each with a different fee or feature but the same portfolio.
- **Fund selector / consultant rating**: a specialist researcher (at a platform, discretionary manager, or an investment consultancy such as those rating pension mandates) who screens and rates funds/managers for buyers; a high rating (e.g. top-tier "buy" rating) is a major distribution lever.
- **RFP** (Request for Proposal) and **DDQ** (Due Diligence Questionnaire): the standard documents institutional buyers use to interrogate a manager's process, people, performance and operations before appointing them; producing accurate, current answers to these at speed is a core job of investment specialists (see Part 4B).
- **Factsheet**: a short, usually monthly, standardised document showing a fund's performance, holdings and commentary, produced for every fund.
- **KIID / KID** (Key Investor Information Document / Key Information Document): short, standardised, pre-contractual disclosure documents retail investors must be given before buying a fund, historically required by UCITS rules (KIID) and by the PRIIPs regulation (KID) — now being replaced in the UK by the Consumer Composite Investments regime (see Part 4B).
- **Prospectus**: the full legal offering document for a fund or listed security, setting out its objectives, risks, charges and terms in detail.
- **ACD** (Authorised Corporate Director): the FCA-authorised firm legally responsible for running a UK OEIC, equivalent to the trustee/manager function for a unit trust.
- **NAV** (Net Asset Value): the value of a fund's assets minus its liabilities, usually expressed per unit/share — the number a pooled fund is priced at.
- **Dealing**: the process of buying/selling units or shares in a fund, usually on a specified daily cycle (a "dealing point").
- **Liquidity**: how easily and quickly an asset can be sold without moving its price — central to whether an open-ended fund can safely offer daily dealing on illiquid underlying assets (a recurring regulatory concern, especially for property funds).
- **Segregated mandate**: as above, a portfolio run for one client only.
- **ESG / stewardship**: ESG (environmental, social, governance) factors used in investment analysis; stewardship is the active use of share ownership rights (voting, engaging with company management) to influence how investee companies behave.

## 4A.7 The structural forces reshaping the sector now (2026), and three competing views on where it goes

Confirmed current-state facts: the UK investment management industry's total AUM reached a record £11.1 trillion in 2025, up 11% from £10.0 trillion in 2024, the second consecutive year of double-digit growth. [S150][S151] Retail investors now account for around 30% of UK-managed assets, having overtaken pension funds (around 25%) as the largest client group — a structural reversal of what was, until 2020, a roughly 80/20 institutional/retail split. [S150][S152]

Fee compression, passive substitution, and margin pressure are the dominant near-term facts, not a matter of opinion: average active fund fees have fallen below 60bps and passive fees average around 10bps [S217]; profitability has been described as being on a "plateau" because passive assets generate disproportionately little revenue relative to their AUM share while costs (technology, data, compliance, personalised reporting) keep rising [S195].

Beyond that shared diagnosis, credible industry sources genuinely disagree about what happens next over 3-5 years:

- **View 1 — distribution, not investment performance, becomes the decisive edge.** Boston Consulting Group's *Global Asset Management Report 2026* argues that "distribution has replaced investment performance as the primary competitive advantage" for asset managers, and that AI could deliver cost reductions of 25-35% while increasing client coverage three-to-five-fold; BCG frames the next few years as growth becoming "harder to capture, more unevenly distributed" and increasingly dependent on distribution strength and technology, not manager skill. (BCG, *Global Asset Management Report 2026*, published 2026; press release "Competition in Asset Management Shifts as Growth Becomes Harder and More Concentrated," 28 April 2026.) [S192][S193][S194]
- **View 2 — the industry has hit a "profitability plateau" and must pivot into private markets and cost discipline to escape it.** Coalition Greenwich (25 February 2026) argues that because passive strategies (about 30% of AUM but only about 7% of revenue in its sample) and alternatives (about 18% of AUM but about 57% of revenue) are so mismatched, managers' next moves over the next few years will be: balancing scale with premium differentiation, AI-driven institutional sales, a shift toward higher-margin private markets products, and disciplined cost control — with success uncertain ("whether managers successfully execute these initiatives... particularly whether they can slow and reverse margin compression" is treated as an open question, not a given). [S195]
- **View 3 — consolidation and capabilities-led M&A, not organic reinvention, is how the industry will actually restructure.** PwC's *Global Asset and Wealth Management Sector Outlook 2026* (survey of 264 executives across North America, Europe and Asia, fieldwork August-October 2025) expects wealth and asset management deal activity to remain resilient through 2026, with consolidation, AI-driven differentiation and capabilities-focused acquisitions outweighing macro pressure — i.e., firms buy their way into new capabilities (private markets, technology, distribution reach) rather than each firm reinventing itself alone. [S191]
- **A fourth, more cautious view worth noting for balance:** Morningstar's long-running research frames the shift more as a slow, structural erosion of active managers' edge than an industry crisis — noting fee compression will "persist longer term" as low-cost funds and platform pressure keep pushing base fees down, while also showing tentative signs that active management is not simply disappearing (just over 40% of active funds beat their average passive peer over the 12 months to June 2026, an improvement on recent history but still a minority). (Morningstar magazine, "New Era for Asset Managers," Q4 2024; Morningstar, "Active Fund Manager Success Rates Ticked Up in 2026, but Passive Funds Still Hold the Advantage," 2026.) [S217][S218]

Other forces confirmed but not yet fully resolved as of September 2026: the "retailisation" of private markets is accelerating, with UK Long-Term Asset Fund (LTAF) assets reportedly growing from about £5 billion in 2025 to about £7.3 billion in 2026 and more than 25 LTAFs now on the market [S214]; and active managers are increasingly launching ETF share classes of existing funds (Dimensional launched the first actively managed ETF-as-a-share-class in the US in 2026, and Fidelity [the Boston-headquartered Fidelity Investments/FMR, **not** Fidelity International] launched its first ETF share classes of mutual funds in June 2026) — a US-led trend not yet confirmed as live in the UK. [S211][S212][S213]
