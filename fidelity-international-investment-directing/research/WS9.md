## 1. FINDINGS

### A. The fair, clear, not misleading rule and how performance can be shown (FCA COBS 4)

The core rule that governs everything a UK regulated firm tells a client is COBS 4.2.1R, in the FCA Handbook's Conduct of Business Sourcebook. It says, word for word, "A firm must ensure that a communication or a financial promotion is fair, clear and not misleading." [S340][Confirmed] This applies to any communication to a client and to any financial promotion (marketing) the firm sends out or approves, whether to retail or professional clients or eligible counterparties, although the level of detail expected differs by audience. [S340][Confirmed]

Under COBS 4.2.5G, a firm cannot describe something as "guaranteed", "protected" or "secure" unless that is genuinely true and the firm gives all the information needed, with enough prominence, to justify the word. [S340][Confirmed] If a firm can show it took reasonable steps to comply, COBS 4.2.6R says a breach does not automatically give a client a right to sue for damages, but it can still be a regulatory breach that the FCA can act on. [S340][Confirmed]

COBS 4.5 deals with the detail of how information must be laid out, including comparisons. COBS 4.5.6R says that if a promotion compares one investment, business or person with another, the comparison must be "meaningful and presented in a fair and balanced way". [S342][Confirmed] COBS 4.5.2R adds that information must be accurate, must give a "fair and prominent indication of any relevant risks" whenever it references benefits, must be pitched so "the average member of the group" it is aimed at can understand it, must not "disguise, diminish or obscure important items, statements or warnings", and risk warnings must be printed in a font at least as large as the main text. [S342][Confirmed] In plain English: you cannot bury the risk warning in small print at the bottom while the return figure is in bold at the top. That is exactly the kind of thing a sales-facing pitchbook can get wrong under time pressure.

COBS 4.6 is the specific rule set on past, simulated and future performance, and it is the one most directly relevant to an Investment Directing intern building a factsheet or pitchbook. Under COBS 4.6.2R, past performance:
- must not be "the most prominent feature of the communication" [S341][Confirmed]
- must cover the preceding five years, or the whole life of the investment if shorter, shown in complete 12-month periods, not partial or cherry-picked slices [S341][Confirmed]
- must state the reference period and the source of the data clearly [S341][Confirmed]
- must carry "a prominent warning that the figures refer to the past and that past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results" [S341][Confirmed]
- must disclose currency and the effect of commissions, fees and taxes where relevant [S341][Confirmed]

Simulated (backtested) past performance is allowed under COBS 4.6.6R only if it is based on the real past performance of the same or a substantially similar investment or index, follows the same presentation conditions as real past performance, and carries its own separate prominent warning that the numbers are simulated, not real. [S341][Confirmed] Future performance forecasts under COBS 4.6.7R cannot be based on simulated past performance, must rest on "reasonable assumptions supported by objective data", should show both positive and negative scenarios, and must carry a prominent warning that they are not a reliable indicator of future performance. [S341][Confirmed] So the "three golden rules" a candidate can quote in interview are: don't lead with performance, use whole 12-month blocks not a flattering start date, and always attach the standard warning at equal prominence.

### B. Consumer Duty (PS22/9): the outcome that bites hardest on marketing teams

The Consumer Duty is not one rule but an overarching standard, set out in the FCA's Policy Statement PS22/9, "A new Consumer Duty". [S343][Confirmed] It rests on three cross-cutting rules that firms must apply to how they treat retail customers: act in good faith towards them, avoid causing them foreseeable harm, and enable and support them to pursue their financial objectives. [S363][Confirmed] Underneath these sit four outcomes: products and services, price and value, consumer understanding, and consumer support. [S363][Confirmed] The Duty came into force for new and existing products on sale to retail customers from 31 July 2023, and for older ("closed") products and services that are no longer sold but still held by customers from 31 July 2024. [S363][Confirmed]

The one most relevant to an Investment Directing desk is the consumer understanding outcome: communications must be likely to be understood by the customers they are aimed at, which for retail-facing material now means firms are expected to test communications, not just draft them and hope. The FCA's own account of good practice describes firms testing communications both before and after they change them, using tools like surveys, comprehension checks and A/B testing, then verifying whether understanding actually improved and keeping the results on file to show the regulator if asked. [S343][Confirmed] Firms are not required to test every single communication, but they are expected to be able to show a working process, not just a policy document that says they do this. [S343][Confirmed]

### C. The anti-greenwashing rule and the SDR labelling regime (PS23/16)

The FCA's anti-greenwashing rule came into force on 31 May 2024, having been finalised in guidance document FG24/3 published on 23 April 2024. [S345][Confirmed] It applies to all FCA-authorised firms, not only fund managers, and it requires that "sustainability-related claims about their products and services must be fair, clear and not misleading" — essentially COBS 4.2's standard, but specifically targeted at ESG and sustainability language. [S345][Confirmed]

The anti-greenwashing rule sits inside the wider Sustainability Disclosure Requirements and investment labels regime, set out in Policy Statement PS23/16, published 28 November 2023. [S347][Confirmed] That regime created four investment labels for funds seeking to make sustainability claims: Sustainability Focus, Sustainability Improvers, Sustainability Impact, and Sustainability Mixed Goals. [S348][Reported] The rollout dates are staged:
- 31 May 2024: anti-greenwashing rule in force for all authorised firms [S345][Confirmed]
- 31 July 2024: UK fund managers can start using the four labels, with accompanying disclosures [S348][Reported]
- 2 December 2024: naming and marketing rules come into force, meaning a fund's name can only use sustainability-related words (like "green", "ESG", "sustainable", "impact") if it is consistent with the fund's actual characteristics and labelling status [S348][Reported]
- 2 December 2025: firms with over £50 billion AUM must start giving ongoing product-level and entity-level sustainability disclosures [S348][Reported]
- 2 December 2026: the same entity-level disclosure duty extends down to firms with more than £5 billion AUM [S348][Reported]

In plain English for an intern: you cannot put "sustainable" or "green" in a factsheet title or marketing line unless the fund is properly labelled or the wording is separately justified, and any ESG claim in a pitchbook has to be checked against the actual portfolio, not just against the brand's general reputation for responsible investing.

### D. Inside information, market abuse and wall-crossing (UK MAR)

Under the UK version of the Market Abuse Regulation (UK MAR), inside information is precise, non-public information which, if it became public, would be likely to have a significant effect on the price of a financial instrument. [S353][Confirmed] Insider dealing covers using that information to deal, or to recommend or induce someone else to deal, and it also covers cancelling or amending an existing order on the back of it. [S353][Confirmed] Unlawful disclosure covers passing inside information to anyone outside the normal, proper exercise of your job. [S353][Confirmed]

"Wall-crossing" is the practice of deliberately bringing someone inside a confidential perimeter so they can be given inside information for a specific, controlled purpose (for example, ahead of a capital raise), usually as part of what UK MAR calls a "market sounding". [S353][Confirmed] The regime sets out a formal process and record-keeping obligations for this so the disclosure is protected and does not itself become unlawful disclosure. [S353][Confirmed]

Investment managers use internal information barriers, commonly called "Chinese walls", to stop information moving between business areas that should not have it. FCA Handbook SYSC 10.2 defines a Chinese wall as an arrangement requiring information held in one part of a business "to be withheld from... persons with or for whom it acts in the course of carrying on another part" of the business, and firms must take reasonable steps to keep such arrangements effective and properly monitored. [S354][Confirmed] Individuals on the other side of a properly maintained wall are treated in law as not having the knowledge held on the other side, which is what allows a firm to keep managing money on one side of the wall while running a corporate or research function on the other. [S354][Confirmed]

This is exactly why an Investment Directing team, sitting close to portfolio managers, has to be careful. If a client or journalist asks "what is the fund doing with Stock X right now," or "have you changed the weighting in Sector Y this week," answering with anything beyond what is already public risks selective disclosure of inside information — even an innocuous-sounding comment like "we've been adding to that name" can amount to pre-announcing a trade or a portfolio change before it is public, which UK MAR is designed to prevent. The safe, correct habit is to talk only about published, historical positioning (the last quarter-end factsheet, the last public commentary) and to route anything current back to compliance or the fund manager rather than improvising an answer.

### E. Assessment of Value: how UK funds have to justify their fees

Authorised fund managers (AFMs) in the UK must carry out an annual Assessment of Value under COLL 6.6.20R (for standalone authorised funds) and COLL 8.5.17R (for authorised contractual schemes), and publish the conclusions. [S349][Confirmed] These rules came into force from September 2019, as one of the remedies that followed the FCA's 2017 Asset Management Market Study. [S349][Confirmed] The assessment must separately address at least seven minimum considerations: the range and quality of services provided, performance against appropriate benchmarks, the AFM's own costs of running the fund, economies of scale, comparable market rates for similar funds, comparable services offered by competitors, and a breakdown by unit class rather than just the fund as a whole. [S349][Confirmed]

The FCA's own multi-firm reviews found real weaknesses in how this is done in practice: many firms assumed their existing charges already reflected economies of scale without checking, some excluded their more expensive share classes from peer comparisons so high fees did not show up, and in general firms tended to "assess value in the abstract, rather than weighing up the value delivered against the costs." [S349][Confirmed] For an Investment Directing intern, the practical lesson is that you cannot talk about a fund's fee purely as a number in isolation in a pitch; the regulatory expectation is that fees are justified against value delivered, so any fee conversation with a client or in a document should be able to point to the substance behind the Assessment of Value disclosure, not just quote the OCF (ongoing charges figure).

### F. Performance reporting integrity: GIPS and the rule against showing only your best fund

The Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS) are voluntary ethical standards for calculating and presenting investment performance, built on "the ethical principles of fair representation and full disclosure," and they are developed, maintained and promoted by the CFA Institute together with GIPS sponsors and standards committees around the world. [S360][Confirmed] Firms that claim GIPS compliance build their performance track records around "composites": groupings of all the actual, fee-paying, discretionary portfolios that share a similar investment mandate, objective or strategy. The purpose of the composite structure, as widely understood in the industry, is to stop a firm hand-picking its single best-performing account or fund to show a prospective client while quietly leaving out the mediocre or poor performers running the same strategy. [Inferred] I could not fetch the underlying GIPS "Standards for Firms" text directly to quote its composite-construction rule verbatim (see Gaps), so this mechanism is described here as industry-standard practice rather than as a verbatim quoted rule.

The practical, real-world version of "don't just show your best fund" is one of the most common integrity questions a junior person on an Investment Directing desk will face: a salesperson wants a strong number for a pitchbook, and the honest, compliant answer is that the number has to be the actual composite or representative account for that strategy, checked and signed off, not a single flattering fund picked because its manager happened to have a good run.

### G. Compliance sign-off, records and how long you have to keep everything

Financial promotions are not just written and sent; firms must keep records. Under COBS 4.11.1R, a firm must keep "an adequate record" of any financial promotion it communicates or approves. [S361][Confirmed] The retention period depends on the type of promotion: three years for most promotions, five years for MiFID-scope business and for non-mass-market or restricted mass-market investment promotions, six years for promotions relating to life policies and pensions, and records relating to pension transfers, conversions, opt-outs and free-standing AVCs must be kept indefinitely. [S361][Confirmed]

Separately, since 7 February 2024, firms that want to approve financial promotions on behalf of unauthorised third parties (for example, an authorised firm approving a promotion for an unregulated fintech) must first get specific FCA permission to do so, under the new "financial promotion gateway" introduced by Policy Statement PS23/13 (published 11 September 2023, updated 16 November 2023). [S362][Confirmed] This does not directly govern an in-house Investment Directing team's own marketing (which is covered by ordinary COBS 4 and Consumer Duty obligations), but it shows the direction of travel: the FCA has been tightening, not loosening, the gatekeeping around who can put promotional material into the market and on what authority.

Within an asset manager, the practical workflow that sits on top of these rules is: marketing or investment directing drafts the material, the performance/reporting team certifies the numbers against the source system, compliance reviews it against COBS 4 and Consumer Duty standards and signs it off (often logged in a "compliance register" — an internal log of what was submitted, by whom, what was approved or rejected, and when), and only then can it go to a client or be published. This is standard "four-eyes" practice: no single person both prepares and approves the same piece of client-facing material.

### H. Real cases: the Woodford saga, and why it matters for how funds are marketed

The clearest, most citable real case for this seat is the collapse of the Woodford Equity Income Fund (LF Woodford Equity Income Fund, "WEIF"), because it directly concerns liquidity, marketing and the duties of the people responsible for telling investors what a fund is doing.

Link Fund Solutions Limited ("Link") was the Authorised Corporate Director (ACD) of WEIF from the fund's launch in May 2014. [S355][Confirmed] Over the period from 31 July 2018 to 3 June 2019, Link failed to manage the fund's liquidity properly even though the fund's own prospectus promised investors they could redeem within four days. [S355][Confirmed] The fund held a number of originally-unquoted stocks (including Sabina Estates Limited, Ombu Group, Industrial Heat LLC and Benevolent AI) that were later listed on The International Stock Exchange but for which there was, in practice, almost no real trading, so Link kept valuing them using fair value pricing even after listing, meaning they remained effectively illiquid despite technically being "listed" securities. [S355][Confirmed] After Kent County Council, then the fund's largest single investor, asked to redeem its full holding, Link suspended dealing in the fund on 3 June 2019. [S355][Confirmed] The fund was later wound up without reopening. [S355][Confirmed] At the point of suspension the fund was worth just over £3.5 billion. [S355][Confirmed]

The FCA's Final Notice against Link, dated 11 April 2024, found that Link had breached Principle 2 (a firm must conduct its business with due skill, care and diligence) and Principle 6 (a firm must pay due regard to the interests of its customers and treat them fairly) of the FCA's Principles for Businesses. [S355][Confirmed] The FCA said it would otherwise have proposed a financial penalty of £50,000,000 (reducible to £35,000,000 for early settlement), but did not impose it because doing so would have reduced the amount available to compensate investors; instead Link agreed a scheme of arrangement under which restitution of up to £298,403,919, but payable up to a cap of around £230 million (later reported as up to roughly £235 million once Link's parent's voluntary top-up was added), would go to affected investors. [S355][Confirmed][S357][Confirmed] The redress plan was announced on 19 April 2023, conditional on Link Group's sale of its fund solutions business completing and on court and investor approval of the scheme. [S357][Confirmed] First payments under the scheme were made in 2024. [S359][Reported]

Separately, and much more recently, the FCA turned its enforcement on the people who actually ran the money. On 5 August 2025, the FCA announced it had fined Neil Woodford £5,888,800 personally and Woodford Investment Management (WIM), his firm, £40,000,000 — a combined total of close to £46 million — for making "unreasonable and inappropriate investment decisions" between July 2018 and June 2019 by disproportionately selling liquid holdings while buying up illiquid ones, worsening the fund's liquidity right as its problems were mounting. [S356][Confirmed] The FCA found that, at the point of suspension, only 8% of the fund's holdings could have been sold within seven days even though investors had been promised access within four days. [S356][Confirmed] Woodford was also banned from ever holding a senior manager role again or managing money for retail investors. [S356][Confirmed] The FCA's joint executive director of enforcement and market oversight, Steve Smart, was quoted saying: "Being a leader in financial services comes with responsibilities as well as profile. Mr Woodford simply doesn't accept he had any role in managing the liquidity of the fund." [S356][Reported — this quote was captured via a summarisation tool reading the FCA's own press release, not read by me character-for-character on the raw page, so treat the exact wording with light caution even though the substance is confirmed]

For a candidate, the Woodford case is the single best real anchor for "how liquidity is described" and "why marketing a fund's flexibility or accessibility needs to be accurate": investors were told they could get their money out within days, the fund's underlying holdings could not actually support that, and the people responsible for both running the money and directing/overseeing the fund were found, years apart, to have failed their most basic duties around that mismatch.

A second, very recent and different kind of case worth knowing is Crispin Odey. On 14 September 2026, the Upper Tribunal upheld the FCA's decision to ban Odey, founder and majority owner of Odey Asset Management, from financial services for life, over "repeated and persistent inappropriate behaviour towards female employees" and, separately, for actively obstructing the firm's own internal disciplinary and governance process once it started investigating him, including dismissing the firm's executive committee to stop it. [S358][Confirmed] The Tribunal found he lacked integrity and showed a "lack of candour" in his dealings with the firm, its clients and investors, and the FCA itself, and reduced the FCA's proposed fine from £1.83 million to £1.53 million while upholding the ban. [S358][Confirmed] This case is less about marketing content and more about the "acting with integrity" and "open and cooperative with the regulator" conduct rules (see section on COCON below) — useful for a candidate who wants a live, non-marketing example of individual conduct rule failure at a well-known asset manager.

### I. What good escalation looks like: COCON, SM&CR, and speaking up

The FCA's Code of Conduct sourcebook (COCON) sets out Individual Conduct Rules that apply to almost everyone working at an FCA-regulated firm, with limited carve-outs for purely ancillary staff such as receptionists, cleaners or IT helpdesk workers who have no role in the regulated activity. [S351][Confirmed] The rules, taken directly from FCA Handbook COCON 2.1, are:

- Rule 1: You must act with integrity.
- Rule 2: You must act with due skill, care and diligence.
- Rule 3: You must be open and cooperative with the FCA, the PRA and other regulators.
- Rule 4: You must pay due regard to the interests of customers and treat them fairly.
- Rule 5: You must observe proper standards of market conduct.
- Rule 6: You must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers.
[S350][Confirmed]

Rules 1-5 have applied since 7 March 2016; Rule 6 was added later, taking effect 31 July 2023, to align individual accountability with the new Consumer Duty. [S350][Confirmed] For senior managers and staff who deal directly with clients (front-office, sales, advisory and, in practice, an Investment Directing desk that talks to clients), these are not abstract; a mis-stated performance number in a pitchbook is not just a marketing slip, it is potentially a Rule 1 or Rule 2 issue for whoever signed it off.

These conduct rules sit inside the wider Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR), which has three parts: the Senior Managers Regime (named individuals hold formal, FCA-approved accountability for specific areas of the business), the Certification Regime (firms themselves certify, at least annually, that staff in specified roles are fit and proper, without needing individual FCA pre-approval), and the Conduct Rules described above, which apply more broadly across the firm. [S352][Confirmed] SM&CR started with banks and larger firms in 2016 and was extended to almost all other FCA solo-regulated firms, including asset managers, from December 2019. [S352][Confirmed] The point of the whole regime is to make sure that when something goes wrong, there is a named, accountable individual, not a diffuse "the firm" to point to.

Good escalation culture in practice, and what a candidate should be able to describe, looks like this: if you spot something wrong (a factsheet number that does not tie out, a marketing line that oversells, anything that smells like inside information landing in your inbox), you say so immediately to your line manager and/or compliance rather than letting it go out on a deadline; nothing client-facing goes out on a single person's say-so (the "four-eyes" principle: at least one preparer and one independent reviewer/approver); and firms are expected to foster a culture where raising a concern is safe and normal, not something that marks you out — this is the practical, day-to-day expression of Conduct Rule 3 (open and cooperative with regulators) and the SM&CR's wider push for individual accountability. If in doubt, the correct instinct in every one of the "collision" scenarios below is: do not guess, do not invent a number, do not answer live from memory, escalate and let the qualified person (compliance, performance team, portfolio manager) give the actual answer.

### J. The collisions this seat actually produces

Drawing directly from the rules above, the realistic pressure points for an Investment Directing intern are:

1. A salesperson wants a return figure in a pitchbook that the performance team has not signed off. The correct answer is grounded in COBS 4.6 (performance must be accurate, from a checked source, with the right reference period) and the four-eyes principle: it cannot go out until performance has verified it, however tight the deadline.
2. A client asks for a view on a stock the fund is currently trading. This is the classic wall-crossing/selective-disclosure risk under UK MAR: even a throwaway comment about current activity can amount to disclosing non-public trading intentions, so the safe answer only ever references what is already public.
3. A journalist or client asks about a fund's underperformance. COBS 4.2's fair, clear, not misleading standard and the Consumer Duty's "avoid foreseeable harm" cross-cutting rule both apply: you cannot spin bad performance, but you also should not editorialise beyond agreed lines; the honest answer sticks to the facts already published and routes anything more to the fund manager or compliance.
4. A portfolio manager is too busy to review commentary before a deadline. This is a genuine tension between commercial pressure and the requirement (in practice, not always a hard written rule) that manager-attributed commentary reflects the manager's actual, current view; the right instinct is to escalate the deadline risk rather than publish unreviewed commentary under the manager's name.
5. A factsheet error is discovered after distribution. Once distributed, a factsheet is a financial promotion that has already gone to clients; the honest, Consumer-Duty-consistent response is to correct it and notify affected recipients promptly, not to quietly fix it going forward, and to log what happened (the kind of record COBS 4.11 requires you to be able to produce).
6. Being asked to present a fund's track record from a flattering start date. This directly breaches COBS 4.6.2R's requirement for complete 12-month periods covering the fund's full history (up to five years) — you cannot choose a start date because it makes the chart look better, and doing so is also the exact "cherry-picking" behaviour GIPS composite discipline exists to prevent.
7. Being cc'd on something that looks like inside information. This is a live UK MAR risk: the correct response is not to trade, not to mention it to any client in any form, and to flag it to compliance/control room immediately so a proper wall-crossing or information-barrier decision can be made, rather than deciding for yourself that it is "probably fine."

## 6. INTERVIEW ANGLES

- If asked "what stops an asset manager just showing its best fund's track record," the candidate can explain GIPS composites and COBS 4.6.2R's requirement for complete 12-month periods over the fund's real history, and can name the Woodford case as the cautionary tale about what happens when the story told to investors (easy 4-day redemption) does not match the underlying reality (illiquid holdings), which the FCA's 11 April 2024 Final Notice and the 5 August 2025 fines both confirmed in detail.
- If asked about ESG/sustainability marketing, the candidate can show they know the exact machinery: the anti-greenwashing rule (31 May 2024), the four SDR labels, and the 2 December 2024 naming and marketing rule, and can make the sharp point that "sustainable" in a fund name or pitch line is now a regulated word, not a marketing adjective.
- If asked how they would handle a request for a number that has not been signed off, the candidate can answer with the actual mechanism: COBS 4.6 governs what the number must look like, COBS 4.11 means it has to be recorded, and the honest answer is "it needs performance-team sign-off before it goes in the deck," tying a soft-skills answer to a hard rule rather than sounding like generic advice.
- If asked about a live-market question from a client (a stock the fund is trading), the candidate can reference UK MAR and Chinese walls/information barriers (SYSC 10.2) to explain exactly why an Investment Directing desk answers only with what is already public, and why "we've been adding to that name" is a genuinely risky sentence to say out loud to a client.
- The candidate can use the Woodford and Odey cases together to show they understand that FCA enforcement in asset management covers two different failure modes: Woodford is about product/liquidity/marketing mismatch (Principle 2 and 6, COBS-adjacent), while Odey is about personal integrity and cooperating with the regulator (COCON Rule 1 and Rule 3) — showing they grasp that "conduct" covers both what you say to clients and how you behave as an individual inside the firm.
- On escalation culture, the candidate can describe the four-eyes principle and COCON's individual conduct rules (especially Rule 1, integrity, and Rule 3, being open and cooperative with regulators) as the reason why, in this seat, "I would flag it and let compliance or the manager decide" is always a better answer than "I'd just fix it quietly" or "I'd use my judgement and send it anyway."
- On fees, the candidate can go beyond "fees should be low" and instead describe the actual Assessment of Value mechanism (COLL 6.6.20R, the seven considerations, and the FCA's own finding that firms often assess value "in the abstract"), showing they understand fee conversations in this industry are a compliance-governed disclosure, not just a sales pitch point.
