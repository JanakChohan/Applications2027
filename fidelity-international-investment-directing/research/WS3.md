# WORKSTREAM 3: COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE FOR FIDELITY INTERNATIONAL

Source ID range used: S120 to S149. Today's date: 2026-09-16. All figures below relate to

# 1. FINDINGS

## 1.1 Overview: FIL against nine peers

Fidelity International is a mid-sized, privately owned global asset manager. It sits well behind
the industry's giants on assets under management (AUM), roughly level with or a little ahead of
some UK-listed rivals depending on which FIL figure is used, and it is dwarfed by its own US
sister company, Fidelity Investments (FMR LLC), which is a separate business.

The clearest, most recent AUM figures found for each firm are (fuller detail and dates in the
Key Numbers table):

- BlackRock: roughly $14-15 trillion AUM, year-end 2025/2026 [Reported] [S135][S138]
- Fidelity Investments (US, NOT the target firm): $7.1 trillion AUM, 2025 [Confirmed] [S137][S139]
- JPMorgan Asset & Wealth Management: roughly $4.3-4.6 trillion, 2025 [Reported] [S128]
- Amundi: €2.38 trillion, 31 December 2025 [Confirmed] [S125][S134]
- Invesco: $2.17 trillion, 31 December 2025 [Confirmed] [S124][S133]
- Schroders: £823.7 billion (full-year figure) or £776.6 billion as of 30 June 2025 (half-year
  figure) [Reported] [S129][S141]
- Fidelity International: two different figures surfaced, not reconciled (see 1.2 below):
  US$1.086 trillion "total client assets" as of 31 December 2025 [Reported] [S120]; or US$469
  billion AUM (excluding Fidelity Canada) plus US$203 billion AUA as of 30 June 2025 [Reported]
  [S140]
- abrdn / Aberdeen Group: £390.4 billion "Investments" AUM (2025, scope unclear) or £556 billion
  group AUMA (2024/25, scope unclear) [Reported] [S131]
- M&G: £375.9 billion AUMA, 31 December 2025 [Confirmed] [S127][S130]
- Janus Henderson: $493.2 billion AUM, 31 December 2025 [Confirmed] [S126][S132]
- Baillie Gifford: £197-203 billion AUM, late 2025/mid-2026 (partnership, does not disclose to the
  same standard as listed firms) [Reported] [S123]

Read plainly: FIL is not a top-three global manager. On a like-for-like "AUM excluding Canada"
basis (US$469bn) it looks similar in size to Janus Henderson or a little bigger than M&G. On the
"total client assets" basis (US$1.09trn) it looks bigger than Schroders. Both cannot be compared
head to head with confidence because the underlying definitions differ and neither figure comes
from a document this session could read in full (see Gaps). This is an honest, sourced answer, not
a single tidy number, and the candidate should be comfortable saying so rather than quoting one
figure as if it were undisputed.

## 1.2 Founding, ownership, and what that does to time horizon

Fidelity International was set up in 1969 as the international arm of the Boston-based Fidelity
organisation, opening first in Tokyo, and was spun off as an independent business in 1980
[Confirmed] [S120]. It is privately owned: the majority of the company is owned by its employees,
with the Johnson family (the same family that controls the separate US Fidelity Investments)
holding a minority stake reported at around 39.89% [Reported] [S120][S148]. It is headquartered at
Pembroke, Bermuda [Confirmed, consistent with the brief's own firm-identity note] [S120]. It does
not report to public shareholders and does not hold quarterly earnings calls.

The nine peers split three ways:

- Fully listed, no controlling family: Schroders (LSE, FTSE 100, though the Schröder family still
  holds about 44% of ordinary shares and so retains real control) [Reported] [S129]; M&G (LSE,
  FTSE 100, demerged from Prudential plc in October 2019) [Reported] [S130]; Aberdeen Group,
  formerly Standard Life Aberdeen then abrdn, renamed again in March 2025 (LSE, FTSE 100)
  [Reported] [S131]; Janus Henderson (NYSE, incorporated in Jersey) [Reported] [S132]; Invesco
  (NYSE, organised in Bermuda but headquartered in Atlanta) [Reported] [S133]; BlackRock (NYSE)
  [Confirmed] [S135].
- Listed but majority-owned by a parent: Amundi is listed on Euronext Paris but Crédit Agricole
  holds 68.35% of it (2025 figure) [Reported] [S134]. JPMorgan Asset Management is a division of
  the publicly listed JPMorgan Chase & Co (NYSE: JPM) [Confirmed] [S128][S136].
- Private, no public listing: Baillie Gifford is an unlimited-liability partnership owned entirely
  by its working partners, describing itself as "the UK's only large-scale independent,
  unlimited-liability investment partnership" [Confirmed, direct quote read] [S123]. Fidelity
  Investments (US) is privately owned by the Johnson family (roughly 40%) and current/former
  employees [Confirmed] [S137].

So FIL sits in the smallest group: privately owned, no public listing, no controlling industrial
parent. Only Baillie Gifford and its own US sister company share that structure among the ten
firms compared here. This has visibly mattered in 2025-2026: two of the listed peers in this set
have become takeover targets. Schroders agreed to be acquired by the US manager Nuveen in a deal
valuing it at about £9.9 billion (agreed February 2026) [Reported] [S129]. Janus Henderson agreed
to be acquired by Trian Partners (which already held a 20% stake as of October 2025) together with
General Catalyst for about $7.4 billion, or $49 a share, expected to complete by mid-2026
[Reported] [S132]. A private, family-and-employee-owned structure removes that specific risk for
FIL: it cannot be bought out by an activist or a rival in the way two of its listed UK/US-listed
peers have just been.

## 1.3 The investment specialist / Investment Directing function

The brief's own JD reconstruction already establishes that FIL calls this function "Investment
Directing" and that its job is to "commercialise Fidelity's investment capabilities" by
representing Equities, Fixed Income, Multi Asset, Systematic Investing and Real Estate to clients,
reporting on portfolios, producing thought leadership, working with product development, and
generating sales marketing material. That is given context, not a new finding, so it is not
re-marked with a source ID here.

What this workstream could add from outside sources was limited, and that limitation is worth
stating plainly. Fidelity International's own public careers page for Investment Management does
not use the phrase "Investment Directing" anywhere in its content; the closest named functions are
a "Product Management team" described as "a conduit between our investment professionals and the
clients they serve," handling reporting and business analysis, and an "Equity Product team" that
"work[s] with the fund managers to market funds" [Confirmed, page read directly] [S122]. This
suggests Investment Directing is either folded into or works closely with these product/reporting
functions, or is simply not the page's focus, but it could not be confirmed either way this
session.

For the nine peers, this workstream searched specifically for the equivalent function (commonly
called "Client Portfolio Manager" or "Investment Specialist" across the industry) at each firm and
could not verify, from a source read this session, the exact title used, where it sits in the
org chart, or its headcount at Schroders, M&G, abrdn, Janus Henderson, Invesco, Amundi, BlackRock,
JPMorgan Asset Management or Baillie Gifford. The one peer page fetched directly for this purpose,
J.P. Morgan Asset Management's own "about us" page, explicitly does not name any client portfolio
manager or investment specialist function; it only directs enquiries to "your J.P. Morgan Asset
Management representative" [Confirmed, page read directly] [S128]. This is flagged honestly in the
Gaps section below rather than filled with plausible-sounding but unverified titles.

## 1.4 Design axes: where FIL sits

Working through the specific axes the workstream was asked to place FIL on:

**Active versus passive.** FIL presents itself as a research-led active manager. Its Real Estate
business is described as taking "a stock-picking approach" to UK and Continental European
commercial property, and its Equities and Fixed Income businesses are framed around proprietary
research and portfolio manager judgement rather than index-tracking [Reported] [S122]. No evidence
was found this session of FIL running an ETF or index-fund range at meaningful scale. Contrast
this with Amundi, now "the second largest ETF provider in Europe behind BlackRock" following its
2021 purchase of Lyxor [Reported] [S134]; BlackRock, where the iShares ETF business made up about
26% of AUM as far back as 2017 (an old figure, but indicative of scale) [Reported] [S135]; and
Invesco, which held $630.2 billion in "ETFs & Index Strategies" plus a separate $407.2 billion in
its QQQ product alone as of December 2025 [Reported] [S124][S133]. FIL has none of this passive
scale, by design, not by accident.

**Public/listed ownership versus private ownership, and time horizon.** Covered in 1.2. FIL and
Baillie Gifford are structurally insulated from the takeover and activist pressure now playing out
at Schroders and Janus Henderson; the eight listed or bank-controlled peers are not.

**Distribution owned versus wholesale-only.** FIL runs its own UK retail platform ("Personal
Investing"), its own adviser platform ("Fidelity Adviser Solutions," described as "one of the UK's
leading investment services platforms") and its own workplace pensions business, all inside the
same group as the asset manager [Reported] [S144]. This means FIL sees end investors directly,
across execution-only retail, advised, and workplace channels, in the UK market, rather than only
selling funds through third-party platforms and distributors. This was not independently verified
for each of the nine peers this session; it is highlighted as a genuine point of difference for
FIL relative to a pure wholesale-only manager such as Baillie Gifford, which distributes mainly
through investment trusts and third-party platforms rather than an owned direct-to-consumer
platform [Reported, inferred from the absence of any owned-platform description on Baillie
Gifford's own history page] [S123].

**Global research footprint owned versus bought.** FIL describes "over 360 investment
professionals and research staff around the world," an equities business "led by almost 300
investment professionals, including portfolio managers and analysts," in-house research or
proprietary insight covering "90% of their fund holdings," and portfolio managers and analysts
attending "more than 17,000 company meetings" a year [Reported, search-derived from FIL's own
institutional/careers materials, not independently fetched and verified word-for-word this
session] [S122][S145]. This is a genuinely large in-house research operation for a firm of this
size, and it is one of the load-bearing facts behind the Investment Directing team's job of
"promoting thought leadership."

**Asia exposure.** FIL's Asia-Pacific history starts in 1969 with the Tokyo office, predating even
the 1980 spin-off from Boston, followed by Hong Kong (1981), Taipei (1986), India (2001) and China
(2004) [Confirmed] [S120]. Until Japan's 1996 "Big Bang" deregulation, Fidelity's Tokyo operation
was legally restricted to producing research on Japanese stocks for clients overseas, and could
not sell funds or compete for pension money locally; that changed only after the 1996 reform
[Reported] [S149]. This is a real, unusually long history for a UK-headquartered manager. It
should not be overstated: Amundi also has a large and growing Asia book, reporting €462 billion of
Asia AUM as of Q1 2025, up 9% year on year [Confirmed, page read directly] [S125]. FIL's Asia
history is a genuine differentiator against most UK-listed peers, not an unmatched global claim.

**Breadth of product versus specialist boutique.** FIL is broad relative to Baillie Gifford (a
concentrated, growth-equity-led partnership now also expanding into private markets, per its own
history page) [S123], but no broader in scope than Invesco, Amundi, BlackRock or JPMorgan Asset
Management, all of which run full multi-asset-class platforms at much larger scale.

**Fee model: flat versus variable/fulcrum.** This is FIL's most concrete, well-documented pricing
innovation, and is covered in full in section 1.5 below.

**Scale of private assets.** Several peers have visibly built out private-markets arms: Schroders
launched "Schroders Capital" in 2021 to consolidate its private assets business [Reported] [S129];
Invesco reports $130.7 billion in "Private Markets" as of December 2025 [Reported] [S133]; M&G
runs Infracapital (infrastructure) and M&G Real Estate alongside its listed fund range [Reported]
[S130]. FIL has a Real Estate line of business (part of the five groups the Investment Directing
team represents, per the JD) but no AUM figure for it could be found this session; this is a gap,
not a finding of absence.

**Vertical integration into workplace pensions and platforms.** Covered above under distribution.
FIL runs Fidelity Workplace Pensions as part of the same group, which is a genuine form of vertical
integration a pure fund manager without an owned platform does not have.

## 1.5 The fulcrum fee: FIL's clearest, best-sourced piece of innovation

In July 2018, Fidelity International launched a "fulcrum fee" or Variable Management Fee (VMF)
share class across five UK-domiciled Oeics: Fidelity American, Fidelity Asian Dividend, Fidelity
European, Fidelity Global Special Situations, and Fidelity Special Situations. FIL described this
as a response to the UK Financial Conduct Authority's 2016 asset management market study, which
had found "little innovation" in fee structures and "significant clustering" around an arbitrary
0.75% charge. Under the VMF structure, the base annual management charge was 0.65% (0.10
percentage points below the standard share class), and the actual fee then moved up or down by up
to 0.20 percentage points depending on performance measured on a rolling three-year basis, meaning
a maximum charge of 0.85% if the fund outperformed its benchmark net of fees, or a minimum of 0.45%
if it underperformed [Confirmed, full article read] [S121].

By May 2021, FIL announced it was scrapping the VMF share class in those five open-ended funds,
moving investors into the ordinary lowest-cost flat-fee "W" share class instead, "following muted
demand from retail investors." A Fidelity International spokesperson was quoted directly: "We've
seen limited client demand in our open-ended funds, and as a result we decided the UK-domiciled
VMF share classes were too small to manage cost-effectively. We continue, however, to see some
clients in the institutional space who are open to variable fees linked to performance outcomes.
Adoption of our model was always a client choice and we are pleased to see that it opened the
doors to wider discussions on pricing structures." [Confirmed, direct quote read] [S121]

FIL kept the fulcrum structure for three closed-ended investment trusts: Fidelity Japan Trust
(formerly Fidelity Japanese Values), Fidelity Asian Values, and Fidelity China Special Situations,
which together held £2.9 billion of AUM at the time, because investment trusts have a single share
class and an independent board that can negotiate one fee structure for all shareholders, which
made the model easier to sustain there than in open-ended retail funds [Confirmed, same article]
[S121]. AJ Bell's head of active portfolios, Ryan Hughes, was quoted saying "the challenge with
fulcrum fees is always that they need explaining," and noted AJ Bell had since sold its holding in
the Fidelity Asian Values trust after "underperformance" linked to its "value style" led its fee to
be adjusted downwards [Confirmed, same article] [S121].

This episode is a genuinely rich, fully sourced example for an Investment Directing candidate: it
shows FIL trying a real pricing innovation, explaining why it worked for one distribution channel
(investment trusts, single board, single share class) and not another (open-ended retail funds,
many small holders, no single negotiating body), and it shows a named, quoted example of one FIL
vehicle underperforming.

   "total client assets" (31 Dec 2025, Wikipedia-sourced) versus US$469 billion AUM plus US$203
   billion AUA (30 Jun 2025, FIL's own Pillar 3 disclosure, but only seen via a search-engine
   summary, not the document itself, because fidelityinternational.com blocked automated

   This session could not fetch FIL's own Annual Report and Accounts PDF
   (https://www.fidelityinternational.com/legal/documents/FF/en/ar.ff.en.xx.pdf) at all; every
   attempt returned an Akamai "Access Denied" error page. This is the single biggest gap in the
   pack and should be resolved by whoever can access the annual report directly (e.g. through a
   browser, not an automated fetch) before using either FIL AUM figure with confidence.

2. FIL's current headcount is not established. The only figure found (7,600+) is dated 2018 and is
   almost certainly stale after the 2024 job cuts and subsequent hiring. No 2025/2026 FIL-specific
   headcount was found.

3. FIL's revenue and operating profit are not disclosed anywhere this session could find or read.
   As a private company, FIL does not appear to publish these in an easily searchable, fetchable
   form the way listed peers do in RNS filings and press releases. This is a real, structural gap,
   not an oversight: it is itself evidence for the "private ownership" point in section 1.2 above.

4. FIL's active-versus-passive AUM split, and any FIL-specific ETF or index-fund AUM figure, could

# 6. INTERVIEW ANGLES

- If asked "who are Fidelity International's competitors," the candidate can give a structured
  answer: giants that dwarf FIL on scale (BlackRock, its own US sister Fidelity Investments,
  JPMorgan Asset Management, Amundi, Invesco); UK-listed peers of a broadly similar or smaller
  scale that are currently going through ownership change (Schroders being bought by Nuveen, Janus
  Henderson being bought by Trian Partners/General Catalyst); and the two firms structurally most
  like FIL, private and free of public shareholders, Baillie Gifford and FIL's own US sister
  company.

- If asked "what makes Fidelity International different," the candidate has a genuinely sourced,
  non-generic answer: it is privately owned with no quarterly earnings calls, unlike almost every
  peer on this list; it has an unusually early and deep Asia history dating to a 1969 Tokyo office
  that predates its own 1980 independence; it owns the whole UK retail-to-institutional chain
  (Personal Investing, Adviser Solutions, Workplace Pensions) rather than selling only through
  third parties; and it is one of the few managers in the industry to have actually launched and
  then partly withdrawn a genuine performance-linked "fulcrum fee," which it can discuss with real
  nuance (worked for investment trusts with one board and one share class, did not work for
  open-ended retail funds with many small holders).

- The candidate should be ready to explain, unprompted if needed, that "Fidelity" in the news
  might mean either company, and should default to clarifying which one a headline or number
  refers to. This avoids the single most embarrassing mistake available in this interview: quoting
  Fidelity Investments' $7.1 trillion AUM, or its 80,000 staff, or its 2026 job cuts, as if they
  belonged to Fidelity International.

- On job cuts and outflows, the candidate should not be defensive: FIL cut roughly 1,000 jobs in
  2024 (about 9% of its workforce) to save around $125 million a year, and it saw meaningful net
  outflows in at least one recent year (€9.2 billion to end-October 2023). A credible answer to "is
  Fidelity International doing well" is one that acknowledges this honestly and then points to the
  more recent, better story where evidence exists (record AUM claims on the "total client assets"
  measure, the continued Asia franchise, the ongoing UK platform business) rather than pretending
  the firm has never had a difficult year.

- On the "investment specialist" function itself, the candidate should be candid in an interview
  that they researched this function specifically and could not find much FIL has put in the
  public domain about it by name; this is a legitimate, good question to ask an interviewer
  directly (for example, "how does the Investment Directing team's reporting line and remit differ
  from the Product Management and Equity Product teams I saw mentioned on the careers site?"),
  which turns a research gap into a genuine, well-informed question rather than a weakness to hide.

- On fee competition, the candidate can note that FIL sits in a crowded, high-scale field where
  BlackRock, Amundi and Invesco have built enormous, low-cost passive/ETF businesses that FIL has
  chosen not to compete in; FIL's answer to fee pressure has instead been an active-management fee
  experiment (the fulcrum fee) rather than a passive price war, which is a coherent, explainable
  strategic choice for an Investment Directing intern to be able to articulate rather than simply
  asserting FIL is "cheap" or "the best."
