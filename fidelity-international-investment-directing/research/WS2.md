# WORKSTREAM 2 — FIDELITY INTERNATIONAL AS AN OPERATING MODEL, FROM THE INSIDE

# 1. FINDINGS

## A. Founding insight, ownership, and who actually owns the firm

Fidelity International (legal entity FIL Limited) traces its roots to Fidelity Management & Research, founded by Edward C. Johnson II in Boston in 1946 [S70][Reported]. Fidelity International itself was established in 1969 as the international arm of the Boston business, opening in Tokyo the same year, then London (1973) and Hong Kong (1981) [S70][Reported]. In 1980, under Edward "Ned" Johnson III, the international business was formally separated from the US parent and became independently owned [S70][Confirmed — matches the brief's own firm-identity note and multiple corroborating sources]. This is the single most important fact for interview purposes: since 1980, Fidelity International (FIL) and Fidelity Investments/FMR LLC (Boston) have been two separate companies under common historical branding and a shared founding family, not one company. FIL Limited, the ultimate holding company, is incorporated in Bermuda with its registered address at Pembroke Hall, 42 Crow Lane, P.O. Box HM 670, Hamilton HM19, Bermuda [S78][Confirmed — this address is confirmed directly in the audited related-party note of FIL Holdings (UK) Limited's 2024 group accounts, filed at UK Companies House].

Barry Bateman is the historically important non-Johnson figure: he joined Fidelity International in 1981 to run UK sales and marketing, became President of Fidelity International in 1991 (responsible for everything outside the Americas), and later served as vice-chairman [S82][Reported]. His son, James Bateman, was CIO for multi-asset at Fidelity International before the two of them left to found a new London boutique, New Value Investment [S82][Reported].

Anne Richards joined as CEO in December 2018, arriving from M&G Investments (where she had been CEO and a main-board director of parent Prudential plc); her earlier career included JPMorgan, Merrill Lynch Investment Managers and Aberdeen Asset Management [S80][Reported]. She was made a Dame (DBE) in 2021 [S80][Reported]. According to career-tracking sources, she moved from CEO to Vice Chair of Fidelity International in March 2024 and to Senior Adviser in January 2026 [S81][Reported — candidate should treat the exact hand-off date and her current title as needing a final live check before interview, since this is a fast-moving personnel change]. The FIL Holdings (UK) Limited 2024 accounts independently confirm a related change at UK level: Cara Hewitt was appointed CEO of the UK entity on 19 July 2024, succeeding Tony Lanser, who resigned the same day; Peter Horrell (formerly Head of the Global Workplace and Personal Business for Fidelity International) chairs that UK board [S78][Confirmed, primary source].

On ownership: FIL Limited is privately held. A partnership controlled by Edward C. Johnson III and his family has been reported to hold voting rights over approximately 39.89% of FIL voting stock, with the balance held by fund managers and senior employees, according to a company spokesperson quoted in secondary reporting [S79][Reported — single source, snippet-level, not independently verified against a primary ownership register; treat the exact split with caution and do not quote it as precise without re-checking a primary filing]. The brief's suggestion of a "roughly 40-45% employee/management" split could not be confirmed from a primary source in the time available; the only concrete figure found is the ~39.89% Johnson family voting stake, which is a different cut of the same ownership pie and not the employee share itself. This is flagged clearly in the Gaps section — do not repeat the 40-45% figure as fact in an interview.

Fidelity International's own stated purpose, quoted directly from its FIL Holdings (UK) Limited 2024 statutory accounts, is: "working together to build better financial futures" [S78][Confirmed, primary source, verbatim]. The company's own "About us" page (archived copy, data as at 30 June 2024) states: "As a private company we think generationally and invest for the long term" and lists four value statements: "Clients are at the heart of our business: Investing clients' money is why we exist," "We act with integrity," and a stated belief that "investing is the key to wealth creation and securing a better financial future" [S76][Confirmed, primary source, verbatim — see Section 3 for full quotes].

## B. How it makes money — the flow from client assets to profit

The clearest, most rigorous numbers available are not for the global FIL Limited group (which, being a Bermuda private company, does not file public group accounts) but for its UK sub-group, FIL Holdings (UK) Limited ("FHL"), whose consolidated accounts are filed at UK Companies House and are audited by PricewaterhouseCoopers LLP [S78][Confirmed, primary source]. For the year ended 31 December 2024:
- Assets under Management (AuM): £189.5bn, up from £178.7bn in 2023 (+6%) [S78][Confirmed]
- Assets under Administration (AuA): £104.4bn, up from £95.6bn in 2023 (+9%) [S78][Confirmed]
- Total customer assets (AuM + AuA combined metric as reported by FHL): £293.9bn (2024) vs £274.3bn (2023) [S78][Confirmed]
- Group turnover: £1,228.093m (2024) vs £1,225.302m (2023) — essentially flat [S78][Confirmed]
- Turnover breakdown: investment management fees £666.772m; service and platform fees £213.701m; other distribution revenue £58.033m; other income £289.587m [S78][Confirmed]
- Group operating profit: £66.848m (2024) vs £103.100m (2023) — a fall of roughly 35% year-on-year, despite turnover being flat, because administrative expenses rose (the strategic report cites continued heavy investment in the UK platform's "multi-year investment programme") [S78][Confirmed]
- Profit before tax: £106.886m (2024) vs £133.799m (2023); profit after tax: £76.208m (2024) vs £99.069m (2023) [S78][Confirmed]
- Total employee costs: £491.7m (2024) vs £474.5m (2023) — up even as headcount fell, implying higher cost per head (mix shift toward client-facing/tech roles, and normal wage inflation) [S78][Confirmed]
- Average headcount: 2,941 (2024) vs 3,370 (2023) — a fall of about 13%, consistent with the "headcount reduction programme" the accounts explicitly reference [S78][Confirmed]
- Net assets: £970.486m (2024) vs £896.362m (2023) [S78][Confirmed]
- No dividend was paid by FHL to its parent in either 2023 or 2024 (accounts state "no interim dividend was paid... and no final dividend is proposed") [S78][Confirmed] — profit is being retained within the UK sub-group, not upstreamed, at least at this level in this period.

This is AUM AND AUA both, and the brief is right that they matter differently: AuM is where FIL is the investment manager and earns a management fee on the asset value; AuA is money sitting on FIL's Personal Investing / Adviser Solutions platforms in funds FIL does not necessarily manage itself (third-party funds, shares, cash), where FIL earns platform/service fees rather than a full management fee. The platform (AuA) side grew faster (+9%) than the asset-management (AuM) side (+6%) in 2024, which matches the strategic narrative of the platform business being the near-term growth engine [S78][Confirmed].

One UK operating subsidiary's own accounts give a second, narrower data point: FIL Investment Services (UK) Limited ("FISL"), the entity that manages Fidelity's UK-domiciled OEICs and AIFs, reported turnover of £313.340m (2024) vs £315.282m (2023), almost entirely investment management fees (£312.998m of the £313.340m); operating profit of £85.404m (2024) vs £88.542m (2023); profit after tax of £69.843m (2024) vs £72.193m (2023); and paid an interim dividend of £70.5m up to its parent in 2024 (vs £60m in 2023) [S77][Confirmed, primary source]. FISL has no employees of its own — staff are employed by another group company, FIL Investment Management Limited (FIML), and costs are allocated across the group [S77][Confirmed].

Globally, Fidelity International's own website states total client assets of USD $862.0 billion as at 30 June 2024, from over 2.84 million clients across Asia Pacific, Europe, the Middle East, South America and Canada [S76][Confirmed, primary source, own website]. A separate Fidelity-owned page (the UK investment trusts site) states total client assets of £682.1bn (undated on the page as fetched, but referencing the same 2.84 million client base) [S75][Reported — likely a different reporting date/currency snapshot; the $862bn and £682.1bn figures are NOT the same measurement date and should not be quoted interchangeably]. A further search-derived figure of £705bn also appeared in secondary sources [S75][Reported, lower confidence]. Candidates should be aware these different headline "client assets" numbers come from different dates/currencies and from a mix of primary and secondary sourcing — quote the FHL Group's audited UK figures (£189.5bn AuM / £104.4bn AuA, FY2024) with confidence, and describe the global $862bn-ish figure as an approximate, dated snapshot rather than a precise current number.

Fee pressure and the "fulcrum fee": In July 2018, Fidelity International introduced a variable-fee share class ("W-Variable Management Fee", branded the "fulcrum fee") on five UK-domiciled OEICs (Fidelity American, Asian Dividend, European, Global Special Situations and Special Situations funds), explicitly in response to the FCA's 2016 Asset Management Market Study, which had found "little innovation" in fee structures and clustering around a flat 0.75% [S72][Confirmed — verbatim reporting, primary quotes captured]. The mechanism: the base annual management charge was cut to 0.65% (from a typical ~0.75%), and then moved symmetrically up or down by up to 0.20 percentage points depending on rolling three-year performance versus benchmark — so the fee could rise to a maximum of 0.85% if the fund beat its benchmark, or fall to a minimum of 0.45% if it lagged [S72][Confirmed]. On 25 May 2021, Fidelity International announced it was scrapping the W-VMF share class on the open-ended funds, citing "limited client demand," and moved those assets into the funds' standard flat-fee "W" share classes; three investment trusts (Fidelity Japan Trust, Fidelity Asian Values, Fidelity China Special Situations), holding about £2.9bn combined, kept the fulcrum-fee structure, because it had "proved popular with investment trusts that only have one share class and an independent board to negotiate a single fee structure" [S72][Confirmed, verbatim]. A Fidelity International spokesperson said: "We've seen limited client demand in our open-ended funds, and as a result we decided the UK-domiciled VMF share classes were too small to manage cost-effectively... Adoption of our model was always a client choice and we are pleased to see that it opened the doors to wider discussions on pricing structures." [S72][Confirmed, verbatim quote, dated 11 May/25 May 2021 announcement]. AJ Bell's head of active portfolios, Ryan Hughes, was quoted the same day noting the structural problem with fulcrum fees generally: "The challenge with fulcrum fees is always that they need explaining... There is also a natural difficulty in explaining to customers that if the fund does well, they will pay higher fees" [S72][Confirmed, verbatim]. This is a genuinely good interview talking point: Fidelity tried a distinctive, thoughtful pricing innovation in 2018, in direct response to a named regulatory study, and then unwound most of it three years later when retail demand didn't materialise — while it survived where governance structure (investment trusts) made it easier to explain and administer.

## C. The business lines

Two headline UK business lines, per FHL's own 2024 governance statement: "Investment Solutions & Services" (ISS) and "Global Platform Solutions" (GPS), supported by "Corporate Enablers" (Finance, HR, Technology) [S78][Confirmed, primary source]. ISS is the institutional/wholesale/professional-investor-facing manufacturing business: it "offers Fidelity's investment expertise to wholesale, institutional and other professional investing clients," across equities, fixed income, real estate (including derivatives) and multi-asset, plus third-party fund products, and provides segregated mandates [S78][Confirmed]. GPS is the platform side — "Fidelity Advisor Solutions" and "Fidelity Personal Investing," the UK's direct-to-consumer investment platform (ISAs, SIPPs, GIAs, Junior ISAs), plus workplace pension administration [S75][Reported]/[S78][Confirmed for the GPS description].

The brief's own JD language maps onto ISS specifically: "Equities, Fixed Income, Multi Asset, Systematic Investing and Real Estate groups" are the investment-management franchises the Investment Directing team represents to clients — these sit inside ISS.

Growth signal, straight from the 2024 accounts: platform/GPS grew AuA faster (+9%) than ISS-side AuM (+6%) in 2024, and the strategic report explicitly frames continued heavy platform investment as "expected to be a significant driver for long term future growth" and states the Group's "ambition and commitment to the UK retail platform market" [S78][Confirmed]. At the same time, ISS made an explicit strategic retrenchment in 2024: it decided to "cease manufacturing of non-core private asset classes in order to pivot resources to areas of unique strength... active ETFs and the distribution of third-party capabilities for both private assets and select public market investments," alongside "key management changes," including "the appointment of a new Equity CIO and the creation of the Asset Management Delivery function" [S78][Confirmed, primary source, verbatim]. So: platform is growing and being invested in; parts of the in-house private-markets manufacturing effort are being cut back in favour of distributing other people's private-asset products and building out active ETFs.

Asia weighting: Fidelity International was built out of Asia early — Tokyo (1969), Hong Kong (1981), India (2001), China (2004) [S70][Reported] — which is unusual among UK-headquartered or UK-associated asset managers (most build a UK/European book first and treat Asia as an add-on). This history is why Fidelity International still runs standalone, long-established single-country/region investment trusts such as Fidelity Japan Trust, Fidelity China Special Situations and Fidelity Asian Values [S72][Confirmed — these three trusts are named directly in the fulcrum-fee reporting above] — vehicles a UK-only competitor typically would not have the depth of on-the-ground research to run credibly.

## D. Risk and control architecture

FIL's UK operations run through several separately FCA-authorised legal entities, not one blanket licence: FIL Investment Services (UK) Limited (FISL, FRN 121939), FIL Investments International (FII, FRN 122170), Financial Administration Services Limited (FASL, FRN 122169), and FIL Pensions Management (FPM, FRN 144345) [S90][Reported — FRNs from a secondary aggregation of FCA register entries; not independently re-verified against the live FCA register in this pass, since the FCA register itself is a JavaScript application that does not expose data to a simple fetch]. FIL Life Insurance Ltd is separately authorised by the Prudential Regulation Authority as well as the FCA [S74][Confirmed, primary source — retirement.fidelity.co.uk regulatory disclosures page]. A newer entity, FIL Platform Solutions (UK) Limited (FPSL, Companies House number 15018212), is also FCA-authorised [S74][Confirmed, primary source].

Under the UK's Investment Firms Prudential Regime (IFPR), four investment-firm subsidiaries within the Group — FPM, FASL, FII and FIL Investment Advisors (UK) Limited (FIA) — are in scope for MIFIDPRU 8 disclosure, and the Group publishes its MIFIDPRU (Pillar 3 style) disclosures on a consolidated basis for the parent company (FHL) on the Fidelity International website [S78][Confirmed, primary source]. The 2024 FHL accounts describe the Group's risk taxonomy explicitly as four categories — non-financial (operational) risk (described as "the largest risk to which the Group is exposed"), strategic risk, financial risk (liquidity/market/credit), and investment risk (borne by fund investors, monitored by the manager to keep it within contractual limits) [S78][Confirmed, verbatim risk language].

Fund governance: as UK Authorised Fund Manager / Authorised Corporate Director for its OEICs, Fidelity International is required under FCA rules to publish an annual public Assessment of Value ("Value Assessment") for each fund it manages, assessing quality of service, comparable market rates, comparable services, economies of scale, net performance, classes of unit and costs; these are published on fidelity.co.uk / fidelityinternational.com and are genuinely useful primary reading for an interview — they are the manager marking its own funds' homework in public [S87][Reported — a specific report ("Value Assessment Report", FINST) was located at a fidelityinternational.com URL but not read page-by-page in this pass; treat the existence and mechanism as confirmed, but do not quote specific fund-level scores without reading the actual document first]. The company's own 2024 accounts confirm this obligation directly: "The FCA requires all Fund Managers in the UK to assess whether their funds represent value for money and report the results to their investors in a publicly available Value Assessment" [S78][Confirmed, verbatim].

Governance structure at UK holding-company level: FHL's board is chaired by a non-executive (Peter Horrell), separate from the CEO (Cara Hewitt) — an explicit separation-of-roles governance choice the accounts call out as aligned with "generally accepted corporate governance best practice" [S78][Confirmed]. The board applies the Wates Corporate Governance Principles for Large Private Companies (the standard UK governance code for large unlisted companies) and delegates to three standing committees: the Audit and Risk Committee (ARC), a Nomination Committee, and a "CASS Oversight Forum" — CASS being the FCA's Client Assets sourcebook, so this is a dedicated forum specifically to oversee protection of client money and custody assets, which is one of the most heavily scrutinised areas by the FCA for any platform/asset-management business [S78][Confirmed, verbatim].

One concrete control failure disclosed in the numbers: as at 31 December 2024, the Group carried a £2.503m provision "to cover potential liabilities arising from processing errors identified in its defined contribution pension business," down from £3.021m at the start of the year, with £613,000 paid out to affected clients during 2024 and roughly half the remaining provision expected to be paid out during 2025 [S78][Confirmed, verbatim]. This is a genuine, disclosed operational-risk event — a remediation programme for DC pension processing errors — not a hypothetical one, and it is a good example to cite if asked about operational risk in the interview.

## E. Strategy and what has grown or been cut back

Cost and headcount: the FHL Group's own 2024 accounts confirm a formal "headcount reduction programme" during 2024, alongside average headcount falling from 3,370 (2023) to 2,941 (2024) [S78][Confirmed]. This lines up with wider press reporting that Fidelity International cut roughly 1,000 roles globally (about 9% of staff) in a restructuring announced around early March 2024, as part of a cost-reduction programme reported to target roughly $125m of savings [S84][Reported — this figure comes from press aggregation rather than a document read in full; treat the ~$125m savings figure as a reported estimate, not confirmed from a primary Fidelity source]. Be careful in interview not to confuse this with 2024-2026 restructurings at Fidelity Investments (Boston) — several very similar-sounding headlines about "Fidelity" cutting 700-1,000 jobs in 2024 and again in May 2026 in fact refer to the separate US business reorganising its technology and product delivery teams, not to Fidelity International [S84][Reported — flagged explicitly to avoid firm confusion, per the brief's own instruction].

ETFs: Fidelity International has been actively building out an active/sustainable UCITS ETF range aimed at the European market, including expansions of its sustainable active ETF range reported in both October 2023 and October 2024, adding active fixed income ETFs (e.g., sustainable EUR and USD high-yield bond, Paris-aligned, multifactor ETFs) to sit alongside its existing active equity ETF range [S85][Reported]. This sits inside the strategic pivot described in Section C — ETFs are one of the "areas of unique strength" ISS is now prioritising over in-house private-asset manufacturing [S78][Confirmed for the strategic framing; S85][Reported for the specific product launches, which were not individually read in primary form].

Private assets / LTAF: Fidelity International received FCA authorisation in August 2025 for its first Long-Term Asset Fund (LTAF) — a UK fund structure designed to let defined-contribution pension schemes hold illiquid private-market assets — and has integrated private assets into its £16.9bn "FutureWise" default investment strategy for UK workplace pension schemes [S89][Reported]. This sits in some tension with the 2024 accounts' statement that ISS was ceasing "manufacturing of non-core private asset classes" — the likely read is that Fidelity is retreating from manufacturing niche/non-core private-asset products directly while still building distribution access to private assets (via LTAFs and third-party partnerships) for its core workplace-pensions client base; a good, nuanced point to make if asked about strategy, while being honest that the full internal rationale was not confirmed from a single primary document [Inferred].

Systematic/quant: the brief's JD explicitly names "Systematic Investing" as one of the groups the Investment Directing team represents, confirming Fidelity International runs a distinct systematic/quantitative investment capability alongside traditional fundamental equities and fixed income (this is consistent with, though not separately verified beyond, the JD language itself) [Inferred from JD, S-none — see BRIEF.md].

Sustainable investing: Fidelity International's Streamlined Energy & Carbon Reporting (SECR) disclosure in the FY2024 accounts states firm-wide commitments to "halve the carbon footprint of our investment portfolios by 2030, from a 2020 baseline, and reach net zero by 2050" and to "achieve net zero emissions across Fidelity's own corporate operations by 2030" [S78][Confirmed, verbatim, primary source]. The Group's own reported UK operational carbon footprint fell from 1,776 tCO2e (2023, location-based) to 1,786 tCO2e (2024) — essentially flat on a location basis but down substantially on a market-adjusted basis (755 tCO2e to 647 tCO2e) reflecting renewable-electricity purchasing [S78][Confirmed].

Fidelity Adviser Solutions and the platform business: FHL's own accounts describe distribution "through Fidelity Advisor Solutions and Fidelity Personal Investing" and "brokerage and custody services" as core, growing parts of the Group's principal activity — this is the GPS side referenced in Section C, and is the part of the business the accounts single out for continued heavy multi-year technology investment [S78][Confirmed].

Diversity/people metrics disclosed: FIL Group's mean gender pay gap for 2024 was 15.9%, which the company states is "significantly lower than the latest available UK financial and insurance sector average of 29.8%" (citing Statista) [S78][Confirmed, verbatim, primary source]. Senior female representation rose from 34.4% to 35.2% between November 2023 and November 2024, against a stated target of 38% by December 2027 [S78][Confirmed].

No disposals of substantive operating businesses were identified in this research pass; the only disposal-type event found in the primary accounts was the liquidation of a long-dormant shell subsidiary (FIL (UK) Limited, dissolved 23 February 2024) and the disposal of a small (3.082%) equity stake in FIL (Luxembourg) S.A. — neither is a strategic divestment of a business line [S78][Confirmed].

## F. Private vs listed — what this means for researching Fidelity International

Because FIL Limited is a private Bermuda company, it does not publish the kind of investor-facing disclosure a listed peer must (no annual report and accounts filed with a stock exchange, no analyst calls, no share-price-driven quarterly reporting cadence, no public remuneration report tying pay to shareholder returns). This is the single biggest research handicap and the single most important thing for a candidate to say explicitly if asked "what don't you know" in interview. The workaround that worked well in this research: FIL's UK operating subsidiaries are UK companies and must file statutory accounts at Companies House like any UK company, and because several of them are individually large enough (turnover, balance sheet, employee count) they must be audited and cannot claim the small-companies exemption — which is how the FIL Holdings (UK) Limited and FIL Investment Services (UK) Limited numbers used throughout this document were obtained [S77][S78][Confirmed]. This route gives excellent detail on the UK business specifically, but nothing equivalent exists for FIL Limited's Bermuda-domiciled global group; global-level numbers (AuM, revenue, headcount) come only from what FIL chooses to publish itself (its own website, occasional press statements) or from press reporting of those disclosures, which is inherently less granular and less audited than a UK Companies House filing [S76][Confirmed for what is published; the underlying global figures are self-reported, not independently audited in public].

By contrast, a listed UK/European peer such as Schroders plc or abrdn plc must publish full annual reports with segmental AuM/AuA, net flows, fee margins in basis points, cost/income ratios, and remuneration disclosure, all filed with the FCA/London Stock Exchange and available on the company's investor relations site without needing to go through Companies House at all; Amundi (Euronext Paris-listed) is similarly transparent under French/EU listed-company disclosure rules. A candidate should be ready to say, credibly: "Fidelity International is private, so I had to build up its numbers from UK subsidiary filings at Companies House rather than from a single investor-facing annual report — which is itself a useful thing to understand about how the firm is structured and how much less external market pressure it faces on disclosure than a Schroders or an abrdn." This was not independently verified against Schroders/abrdn's own filings in this research pass, but the general disclosure-regime difference (listed vs private) is a well-established structural fact [Inferred].

## G. Leadership's stated view of the future, and what hiring implies

Two internal management moves in 2024, confirmed directly from the audited accounts, tell a consistent strategic story: the appointment of "a new Equity CIO" and "the creation of the Asset Management Delivery function to support accelerated operational and technological developments" [S78][Confirmed, verbatim, dated to FY2024]. Read together with the CEO succession (Cara Hewitt replacing Tony Lanser as UK CEO on 19 July 2024) and the pull-back from non-core private-asset manufacturing in favour of active ETFs and third-party distribution, the pattern that emerges is: consolidate/professionalise UK platform technology, keep headcount lean, invest selectively in ETFs and workplace-pensions private-markets access, and put fresh leadership in place on both the UK platform CEO seat and the equities CIO seat within the same year [S78][Confirmed for the facts; the "consolidate and reinvest selectively" characterisation is [Inferred] as a synthesis, not a directly quoted strategic statement].

On investment views specifically (as opposed to corporate strategy), Fidelity International's senior investors have been publicly framing AI as the dominant theme into 2026: Niamh Brodie-Machura (Chief Equity Investment Officer) has been quoted stressing diversified equity allocations and income/dividend-paying stocks as a way to participate in AI-driven earnings growth, and Matthew Quaife (Global Head of Multi-Asset Investments) has highlighted AI as a critical global growth driver, particularly in the US, and its influence on employment dynamics [S92][Reported — sourced via a low-provenance news aggregator rather than fetched directly from Fidelity's own "Outlook 2026" document; a copy of that document exists at fidelityinternational.com but was not read in full in this pass — treat the substance as directionally right but re-verify direct quotes before using them word-for-word in interview] [S93][Reported, document located but not read].

## I. The skeletons — told straight

No FCA enforcement action (fine or public censure) specifically against a Fidelity International UK entity was identified in this research pass. This is a genuine gap, not a clean bill of health that was actively confirmed — the FCA's own enforcement pages and the RegActions aggregator were searched but returned no clear Fidelity International-specific hits; a full check of the FCA's Financial Services Register enforcement history for each of FISL, FII, FASL and FPM individually would be needed to state this with full confidence [Gap — do not tell an interviewer "Fidelity has never been fined by the FCA" as a confirmed fact; say instead that no such action was found in the research and that the register would need checking directly].

What is confirmed and disclosed by the firm itself: the £2.503m redress provision for defined-contribution pension processing errors (Section D above), an ongoing remediation programme as at 31 December 2024 [S78][Confirmed, verbatim, primary source].

Headcount reduction rounds: a substantial, board-disclosed headcount reduction in 2024 (3,370 to 2,941 average headcount, roughly 13% down) [S78][Confirmed], consistent with wider press reports of ~1,000 roles (~9%) being cut globally in a March 2024 restructuring [S84][Reported]. The brief also asked about 2019-2021 job-cut rounds specifically; none could be confirmed from a primary or clearly-dated secondary source in this pass — this is a genuine gap, flagged honestly below rather than invented.

Executive churn: within a roughly 12-18 month window, Fidelity International saw the UK holding-company CEO change (Tony Lanser out, Cara Hewitt in, July 2024), a co-CIO for fixed income/multi-asset/private assets step down (Andrew McCaffery, reported May 2024), and a further fixed-income CIO transition (Steve Ellis departing for a career break, Marion Le Morhedec joining from AXA IM in September 2025) [S78][S73][S90][mixed Confirmed/Reported as cited above]. That is a genuinely high rate of senior investment-leadership turnover for an 18-month window, and is fair to note as a real feature of the current period rather than something to gloss over.

Fund/franchise performance: no specific, dated evidence of sustained underperformance in a named Fidelity International franchise was substantiated in this pass beyond one incidental data point — Fidelity Asian Values investment trust was described by a wealth platform (AJ Bell) as having seen "its value style and underperformance" lead to a downward fee adjustment under its fulcrum-fee mechanism, and AJ Bell subsequently sold its holding in the trust for asset-allocation reasons (not solely performance) [S72][Confirmed, verbatim, dated 2021]. - Show you understand the ownership structure is genuinely unusual and matters commercially: "Fidelity International has been privately owned since the 1980 split from the Boston business, majority-owned by the Johnson family and by fund managers and senior employees — which I think is part of why the firm still talks about thinking generationally rather than quarter-to-quarter, and part of why it's harder to research than a listed peer like Schroders."
