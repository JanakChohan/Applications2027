# WORKSTREAM 1 — DECODING THE JOB: INVESTMENT DIRECTOR / INVESTMENT SPECIALIST AT FIDELITY INTERNATIONAL

## 1. FINDINGS

### 1.1 What the job family actually is

An Investment Director at Fidelity International sits in the space between the people who manage money (portfolio managers, "PMs") and the people who sell it (Distribution/Sales). The industry's generic name for this seat is "Client Portfolio Manager" (CPM). Fidelity uses "Investment Director" as its own house title for the same function, and calls its junior rung "Associate Investment Director" [S1]. Other firms use different labels for close variants of the same job: Schroders and Janus Henderson use "Client Portfolio Manager" directly [S2][S3]; M&G uses "Investment Specialist" [S4]; BlackRock uses "Product Strategist" / "Product Specialist" [S5]; Baillie Gifford splits the job in two, pairing a "Client Manager" (owns the commercial relationship) with an "Investment Specialist" (owns the investment content) who work together rather than one person doing both [S6]. The role never appears inside the portfolio management team's own headcount and never appears inside pure sales headcount either — it is a distinct, named function everywhere it exists. That consistency across five to six competing firms is itself evidence that this is a stable, structural job family in asset management, not a Fidelity quirk. [Confirmed]

The core economic reason the job exists: a fund manager who is excellent at picking stocks or bonds is not necessarily good at explaining, in front of a room of pension trustees or on a compliance-approved slide, why the portfolio did what it did last quarter, or at withstanding two hours of adversarial due-diligence questions from an institutional consultant. Firms do not want their scarcest, highest-paid investment talent (portfolio managers) spending their day on that, nor do they want salespeople who lack the technical depth to answer a probing question about duration positioning or factor exposure. The Investment Director/CPM is the person built to do both: technically credible enough to sit in for or alongside the PM, and commercially fluent enough to read a room of clients or consultants. ### 1.2 The five JD "team responsibility" lines, decoded

The brief's reconstructed JD gives five team-level responsibility lines. Below is what each actually involves, decoded against real postings.

**"Representing the Equities, Fixed Income, Multi Asset, Systematic Investing and Real Estate groups to prospective and existing clients."**
This tells you Fidelity organises its investment engine into five distinct asset-class "groups," and Investment Directors are assigned to cover a specific one (an Associate Investment Director job actually seen live was scoped specifically to "Systematic Investing," with a Fixed Income technical emphasis) [S1]. "Representing" means: the Investment Director, not the PM, is usually the person in the room for a prospect meeting, an existing-client review, a conference panel, or a consultant call — carrying the house view on that asset class. Internal counterparties: the PMs and research analysts inside that group (the ID must reflect the PM's actual process and views, not invent one) [S1][S10]. External counterparties: institutional clients (pension schemes, insurers, sovereign wealth funds), wholesale fund selectors, platforms, private banks and wealth managers. "Good" here means technical accuracy that would survive the PM checking it, plus the ability to translate it for a non-specialist audience without dumbing it down to the point of being misleading (a live compliance concern, see section on control). Real Estate is notable as the odd one out: it is a physical, illiquid asset class (UK and Continental European commercial property, stock-picked with active lease renegotiation and development), which structurally requires different reporting and client-communication approaches than a daily-priced equity or bond fund [S10].

**"Reporting on portfolios."**
This is the factsheet/commentary/attribution production line: monthly and quarterly factsheets, portfolio review packs for existing clients, and performance attribution ("why did we beat or miss the benchmark, and by how much, and from what — stock selection, sector allocation, duration, currency"). Internal counterparties: a performance & attribution team supplying the numbers, portfolio managers supplying or approving the narrative, and Compliance signing off the final wording before it can leave the building. External audience: the client or consultant reading the pack. "Good" means the numbers must tie out exactly to the fund's official, GIPS-governed performance record [S7] — an Investment Director cannot round favourably or omit an inconvenient period, because the client (or a consultant such as Mercer, who is likely re-running the same numbers independently [S8]) will check.

**"Promoting thought leadership on topics."**
Whitepapers, "viewpoints," webinars, conference panels, and market outlook pieces. This is not really about that specific fund's last quarter; it is brand-building that keeps a house's investment view visible even when there is no live mandate on the table, so that when a client eventually does have a search, the firm is already "front of mind." Internal counterparties: strategists/economists, PR/marketing, digital content teams. External counterparties: trade press, conference organisers, consultants who track manager insight quality as a soft qualitative factor. This content still has to clear Compliance under the same "fair, clear and not misleading" bar as a formal pitch document (see control section) — a whitepaper is a financial promotion in FCA terms just as much as a factsheet is [S9].

**"Working with product development to create new products to meet investors' demands."**
This confirms that Fidelity has a distinct Product function separate from both the Investment Directing/Distribution side and the PM side — a genuine "manufacture vs. manage vs. sell" three-way split. A live Fidelity International vacancy titled "Product Owner - Equity" appearing in the same London job list as the Investment Director role is consistent with this [S10]. Investment Directors are the conduit for structured client demand signals — e.g., recurring RFP questions, consultant feedback, wholesale platform requests — flowing back into decisions about launching a new share class, a new strategy, or reshaping an existing one; a live FII posting explicitly lists "Content for Product launches, reshapes, DDQ, RFPs, perspectives" as an Investment Director deliverable [S1].

**"Generating marketing material to support sales activity."**
Pitchbooks, RFP and DDQ (due diligence questionnaire) answers, one-pagers, case studies. This is the highest-volume, most junior-accessible output category, and almost certainly where an intern spends the largest share of their time, because it does not require years of PM-level judgment to draft a first version, only accuracy, structure, and attention to detail — exactly the traits the internship's own person spec asks for ("attention to detail," "creative thinking") [Brief JD, Reported]. Internal counterparty: Sales/Distribution, who own the commercial relationship and the P&L target; Compliance, who must approve the material before it is used with a client, because it is a financial promotion under FCA rules [S9].

### 1.3 Intern duties, decoded against the team-level duties

The four intern-duty lines in the brief map directly onto the above: "support investment directing and investment specialists" (the intern is embedded support, not client-facing in a lead role); "prepare core marketing presentations and reporting materials" (the pitchbook/factsheet grind, lowest bar to entry); "work with internal stakeholders to source key data and obtain the latest internal views" (the intern is effectively acting as a junior information-broker — going to a PM's desk or an analyst to get an up-to-date number or view, then packaging it correctly, under time pressure, without misquoting the source); "assist with project management tasks" (logistics: scheduling a roadshow, tracking follow-up actions from a client meeting, maintaining a CRM record, building a reusable template). None of this requires prior technical investment skill, consistent with Fidelity's own stated selection philosophy that it does not require "pre-existing technical skills" but does require "an understanding of what we do" [Brief JD, Reported].

### 1.4 A realistic day in the seat — intern and full Investment Director (London hours) [Inferred — reconstructed from the sourced JD facts above and general industry practice, not itself drawn from a single source]

This is a plausible reconstruction, not a verified schedule. Fidelity International's business spans Asia Pacific, Europe and the Americas, and the JD explicitly covers five asset-class groups that trade around the clock, so a London-based Investment Director's morning routinely starts with digesting what happened in Asia overnight and ends with a partial overlap into the US afternoon.

*Intern, typical day:*
- 07:30–08:30 — Arrive, read the overnight market wrap (Asia close, US close), skim any overnight emails from Hong Kong/Tokyo-based colleagues or PMs about portfolio moves relevant to funds the team covers.
- 08:30–09:30 — Team stand-up / morning meeting: what client meetings are happening that day, what's due (a factsheet, an RFP answer, a slide for a pitch next week).
- 09:30–12:00 — Core production work: drafting or updating a pitchbook slide, pulling performance/attribution numbers from the reporting system, chasing an analyst for the latest house view on a data point needed for a client answer.
- 12:00–13:00 — Lunch, possibly a lunchtime internal talk (interns are usually given exposure sessions with senior PMs or strategists).
- 13:00–15:00 — Compliance review loop: material drafted in the morning goes to a reviewer (a senior Investment Director or Compliance) for comment; the intern incorporates changes.
- 15:00–17:00 — Project/admin task: updating a CRM record after a client meeting, building or refreshing a template, helping prep logistics for an upcoming roadshow or webinar.
- 17:00–18:00 — Wrap-up, prepare the next day's data pulls, check for any late-afternoon US market-open items that affect a US-focused client deliverable due the next morning.

*Full Investment Director, typical day:*
- 07:00–08:00 — Reads the Asia overnight handover note/PM commentary, checks anything that moves a covered portfolio materially (a rate decision, a currency move, an earnings surprise in an Asian holding), because the first client call of the day may ask about it.
- 08:00–09:00 — Internal sync with PMs/analysts in the covered asset-class group to get the "line to take" on anything material overnight.
- 09:00–12:00 — Client-facing block: portfolio review meeting with an existing institutional client, or a due-diligence call with a consultant, or final prep and delivery of a new-business pitch. This is the part of the job the JD's "representing... to prospective and existing clients" line refers to directly.
- 12:00–13:00 — Working lunch, often with Distribution/Sales colleagues to align on an upcoming pitch or an at-risk mandate.
- 13:00–15:00 — Content production/oversight: reviewing and signing off marketing material or a factsheet commentary drafted by more junior team members or the intern, ensuring it is technically accurate and compliant before sign-off.
- 15:00–17:00 — Overlap window with the US East Coast opening; a US-based client or the New York consultant-relations team may need a call in this slot.
- 17:00–19:00 (variable) — Thought-leadership work (drafting a viewpoint piece, prepping conference remarks) or project work with Product on a data point a client has been asking for repeatedly, plus setting up questions/materials for the next Asia overnight handover.

### 1.5 Full taxonomy of outputs

Confirmed against real postings and the JD: factsheets and monthly/quarterly commentary; portfolio review packs and client reporting; RFP and DDQ answers; pitchbooks and client pitch presentations; due diligence meetings and consultant meetings; roadshows and webinars; whitepapers/"perspectives"/thought leadership; market updates; performance attribution analysis; holdings reviews; product launch and product "reshape" material; and fund-manager Q&A content used to brief the Investment Director before a client meeting [S1][S5]. A live Fidelity International vacancy for the Systematic Investing team lists, verbatim, "Query management and reporting," "Client servicing and marketing including key investment marketing collateral, client pitch presentations," and "Content for Product launches, reshapes, DDQ, RFPs, perspectives" [S1] — this is a near-complete match to the taxonomy above from a single, real, current FII posting.

### 1.6 Internal clients and external counterparties

Internal: portfolio managers and research analysts (source of the investment view, must be represented accurately); Distribution/Sales (own the commercial relationship and revenue target, the ID supports their pitches); consultant relations specialists (own the relationship with third-party investment consultants); Product development/management (owns the manufacturing decision on new or reshaped funds; the ID feeds it client-demand intelligence) [S10]; the Performance & Attribution team (source of the numbers that go into reporting); Risk; Legal & Compliance (approval gate for every piece of client-facing material); Marketing/Brand; Digital (distribution of content, webinars, digital client portals) [S1][S10].

External: institutional clients (pension schemes, insurers, sovereign wealth funds); investment consultants such as Mercer, WTW, Aon, bfinance and Redington, who act as gatekeepers — a manager typically has to be "on the buy list" or independently rated by a consultant before most institutional searches will even consider it, and Mercer describes being rated as "a prerequisite for inclusion in most client searches" [S11]; wholesale fund selectors and platforms; wealth managers and private banks. The historic UK "investment consultants market" itself was significant enough that the Competition and Markets Authority ran a formal market investigation into it, underlining how much gatekeeping power sits with this small group of consultants [S12].

### 1.7 What the function is judged on

Individual firms' actual internal KPI targets were not found in public sources (see Gaps), but the structural logic of the job, and general industry commentary, points to: net flows and asset retention for the strategies covered; RFP/pitch win rate (one industry study found firms using dedicated RFP software saw a materially higher win rate and more revenue than firms that did not, illustrating how seriously the industry treats RFP execution as a measurable, improvable skill) [S13]; and consultant ratings/buy-list status, since a downgrade or removal from a major consultant's rated list can choke off a large channel of new institutional business in a way no amount of marketing can offset [S11][S12]. Retention of assets specifically during drawdowns (i.e., whether clients stay invested through a bad quarter because the Investment Director successfully explained what happened and why the thesis still holds) is a plausible, industry-standard measure of the "communication under pressure" half of the job, though no source with an exact Fidelity-specific metric was found. ### 1.8 The control dimension — why a candidate cannot say "I'd just write what sells"

This is the single highest-value thing a candidate can demonstrate unprompted, because it is precisely the naive answer ("I'd write persuasive material to help win the mandate") that marks someone as not having done the reading. Every output line in section 1.5 is, in FCA terms, either a "communication with a client" or a "financial promotion," and both are bound by hard rules, not just internal taste:

- **COBS 4.2.1R ("fair, clear and not misleading")**: the FCA Handbook states plainly, "A firm must ensure that a communication or a financial promotion is fair, clear and not misleading" [S14]. This is the umbrella rule under which almost everything an Investment Director writes or says to a client is tested. Guidance under the same section (COBS 4.2.5G) further says firms should not use terms like "guaranteed" or "protected" unless they can actually be described that way fairly [S14].

- **COBS 4.6 (past, simulated past and future performance)**: rules require that, when past performance is shown to a retail client, it must not be made "the most prominent feature" of the communication, must be related to a proper benchmark/index, must normally cover the preceding five years (or the whole life of the product/service if shorter), must disclose whether it is gross or net of fees, and must include a currency-risk warning if the figures are in a different currency to the client's [Reported, from FCA Handbook COBS 4.6 as summarised in search results — the underlying rule provisions were not independently re-verified against the primary Handbook text on a successful fetch, see Gaps]. In practice this means an Investment Director genuinely cannot simply "pick the good five-year window" — the rule constrains the window itself.

- **The UK Consumer Duty (FCA Policy Statement PS22/9, "A new Consumer Duty," published 27 July 2022)**: introduced the requirement that retail communications support customers' understanding and that firms can show a "reasonable relationship" between price and the benefits customers actually receive (the "fair value" outcome), plus a duty to define and stick to a genuine target market for each product [S15]. This binds marketing tone directly — a promotion that is technically true but written to obscure risk or cost fails the Duty even if it does not breach a specific older rule.

- **MiFID II product governance**: requires firms manufacturing or distributing a fund to define a target market for it and review that target market and how the product is performing against it on an ongoing basis, and to give distributors the information they need to sell it only into that target market [S16]. An Investment Director's marketing material has to be consistent with the fund's stated target market — it is not free to be pitched at just anyone who might buy.

- **Compliance sign-off**: every source describing the Investment Director/CPM role independently confirms marketing and client material passes through a compliance/approval step before use (this appears structurally across the postings reviewed, e.g. the explicit coordination with Compliance implied by "DDQ, RFPs" content requiring sign-off) [S1][S9]. Nothing goes to a client "as drafted."

- **Inside information / MNPI and wall-crossing**: because Investment Directors sit close to PMs who may hold price-sensitive views on listed companies (and because due diligence conversations with consultants or large prospective clients can shade into sharing sensitive detail about a strategy's current positioning), the same discipline that binds portfolio managers under the UK Market Abuse Regulation (MAR) binds anyone handling that information. The FCA's 2015 thematic review "Asset management firms and the risk of market abuse" (TR15-01, published 2 March 2015) set out good and poor practice on this: good practice includes independent initial contact points for "soundings" so information can be assessed and, if necessary, declined before a fund manager is told a company name, detailed logs restricting knowledge of inside information strictly to those who need it, and pre-clearance from Compliance before meeting an investee company close to results; poor practice includes fund managers being told a company name during a sounding, deciding not to trade, but never formally logging that they had been wall-crossed [S17]. A candidate should be able to say, unprompted, that being close to real-time portfolio information is precisely why this function cannot simply improvise its communications.

- **GIPS (Global Investment Performance Standards)**: any performance figure quoted in an Investment Director's marketing or RFP material typically has to trace back to a GIPS-compliant composite, calculated to specific, non-negotiable rules (e.g. all discretionary, fee-paying, segregated accounts must sit in at least one composite, valuations must use a proper fair-value hierarchy, and a track record of five, building to ten, years must generally be shown) [S18]. GIPS compliance is treated by the industry as close to a baseline expectation for institutional credibility, and many RFPs require it as a precondition for even being considered [S18].

- **The FCA's sustainability/anti-greenwashing regime**: the anti-greenwashing rule, applying to all FCA-authorised firms, took effect on 31 May 2024 and requires that any sustainability-related claim about a product or service be fair, clear and not misleading [S19]. Separately, the FCA's naming and marketing rules for retail investment products (in the ESG sourcebook, ESG 4.3.2R to ESG 4.3.8R) require a fund's name to accurately reflect its actual sustainability characteristics, took effect from 2 December 2024, with a temporary extension to 5pm on 2 April 2025 for firms that had applied for it by 1 October 2024 under "exceptional circumstances" [S19]; the underlying policy package (PS23/16, "Sustainability Disclosure Requirements (SDR) and investment labels") was published on 28 November 2023, following a consultation (CP22/20) that opened on 25 October 2022 and closed on 25 January 2023, and introduced four investment labels that funds could start using from 31 July 2024 [S20]. For an Investment Director, this means a fund cannot be called or marketed as "sustainable," "ESG," or "impact" unless it earns that description under specific, dated FCA rules — this is exactly the kind of exact-date, exact-rule-name fact a candidate should be able to produce unprompted to show they understand why marketing content in this industry is constrained by law, not by taste.

Put together, the honest answer to "why can't you just write what sells" is: because nearly everything this function produces is legally a regulated communication, is checked against a defined benchmark/target market, must trace to an auditable, standardised performance number, must not overstate sustainability characteristics under specifically dated rules, and sits close enough to live portfolio information that the same market-abuse discipline that binds a fund manager binds the people around them too.

### 1.9 Career path

Confirmed rungs, from real FII job-title evidence: Associate Investment Director exists as a distinct, named grade at Fidelity International (seen in a live London vacancy, "Associate Investment Director – Systematic Investing") [S1], sitting below "Investment Director." The plausible full ladder — intern → analyst → Associate Investment Director → Investment Director → senior/Head of the function — follows standard asset-management seniority conventions and the one confirmed intermediate rung, but the "senior/Head" end of the ladder was not independently confirmed with a named live FII posting in this research pass [Inferred, flagged in Gaps]. Lateral moves this seat is well positioned for, based on the internal counterparties it works with daily (section 1.6), are into Product/Product Development, into Distribution/Sales, or — for those who develop the deepest technical grounding — into an investment (research/PM) seat itself, since the ID role requires and builds genuine technical fluency in the asset class it covers [Inferred, following directly from the counterparty map].
