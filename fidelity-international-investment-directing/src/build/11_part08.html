Part 8

Forming your own view

Six arguments where a thoughtful person could land on either side, with the evidence for each. The point of this section is not to tell you what to think. It is to make sure that whatever you think, you know the strongest version of the opposing case.

Interviewers at this level are not testing whether you agree with them. They are testing whether your view survives contact with a counter-argument. A candidate who holds a position under mild pressure and concedes a genuine point gracefully beats a candidate with a more fashionable opinion every time.

8.1 Is active management worth paying for?

The case for
  • Underperformance statistics are dominated by large, efficient markets, especially US large-cap. They are much weaker evidence about Asian smaller companies, credit or property.
  • Most savers do not want a benchmark return. They want an outcome: income in retirement, capital preserved, volatility they can tolerate. An index cannot be asked for that.
  • Someone must set prices. If everyone indexes, price discovery degrades. Active management is the mechanism, whoever pays for it.
  • Just over 40% of active funds beat their average passive peer in the twelve months to June 2026[S218]. A minority, but a rising one.
The case against
  • 94.1% of US active funds underperformed over the twenty years to 2024[S307]. Two decades is not noise.
  • Morningstar's ten-year success rate was 21%[S308]. Picking the winner in advance is a second, harder problem on top.
  • Fees compound relentlessly. Roughly 60 bps against roughly 10 bps is a 50 bp annual handicap before any skill is demonstrated[S217].
  • Clients are voting: 70.4% of UK platform net inflows went passive in Q2 2026[S232].
Where to land

The defensible position is narrower than "active is worth it". It is: active earns its fee in specific, less efficient places and for specific client outcomes, and an honest firm should concede the passive case in large, liquid, heavily researched markets. Note that this argument has a consequence you should be willing to follow: it implies a firm should be smaller in some markets than it would like to be. Being willing to say that is what makes it sound like a view rather than a script.

8.2 Is private ownership an advantage or an excuse?

AdvantageExcuse
No quarterly earnings call, so it can invest through a cycle. The UK platform's "multi-year investment programme" visibly cost 2024 profit[S78] and no shareholder forced a retreat. Less disclosure means less external scrutiny. There is no analyst asking awkward questions on a call, and no published segmental flow data.
Clients get continuity. Two close comparators are currently being acquired[S129][S132]; this one cannot easily be. No listed equity means no acquisition currency and a harder time paying people in shares, which matters in a talent business.
Management and family ownership aligns decisions with long horizons[S76]. It also concentrates control. The clearest public ownership figure appears to date from 2012[S79]. That is not transparency.
It still cut about 1,000 roles in March 2024[S142] — so privacy did not mean avoiding hard decisions. Which cuts both ways: if a private firm restructures like a listed one, how much is the ownership really buying?
Your opinion goes here

The last row is the sharpest challenge an interviewer could put to you, and it is worth having an answer ready. A reasonable one: private ownership does not remove commercial pressure, it removes the obligation to time decisions around a reporting calendar. The 2024 cuts and the simultaneous platform investment are consistent with exactly that.

8.3 Should it own a platform at all?

For: it reaches the fastest-growing client group directly. Retail is now roughly 30% of UK-managed assets, having passed pension funds[S150]. Platform assets grew 9% against 6% for managed assets[S78]. Against: it drags group operating margin to roughly 5% against roughly 27% for the fund manager alone, and commits the firm to a permanent technology race against specialists who do nothing else[S77][S78].

8.4 Four more, in brief

QuestionOne sideThe other
Pulling back from onshore China Disciplined. Reportedly needed $14bn of assets to break even against roughly 4.5bn yuan actually raised[S223]. Stopping is the right call. Short-termist. Three years is nothing in a market that size, and re-entry later would be expensive and slow.
Private assets in workplace pensions Better long-term returns for savers who have decades before they need the money, and the policy direction is set[S235]. Illiquid, harder to value, and harder to explain. Default-fund members did not choose it. If it goes wrong it goes wrong for people who were not asked.
AI in this function BCG projects 25-35% cost reduction and three to five times more client coverage[S192]. Drafting a factsheet is an obvious use. Every output is a regulated communication. A drafting tool that is confidently wrong is a compliance incident, and the reviewer still carries the liability.
Does this job get automated? The production half might substantially. Assembly from approved components is exactly what software does well. The judgement half will not: deciding what can honestly be claimed, and sitting in a room while a client is angry.
Say this in the interview — if asked whether AI threatens the role "I think it splits the job in two. The production side — assembling a factsheet from approved components, drafting a first version of an RFP answer — is genuinely exposed, and probably should be. The part that isn't is the judgement about what you're allowed to claim and what the evidence actually supports, because that sits under COBS 4.2 and someone has to be accountable for it. A model can draft it. It can't be the person who signs it off. If anything that makes the reviewing skill more valuable, not less, and it's what I'd want to learn."

Why it works: it concedes the real threat rather than being defensive, it uses a rule reference, and it ends on what you want to learn.
