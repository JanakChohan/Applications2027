Part 3

Why the firm is built this way

Four mechanisms: why it is shaped like this, how the parts connect, what clients are actually buying, and how big the non-investing half is. This is the part of the pack that cannot be transplanted to a rival by changing the name.

3.1 The structural choice: it makes the funds and owns the shelf

Most active asset managers manufacture funds and then pay someone else to distribute them. Fidelity International does both. In the UK it runs the fund range, a direct-to-consumer platform, an adviser platform and a workplace pensions business. That is the single most unusual thing about its shape.

The firm's own Distribution pages name the constituent teams: a Global Institutional Team, a Government Institutional Group, retail and wholesale funds, DC and Workplace Savings, Personal Investing and Adviser Solutions[S303] Confirmed. The audited UK accounts put numbers against the two halves: £189.5bn managed, £104.4bn administered[S78] Confirmed.

The conflict, named

If you sell your own funds on your own platform, you have a conflict of interest, and pretending otherwise is the wrong answer. The governing framework is the FCA's SYSC 10, which requires firms to identify and manage conflicts through information barriers and a conflicts policy, and treats disclosure as a last resort rather than a fix[S304] Confirmed.

The concrete mitigant worth knowing: Fidelity's UK platform offers whole-of-market fund choice, with thousands of funds from other managers, rather than a closed shelf of its own products[S305] Reported. That is the difference between a distribution business and a shop window.

Say this in the interview "The structure I find most interesting is that it manufactures and distributes. In the UK it runs the funds, a direct platform, an adviser platform and workplace pensions. That's rare for an active manager, and it cuts both ways. It means you see client demand first-hand instead of through a third-party platform. It also means you're carrying a conflict — selling your own funds on your own shelf — which is a SYSC 10 problem you have to manage with real information barriers, not disclose your way out of. The fact that the platform is whole-of-market rather than a closed shelf tells you something about how they've chosen to handle it."

Why it works: almost no candidate raises the conflict unprompted. Naming it, naming the rule, and naming the mitigant in three sentences reads as someone who has actually thought about the business.

3.2 Why the research platform has to be shared

Fidelity International's pitch rests on proprietary research: its own analysts meeting companies, rather than buying someone else's view. That is expensive, and the economics only work if the cost is spread across every asset class and every product built on top of it Inferred.

Source uncertain — the size of the research platform

Two different Fidelity pages give different headline numbers: roughly 360 versus 400-plus investment professionals, and roughly 16,000 versus 17,000 company meetings a year[S306]. The discrepancy is probably different dates or different definitions of who counts. Do not quote a precise figure. Say "several hundred investment professionals and something like sixteen to seventeen thousand company meetings a year, depending which of their own pages you read" — which is accurate, and quietly shows you read more than one.

3.3 What clients are actually buying, and the case against

Be careful here. The weak answer is "strong long-term performance". The evidence does not support that as a general claim about active management, and an interviewer at an active house knows the numbers better than you do.

The honest case for paying an active feeThe case against, with numbers
Access to proprietary research and to markets a retail investor cannot reach directly, particularly in Asia, where this firm has been present since 1969[S70]. SPIVA data shows 94.1% of US active funds underperformed over the twenty years to 2024[S307] Reported. That is the headline number an interviewer may throw at you.
One provider across a saver's whole life: workplace pension, ISA, drawdown. Switching costs are real and service has value. Morningstar's Active/Passive Barometer reported a 38% one-year success rate for 2025, and only 21% over ten years[S308] Reported.
Breadth: equities, fixed income, real estate and multi asset under one relationship, which matters to an institution that wants fewer managers to monitor. Fees compound against you. Passive funds at roughly 10 bps against active at under 60 bps on average means active must beat the index by the fee difference every year merely to draw level[S166].
Durability. A private, family-and-management-owned firm is not going to be sold out from under a client mid-mandate — a live risk elsewhere, as Part 4 shows. UK platform data for Q2 2026: passive took 70.4% of net inflows, active 29.6%[S231]. Clients are voting.

Your opinion goes here — and you will be asked

"If most active managers underperform, why should anyone pay for active management?" is a near-certain question at an active house. The losing answers are denial ("our funds beat the index") and capitulation ("honestly, they probably shouldn't"). A defensible position: the index-beating claim is the wrong claim. Active earns its fee where markets are less efficiently priced and information is harder to get — smaller Asian companies, credit, property — and where a client needs a specific outcome rather than a benchmark return, such as income in retirement. In a large, liquid, heavily covered market, the passive case is strong and an honest firm should say so. Write your own version. Do not use these words.

3.4 The non-investing side, which is most of the firm

Candidates imagine an asset manager as a floor of fund managers. It is not. The firm describes itself in four parts: Investment Management, Distribution, Customer Operations and Central Functions[S309] Reported. The audited UK accounts name the same shape differently — ISS, GPS and Corporate Enablers[S78].

Scale indicators from the firm's own material: around 280 people in Client Services, more than 230 in Fund Accounting, and more than 800 institutional clients supported[S309] Reported. Against a UK average headcount of 2,941[S78], the operational machine is plainly a large share of the firm, not a back office.
