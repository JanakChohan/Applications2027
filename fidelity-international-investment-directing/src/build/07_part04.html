4.2 The design axes

4.3 Six things only this firm can claim

  1. It cannot be bought out from under its clients right now, and two named peers can. Schroders has an agreed acquisition by Nuveen for about £9.9bn from February 2026[S129], and Janus Henderson an agreed roughly $7.4bn deal with Trian and General Catalyst expected to close in mid-2026[S132] Reported. Why it matters: the single hardest question in an institutional due diligence meeting is "will you still be here, unchanged, in five years?" Private ownership is a real answer to that, and right now it is a differentiated one.
  2. It manufactures funds and owns the UK shelf they sit on. £189.5bn managed and £104.4bn administered in the UK alone[S78] Confirmed. Why it matters: the Investment Directing team's content reaches advisers and end investors through channels the firm controls.
  3. It was built out of Asia before it was built out of London. Tokyo 1969, London 1973, Hong Kong 1981[S70]. Why it matters: it can staff a China or Japan strategy with its own analysts. Most UK-headquartered peers of similar size cannot.
  4. It ran a real fee experiment and reported honestly when it failed. The 0.65% base fee flexing between 0.45% and 0.85%, launched July 2018 against the FCA's market study, withdrawn from open-ended funds in May 2021[S72] Confirmed. Why it matters: you can discuss a pricing mechanism and its communication problem, which is exactly this team's problem.
  5. There is no quarterly earnings call. It files no public group accounts; the numbers in this pack came out of UK subsidiary filings at Companies House[S78]. Why it matters: saying this proves you did primary research, and it is the honest answer to "what couldn't you find out?"
  6. It shares a brand with a much larger company it has not been part of since 1980.[S70][S402] Why it matters: it is a live brand-confusion problem for the firm, and demonstrating you are not confused is a quiet, powerful signal.

4.4 Where it does not lead — read this before you overclaim

Don't say this

"Fidelity is the leading asset manager." It is not, on any measure, and the person opposite you knows it.

  • Not the biggest. Far smaller than BlackRock, Amundi, Invesco, JPMorgan AM, and smaller than its own Boston namesake at about $7.1trn[S137].
  • No passive scale. Nothing resembling iShares. It competes on active and active ETFs[S85].
  • It has shed staff. About 1,000 roles globally in March 2024, roughly 9%[S142], and UK average headcount down from 3,370 to 2,941[S78].
  • It has had outflows. Reported net outflows of €9.2bn across funds and ETFs in the year to end-October 2023[S144] Reported.
  • UK group profit fell about 35% in 2024 on flat revenue[S78].
  • At least one named vehicle has struggled. Fidelity Asian Values saw value-style underperformance trigger a downward fee adjustment under the fulcrum mechanism[S72].
Say this in the interview "I wouldn't pretend it's the biggest or the best-performing — it isn't, and 2024 was clearly a hard year, with the job cuts and UK operating profit down around a third on flat revenue. What I think it does have is a structural position very few others have: private ownership, its own distribution in the UK, and genuine Asian research depth going back to 1969. Given two of its closest listed peers are currently being acquired, being un-buyable is worth more to an institutional client today than it was five years ago."

Why it works: conceding the weak points first buys you the right to make the strong claim. A candidate who only praises sounds like they read the website. A candidate who concedes accurately sounds like they read the accounts.
