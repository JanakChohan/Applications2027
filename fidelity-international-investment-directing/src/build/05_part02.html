2.1 The founding insight, and the split that explains everything

Fidelity Management & Research was founded in Boston by Edward C. Johnson II in 1946[S70]. Fidelity International was set up in 1969 as its international arm, opening in Tokyo that same year, then London in 1973 and Hong Kong in 1981[S70] Reported. In 1980, under Edward "Ned" Johnson III, the international business was separated from the US parent and became independently owned[S70][S402] Confirmed.

Three consequences follow from that, and they run through the rest of this pack.

  • They are two companies. Fidelity International and Fidelity Investments share a name, a founding family and a pre-1980 history, and nothing else operationally. Get this wrong and the interview effectively ends.
  • Asia is not an afterthought. Tokyo came before London. Most UK-associated asset managers built a domestic book first and bolted Asia on later. This one did the reverse[S70]. That is why it still runs long-established single-country vehicles such as Fidelity Japan Trust, Fidelity China Special Situations and Fidelity Asian Values[S72] — products a UK-only competitor would struggle to staff credibly.
  • It stayed private. FIL Limited is incorporated in Bermuda, registered at Pembroke Hall, 42 Crow Lane, Hamilton HM19[S78] Confirmed from the audited related-party note of the UK group accounts.

Who owns it

A partnership controlled by Edward C. Johnson III and family has been reported to hold voting rights over approximately 39.89% of FIL voting stock, with the balance held by fund managers and senior employees[S79][S402] Reported.

Source uncertain — the ownership split

The 39.89% figure is widely repeated but traces to a single secondary report of a company statement, and appears to date from a 2012 Bermuda filing[S79]. The commonly quoted "roughly 40 to 45 per cent employee-owned" claim could not be verified against any primary source and is not repeated here as fact. Say "management and the founding family, with the family reported at just under 40 per cent of voting stock" and you are on safe ground.

The firm's own stated purpose, quoted verbatim from the 2024 statutory accounts, is "working together to build better financial futures"[S78] Confirmed. Its own website adds a line worth remembering because it connects ownership to behaviour: "As a private company we think generationally and invest for the long term"[S76] Confirmed.

Say this in the interview "The 1980 split is the fact I'd want to get right first. Fidelity International has been a separate company from the Boston business since then, and it was built out of Asia before it was built out of London — Tokyo in 1969, London in 1973. That's why it can run a China or a Japan trust with its own research behind it, which most UK-headquartered managers of that size cannot."

Key term · basis point

One basis point is one hundredth of one per cent, written "bp" or "bps". A 35 bp fee on £1,000 is £3.50 a year. The whole industry is priced in basis points because the fee is charged on the value of the assets, so revenue rises and falls with markets without anyone issuing an invoice. This is why a market fall is an immediate revenue event for an asset manager, and why cost discipline matters so much.

Your opinion goes here

Is the platform worth it? The case for: it grew faster than the managed book, it owns the end-client relationship, and it survives even when a fund underperforms. The case against: it drags group margin from roughly 27% to roughly 5%, it eats capital in a "multi-year investment programme", and it puts Fidelity in a technology arms race against businesses that do nothing else. Pick a side before the interview. Either is defensible; having no view is not.

2.5 The risk and control architecture

The UK business runs through several separately FCA-authorised entities rather than one licence, including FIL Investment Services (UK) Limited, FIL Investments International, Financial Administration Services Limited and FIL Pensions Management[S90] Reported. The group's own accounts set out a four-part risk taxonomy, and name non-financial operational risk as "the largest risk to which the Group is exposed"[S78] Confirmed — not market risk, which is the answer most candidates would guess.

Governance: the UK board is chaired by a non-executive, Peter Horrell, separately from the chief executive, Cara Hewitt, appointed 19 July 2024[S78]. It applies the Wates Principles for large private companies and runs three committees, one of which is a dedicated CASS Oversight Forum — CASS being the FCA's client assets rules[S78] Confirmed. A board-level forum devoted solely to client money is a strong, specific detail to have.

2.6 Recent moves, and the skeletons told straight

The strategic tension worth noticing

The 2024 accounts say ISS decided to "cease manufacturing of non-core private asset classes in order to pivot resources to areas of unique strength... active ETFs and the distribution of third-party capabilities for both private assets and select public market investments"[S78] Confirmed. In August 2025 it launched an LTAF to put private assets into workplace pensions[S89]. Those look contradictory until you see the distinction: stop building niche private-asset products in house, keep building access to private assets for the pensions business, largely using other people's Inferred. That is a sophisticated observation and a very good thing to raise.

The skeletons

  • A disclosed remediation. At 31 December 2024 the group carried a £2.503m provision for "potential liabilities arising from processing errors identified in its defined contribution pension business", with £613,000 paid to affected clients during 2024[S78] Confirmed. A real, disclosed operational-risk event. If you are asked for an example of operational risk at Fidelity, use this one.
  • Job cuts. Roughly 1,000 roles globally in March 2024[S84], and a UK headcount fall of about 13%[S78]. Do not pretend this did not happen.
  • Senior investment churn. Within roughly eighteen months: a UK chief executive change, a co-CIO stepping down, and a fixed income CIO transition[S78][S73][S90]. That is a lot of turnover at the top of the investment organisation, and it is fair to note.
  • A pricing experiment that was withdrawn. See below.
Source uncertain — enforcement history

No FCA fine or public censure against a Fidelity International UK entity was found in this research[S78]. That is not the same as confirming there has never been one. The FCA register was not queried entity-by-entity. Never tell an interviewer "Fidelity has never been fined." Say "I didn't find any enforcement action in my research, though I wouldn't claim that's exhaustive." One of those sentences can be wrong. The other cannot.

2.7 The fulcrum fee: the best story in the firm's recent history

This is the single most useful episode to know, because it shows the firm doing something genuinely unusual, and then honestly abandoning it.

In July 2018 Fidelity International launched a variable management fee share class on five UK funds, explicitly in response to the FCA's 2016 Asset Management Market Study, which had criticised the lack of fee innovation and clustering around a flat 0.75%[S72] Confirmed. The base charge was cut to 0.65%, then moved symmetrically up or down by up to 0.20 percentage points on rolling three-year performance against benchmark: a maximum of 0.85% if the fund beat the benchmark, a minimum of 0.45% if it lagged[S72].

On 25 May 2021 Fidelity scrapped it on the open-ended funds, citing limited client demand. A spokesperson said: "We've seen limited client demand in our open-ended funds, and as a result we decided the UK-domiciled VMF share classes were too small to manage cost-effectively... Adoption of our model was always a client choice and we are pleased to see that it opened the doors to wider discussions on pricing structures"[S72] Confirmed. It survived on three investment trusts holding about £2.9bn, because a trust has one share class and an independent board to negotiate with[S72].

AJ Bell's Ryan Hughes named the flaw: "The challenge with fulcrum fees is always that they need explaining... There is also a natural difficulty in explaining to customers that if the fund does well, they will pay higher fees"[S72] Confirmed.

Say this in the interview "The fulcrum fee is the thing I found most interesting. In 2018 Fidelity cut the base fee to 0.65% and let it flex between 0.45% and 0.85% on three-year performance, directly answering the FCA's market study. Then they pulled it in 2021 on the open-ended funds because clients didn't take it up, and it only survived on the investment trusts where there's one share class and a board to negotiate with. What strikes me is that the reason it failed is a communication problem — you have to explain to a customer that good performance means a higher fee. That is exactly the kind of problem this team would own."

Why it works: it is specific, correctly dated, it shows you understand a pricing mechanism, and the last sentence turns a firm fact into a reason you want this seat. Very few candidates will have this.
