11.1 The four-step protocol

Every scenario question is the same question underneath: when this gets hard, what kind of person are you? The protocol below buys you three seconds of thinking time, imposes a structure, and ends with you in control of the conversation rather than waiting to be rescued.

Why the close matters more than the rest

Most candidates produce a reasonable middle and then evaporate: "...so yeah, I think that's what I'd do." The close does two things. It converts your answer from an anecdote into a principle, which is what gets remembered. And handing a question back turns an interrogation into a conversation, which is the position you want to be in for the next forty minutes.

11.2 The priority order when good things collide

Almost every scenario in this job is a collision between four legitimate goods. They are not equal, and knowing the order is most of the answer.

That closing sentence is the one line to hold every frame. It works because it concedes that speed matters, rather than pretending it does not, and then explains why it still loses.

The conduct rules underneath it

You do not need to recite these, but knowing they exist changes how you answer. The FCA's Code of Conduct sets out Individual Conduct Rules applying to almost everyone at a regulated firm[S351] Confirmed:

  1. You must act with integrity.
  2. You must act with due skill, care and diligence.
  3. You must be open and cooperative with the FCA, the PRA and other regulators.
  4. You must pay due regard to the interests of customers and treat them fairly.
  5. You must observe proper standards of market conduct.
  6. You must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers.

Rules 1 to 5 have applied since 7 March 2016; Rule 6 was added on 31 July 2023 to align individual accountability with Consumer Duty[S350] Confirmed. These sit inside the Senior Managers and Certification Regime, extended to asset managers from December 2019[S352].

The point for you: a mis-stated performance number in a pitchbook is not a marketing slip. It is potentially a Rule 1 or Rule 2 matter for whoever signed it off. That is why the people around you will seem disproportionately careful, and why matching their care is how you earn trust as an intern.

11.3 Eight ways they will break your frame

Some interviewers deliberately apply pressure to see what happens. None of this is personal, and all of it has a counter. The general principle: do not speed up, and do not abandon a correct position just because someone frowned.

Say this when you genuinely do not know "I don't know. I'd want to check that with the performance team rather than guess, because it's the kind of number that ends up in a client document. What I can tell you is how I'd think about it..."

This converts ignorance into evidence of judgement. It is one of the strongest things you can say in the whole interview, and it is only available to people who are willing to say the first three words.

11.4 Staying physically calm

  • Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Four in, six out, twice, before you go in. It slows your heart rate. It is not mysticism, it is the vagus nerve.
  • Slow your first sentence deliberately. Nerves show up as speed. If the first sentence is slow, the rest follows.
  • Put both feet on the floor and your hands still. On video, keep your hands in frame; fidgeting is much more visible on camera than in a room.
  • Drink water when you need thinking time. It is a legitimate, invisible pause.
  • If you lose your thread, say so. "Sorry, I've lost where I was going — can I restart that answer?" Universally granted, and far better than trailing off.
