11.6 The composure set

Five situations designed to make you uncomfortable. For each there is a decision tree simple enough to redraw from memory in the thirty seconds before you answer.

Why these have real cases behind them

Two of these scenarios are grounded in actual FCA enforcement, so you can reference a real consequence rather than a hypothetical one. The Woodford Equity Income Fund was suspended on 3 June 2019 holding about £3.5bn. On 11 April 2024 the FCA issued a Final Notice against Link Fund Solutions, the fund's authorised corporate director, ordering restitution of £298,403,919 and stating it would otherwise have proposed a penalty of £50m[S355] Confirmed. On 5 August 2025 the FCA announced fines of £5,888,800 against Neil Woodford personally and £40m against Woodford Investment Management[S356] Confirmed. Separately, on 14 September 2026 the Upper Tribunal upheld a lifetime ban on Crispin Odey[S358] Confirmed — a pure integrity case rather than a marketing one.

You do not need to cite these unprompted. But if an interviewer asks whether the controls really matter, having one precise number is worth more than any amount of principle.

Composure 1 · Two senior people want the same scarce resource

Composure 2 · A senior person asks you to skip a control

Say this in the interview "I'd separate the person from the request. I'm not refusing them, I'm declining one specific action, and I'd try hard to solve the underlying problem — send the approved slides now, flag what's outstanding, chase compliance with the real deadline. If they went ahead anyway I'd tell my manager the same day. In a regulated firm that's not informing on someone, it's the control working."

Composure 3 · You hear something that might be inside information

The tree: Stop → say so immediately, in the room if possible → leave if appropriate → tell compliance the same day → write down what you heard, from whom and when → do not repeat it to anyone, including colleagues.

Why the write-down matters: the FCA's 2015 thematic review identified, as poor practice, fund managers being wall-crossed, deciding not to trade, and never logging that it happened[S17] Confirmed. The record is the control. Saying that shows you understand why the paperwork exists.

The line: "I'd rather ask an awkward question in the moment than be someone who has to be untangled from a list three months later."

Composure 4 · A mistake you made

The tree: Confirm it is real → tell your manager before you fix it → establish the blast radius (who received it, is it material) → fix → propose the change that stops it recurring.

The structure for the answer: spend 20% on what happened, 20% on how fast you disclosed, 60% on what changed afterwards. Most candidates invert this and spend the whole answer on mitigation, which reads as defensiveness.

Don't say this

"I can't think of a significant mistake." This reads as either dishonest or inexperienced, and in a regulated firm it is close to a disqualifying answer. The willingness to report your own errors is a core competence of the job, and you are being asked to demonstrate it right now.

Composure 5 · Acting on incomplete information against a deadline

The tree: Separate what you know from what you are assuming → decide whether the gap is material to the decision → if not, proceed and state the assumption in writing → if yes, deliver the part that is solid, name the gap, give a time for the rest.

The principle: "Incomplete and labelled is fine. Incomplete and unlabelled is the problem." This is the answer to the false-choice tactic in Part 11.3, and it works for almost any case-study question too.

11.7 The CV grill

A good interviewer takes one line of your CV and goes three levels down. Level one is what you did. Level two is how you did it. Level three is whether you actually understood it. Most candidates are solid at level one, fine at level two, and fall apart at level three.

Eight attacks, and how to hold

The attackHow to hold your frame
1 "That sounds like it was mostly your team, not you." Claim your part precisely and give the others theirs. "The analysis was mine, the client relationship wasn't. What I owned specifically was..." Over-claiming here is fatal; under-claiming is nearly as bad.
2 "Why did you choose that approach?" Have a reason that is not "that's what we were told to do." If it was, say so and add what you would do differently now.
3 "What would you do differently?" Have a real answer, and make it a judgement change, not a resource complaint. "More time" is not an answer.
4 "What was the actual outcome?" If you do not know, say so: "I left before it concluded, so I genuinely don't know — what I can tell you is what we'd expected and why." Never invent a result.
5 "That doesn't sound very impressive." Do not inflate in response. "It was a small piece of work. What made it useful to me was..." Re-anchoring on the learning beats defending the scale.
6 "Explain that to me as if I don't know the sector." This is the job interview inside the job interview. Take it seriously. No jargon, one concrete analogy, sixty seconds. Then check: "Does that land, or shall I take a different angle?"
7 "Walk me through the numbers again — that doesn't add up." Slow down. Go back to the source. If you cannot reconcile it live: "I can't square that from memory and I don't want to guess at it. I'll send you the workings." That answer is fine here for exactly the reasons it is fine in the job.
8 "Why did you leave?" / "Why this gap?" One clean sentence, no defensiveness, no criticism of anyone, then forward. Length signals discomfort more than content does.
The four-line drill — do this for every bullet on your CV
  1. What I did, in one sentence, with my part clearly separated from the team's.
  2. One number attached to it, that I can defend and explain the source of.
  3. The hardest judgement call I made, and why I made it that way.
  4. What I'd do differently, phrased as a change in judgement rather than a change in circumstances.

Four lines per bullet. If your CV has ten bullets that is forty lines, which is about an hour of work and covers perhaps 70% of everything you will be asked. There is no higher-return preparation available to you.
