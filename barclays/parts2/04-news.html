PART EIGHTNews, deals, wins and losses

You asked for success stories, deals and closures. Here is everything material in the last eighteen months, with what each one actually tells you.

WON — Premier Wealth Management launched, April 2026

Advice and financial planning for Barclays Premier customers with £150,000+ to invest. Begins with a no-obligation in-depth financial review carrying no upfront fee; clients pay ongoing fees only if they proceed.

What it tells you: this is the mass-affluent conversion engine going live. CEO C.S. Venkatakrishnan described the strategy as a play to own the entirety of the customer. Removing the upfront fee removes the last friction between four million existing customers and an advice relationship.

WON — Direct Investing custody fees abolished

Barclays removed the monthly customer fee on Investment ISAs and Investment Accounts held with Direct Investing (renamed from Smart Investor).

What it tells you: deliberate margin sacrifice to buy scale and volume at the bottom of the ladder, betting those clients climb it later. It is also a direct response to zero-fee digital platforms.

LOST — the Evelyn Partners auction, February 2026

Barclays bid for Evelyn Partners (£69bn AUM, £250k minimum for discretionary management). NatWest won at £2.7bn, completing summer 2026, taking its combined wealth AUM to about £128bn. RBC and several private equity firms also participated in the Evercore-run process.

What it tells you: the single most important competitive fact in UK wealth right now. Barclays wanted to buy scale in exactly the segment it is targeting, was willing to pay billions, and was beaten. Organic execution is now the only route.

WON — GoHenry acquired, June 2026

Roughly £180m for GoHenry's UK business, brand and app — a money-management platform for 6–18 year-olds with more than 500,000 UK children (about 2.3 million globally). Completing Q4 2026, pending regulatory approval. GoHenry will keep running as its own brand rather than being absorbed.

What it tells you: lifecycle banking taken to its logical extreme. Win the parent, win the child, hold the relationship from a first debit card to a family estate plan.

BUILDING — Singapore booking centre, 2026

A full return to on-the-ground private banking in Singapore, upgrading it from a sales office to a booking centre. Werner Schlossmacher appointed COO for Barclays Private Bank Asia, based in Singapore, reporting to Leo Müller.

What it tells you: Asia is where new wealth is being created fastest. A booking centre — not just a sales office — means assets can be held locally, which is what serious Asian and Middle Eastern clients require.

BUILDING — 100 wealth managers, tech spend up 75%+

Up to 100 new advisers — relationship managers and operational staff — embedded in FY2026 cost guidance. Annual technology spend for wealth up more than 75% on its 2021–22 level. Hubs: London, Geneva, Dubai, India.

What it tells you: the division is actively hiring, which is directly relevant to you. It also explains the 11% cost growth in H1 2026.

CLOSED — US Wealth & Investment Management sold to Stifel, 2015

Completed 4 December 2015: approximately 180 financial advisers and $56bn of client assets transferred to Stifel Financial.

What it tells you: Barclays has failed at American wealth before and has explicitly ruled out returning on cost and competition grounds. This is the honest counterweight to the growth story, and mentioning it shows you have looked past the good news.

CONTEXT — the transfer that created today's division

In 2024 Barclays moved Wealth Management & Investments out of Barclays UK and into the private bank, creating the five-division structure and making PB&WM a standalone reported business under Sasha Wiggins from February 2024. Private bank income rose 17% to £1.2bn that year, with client assets and liabilities reaching nearly £180bn.

What it tells you: the division exists in its current form only because Barclays decided wealth deserved its own scoreboard. It is a young reporting line with old roots.

PART NINEWhy a client picks Barclays over anyone else

This is your question — what is it in the business model? The answer is not service quality or investment performance, because every competitor claims both.

The four genuine structural advantages

  1. The referral engine no independent can replicate. Barclays does not have to find wealthy clients. It banks around 20 million UK customers and can see, in its own data, who has just received a large sum, whose income has jumped, whose business account just recorded a sale. Roughly four million existing customers sit in the £250k–£3m target band. An Evelyn Partners or a Rathbones must win every single client from a standing start; Barclays sends an invitation up its own ladder. This is the whole argument, and everything else is secondary.
  2. The investment bank connection. When Barclays' investment bank advises a founder on selling their company, the private bank is present at the moment the cash lands. Coutts, Julius Baer and every UK independent lack a bulge-bracket M&A franchise. UBS has one — which is precisely why UBS is the most credible comparison.
  3. Balance sheet. Barclays can lend meaningfully against a portfolio, a property, or an unlisted holding, structured case by case, funded by a £1.7trn bank. A pure wealth manager cannot lend at all, and this matters enormously to entrepreneurs who are asset-rich and cash-poor. It is also why the division's lending carries almost no credit losses.
  4. Cross-border booking that took a century to build. Switzerland, Monaco, the Crown Dependencies, India, the Gulf, and Singapore from 2026. For a family with money and members in three countries, that is not a nice-to-have — it is the entire requirement, and most UK competitors simply cannot serve it.

The honest weaknesses — say these too

Scale. £55.8bn of AUM is a fraction of UBS or Morgan Stanley, and NatWest has just leapfrogged Barclays on UK AUM by acquisition.

Cost. A 73% cost:income ratio is the worst in Barclays, and costs are currently growing five times faster than income.

The conflict question. Independents will always argue that a bank-owned wealth manager is conflicted — pushing in-house product and using wealth as a funnel for the rest of the bank. Barclays' answer is that integration is a feature, not a bug; the client gets lending, banking and advice in one place. Both arguments are legitimate.

Rate dependency. 58% of income is net interest income. As rates fall that half comes under pressure, and the fee half has to grow fast enough to cover it.
