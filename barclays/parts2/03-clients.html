PART SIXThe clients, and why these ones

Barclays has been unusually explicit about who it is chasing. The target is not, primarily, the ultra-rich.

The gap Barclays is aiming at

Sasha Wiggins, chief executive of the division, frames the opportunity in one number: The UK is a large market with client investable assets totalling some 3.5 trillion pounds. Against that, Barclays' own £140.6bn of invested assets is about 4% — which is the point. There is enormous room to grow inside a market Barclays already banks.

Read that first number again. Roughly four million people who already bank with Barclays have between £250,000 and £3m to invest. Barclays does not need to find them, market to them, or buy them — it already has their salary, their mortgage and their app. It simply needs to convert them. That is the single most important sentence in this report, and it is why Premier Wealth Management was launched in April 2026 with no upfront fee and why custody fees on Direct Investing were scrapped: remove every possible reason to say no.

Where in the world

Barclays named its growth hubs explicitly: London, Geneva, Dubai and India, plus Singapore, where a full booking centre is being built during 2026 — Werner Schlossmacher was appointed COO for Barclays Private Bank Asia, based in Singapore, reporting to Leo Müller, the division's COO. Barclays has had a presence across Asia since 1864 and has provided investment services in India since 1990.

And where it has explicitly ruled out

The United States. Barclays has publicly ruled out re-entering US wealth management on grounds of cost and competition — a striking decision for a bank with a bulge-bracket Wall Street investment bank and a large American card business. It is also a decision with history behind it: Barclays sold its US Wealth & Investment Management business to Stifel in December 2015, roughly 180 advisers and about $56bn of client assets. UBS, by contrast, is pushing hard into US mass-affluent wealth. The two firms have diverged deliberately, and being able to name that divergence is a genuinely sophisticated observation.

You asked how long they have been around. In wealth management, longevity is not sentiment — it is licences, booking centres and trust businesses that competitors cannot replicate quickly.

Why the history is a commercial moat, not a brochure line

To serve an international private client you need a booking centre in the right jurisdiction, a trust and fiduciary licence, cross-border marketing permissions, and a regulator who trusts you. Barclays has been over a century in the Crown Dependencies, is the longest-serving foreign wealth manager in Monaco, established Geneva and Zurich in the mid-1980s, and has run investment services in India since 1990. A well-funded new entrant cannot buy that in five years — which is precisely why NatWest had to spend £2.7bn to buy scale rather than build it.

The 2003 deal that made the modern business

The most important single transaction in this division's history is one almost nobody mentions. In December 2003 Barclays bought the Gerrard group from Old Mutual for £210m. Gerrard came with £12.5bn under management and 116,000 clients, and a lineage traceable to at least 1870. Combined with Barclays Investment Management it created a business running more than £21bn in discretionary and advisory portfolios — at the time the UK's largest adviser to wealthy investors.

What Barclays specifically did: it bought a distribution network and a client book, not an investment process. Gerrard's value was 116,000 existing relationships and the advisers who held them. Barclays then folded Gerrard together with Barclays Private Bank, Barclays Stockbrokers, the International Private Bank and the Estates & Trust business to create Barclays Wealth. Every rung of today's ladder above Premier traces to that consolidation. It is also the template Barclays tried to repeat with Evelyn Partners in 2026 — buy the client book, plug it into the existing platform — and this time it was outbid.
