2.1 The founding insight

The history starts in 1804, "when JH Schröder became a partner in J.F. Schröder & Co, a London-based firm founded by his brother JF Schröder" [S72]. J. Henry Schröder & Co was set up in London in 1818 [S81] Reported. For most of the next two centuries Schroders was a merchant bank: it financed trade and "became one of London's leading merchant banks, arranging bonds for transformative infrastructure", including an 1870 bond for Japan's first railway [S80].

Asset management grew out of that banking business. Schroders launched its first investment trust in 1924, set up an Investment Department in 1926 and won its first UK pension fund mandate in 1947. It listed on the London Stock Exchange in 1959, "oversubscribed eighteen times" [S80].

The decisive pivot came in 2000. Schroders sold its investment banking arm to Citigroup for about £1.35bn (US$2.2bn), with around 3,000 staff moving to Citi [S81, S82] Reported. Schroders' own history page says the sale was to focus on "asset and wealth management" [S80]. It then built out by acquisition:

  • 2005. Joint venture with Bank of Communications in China [S80].
  • 2013. Cazenove Capital, for about £424m [S81] Reported.
  • 2019. A majority stake in BlueOrchard [S80].
  • 2022. River & Mercantile's UK solutions business, which became Schroders Solutions (enterprise value £230m) [S106] Reported.
  • Announced December 2021. 75% of Greencoat Capital, for an initial £358m [S105] Reported.
Why the history matters for a sales role

A merchant bank lives on relationships and reputation over decades. That DNA survives in the client model. The annual report notes 13 agreements with 12 institutional clients that are more than 25 years old, and one wealth family "served for over 75 years, across four generations" [S72]. Inferred

In the firm's own words today [S72]:

  • Purpose: "Creating prosperity together."
  • Vision: "We partner with our clients to provide trusted advice and invest in the assets and markets that matter to them, building their future prosperity through delivering excellent investment outcomes."
  • Values: "We strive for excellence. We promote innovation and teamwork. We have passion and integrity."

The brand campaign since 2025 is "Active Edge" [S71]. The JD's older line, "excellent investment performance to clients through active management", is still on the advert [S263].

2.4 The risk and control architecture

Asset managers do not risk their own balance sheet the way banks do. The main risks are to clients' money, to reputation and to revenue. Schroders' structure, from the 2025 annual report [S72]:

  • The Board has "ultimate accountability for identifying, managing and monitoring the firm's principal risks". A combined Audit and Risk Committee reviews a quarterly risk dashboard.
  • The Chief Risk Officer chairs the Group Risk Committee, which meets five times a year. Its sub-committees cover conflicts, financial crime and information security.
  • Three lines of defence. The first line is the business, including Client Group. The second line was narrowed in 2025 to Risk and Compliance only. The third line is Internal Audit.
  • Principal risks for 2025.
    • Business model disruption
    • Financial risk
    • Information security and technology
    • Investment performance
    • Operational process
    • People and conduct
    • Regulatory (including financial crime)
    • Sustainability
    • Third-party risk: new in 2025 "as a result of our increased use of outsourcing"
    • Transformation risk
  • Liquidity rules for private-market funds. The liquidity risk framework was updated for open-ended private-market vehicles such as evergreen funds and LTAFs.
Key term: three lines of defence

A control model. The first line owns and manages risk day to day: the people doing the work, including you. The second line sets rules and checks them: Risk and Compliance. The third line independently audits both: Internal Audit.

Why this produces the firm's characteristic result. An active manager's product is trust over many years. The controls that matter most to Client Group are the ones that protect that trust: approvals for anything a client sees, conflicts management, and the liquidity rules that stop a fund promising more access than its assets allow Inferred.

@@A028@@

3.1 Why the business is structured this way

Schroders runs three businesses: Public Markets (£533.1bn at end-2025), Schroders Capital, its private markets arm (£72.6bn), and Wealth Management (£123.9bn). Joint ventures add £94.1bn [S71]. A single Client Group sells across them, and a corporate centre (technology, operations, finance, risk and compliance, people, legal, audit, tax) supports all three [S72].

The theory: five reasons for one central Client Group

This argument is the author's reading of the firm's own words Inferred. It is not stated in one place.

  1. Concentrate support on a few propositions. The nine "leading capabilities" produce more than 85% of Public Markets revenue [S301]. More than 16% of the fund range is being merged or closed [S71]. A central team can decide where marketing money and sales time go. Sales teams inside each business would each push their own long tail. The JD calls this area Value Proposition.
  2. One client, many capabilities. "By sharing insights and expertise across public and private market capabilities globally, we are able to deliver bespoke solutions" [S72]. A pension scheme may want fiduciary management, private credit and global equities at once. One relationship owner stops three business lines calling the same chief investment officer. The Mercer LTAF and the Apollo partnership are cross-capability products [S71, S96].
  3. Global centre, local execution. "We engage with clients through specialist teams based in regional hubs around the world ... delivering dedicated support through Client Service Hubs" [S72]. The centre designs; the regions sell. The JD calls this Sales Activation.
  4. Scale in the plumbing. The CRM, data and marketing technology are shared. The sales enablement team sits "in a virtual network with Distribution & Marketing, Technology, Finance, Product Management" and drives "consistent adoption of CRM (Salesforce) across Distribution teams" [S317]. The JD calls this Delivery.
  5. Cost. The 2025 strategy named "focused sales and marketing" and removing "duplication and overlapping processes" as sources of the £150m saving [S301]. Adjusted cost:income fell from 75% to 68% [S71, S70].

What the structure costs

  • Distance from complex products. Schroders made an exception for private markets. It built a dedicated Schroders Capital team of "40 sales specialists" [S71]. That concedes a generalist salesforce is not enough for complex products. Whether that team reports into Client Group is not confirmed.
  • Wealth runs its own client model. Cazenove's advisers own their client relationships [S72] Inferred.
  • Disruption. Job cuts, market exits and fund closures mean some clients lose products and some staff lose roles [S88, S71].
  • A single point of failure. If the centre picks the wrong propositions, every region sells the wrong thing Inferred.

Why combine asset management, private markets and wealth

The margin table in Part 2 is the answer in numbers. Private markets earn about 57bp and Cazenove about 48bp, against 6bp in core solutions [S71]. Wealth adjusted operating profit grew at a compound 15% a year for three years to £198.3m [S71]. Schroders Capital's strategy is to "enhance access to the Wealth segment" with evergreen and semi-liquid funds [S301]. So wealth gives stable, high-margin revenue and a captive route to market for private assets, while public markets gives scale Inferred.

Honest caveat

Wealth net new business was 2.7% in 2025, against a 5 to 7% target [S71]. Selling Benchmark narrows Wealth to Cazenove in the UK and Schroders Wealth Management internationally [S70]. The combination works best at the high-net-worth end, not through adviser platforms.

@@A039@@

5.1 What an asset manager does

An asset manager invests other people's money for a fee. It does not own the money. A pension scheme, an insurer or a saver owns it, and a separate bank (the custodian) holds the assets safely. The manager makes the decisions: what to buy, what to sell, how much risk to take [S150].

The fee is almost always a small percentage of the money managed, charged every year. It is not a share of the profit. So an asset manager earns more when it manages more money, whether that money grew because markets rose or because new clients arrived.

Key term: AUM

Assets under management (AUM) is the total market value of the money a manager runs for clients. Schroders reported £867.8bn at 30 June 2026 [S220]. AUM is the base on which fees are charged, so it is the single most watched number in the industry.

Key term: basis point (bp)

One basis point is one hundredth of one percent. 0.45% is 45 basis points. Worked example: a client with £100m in a fund that charges 45bp pays £450,000 a year. If the fund's value rises to £110m, the fee rises to £495,000 without any extra sales effort.

Inside every asset manager there are two halves [S150]:

  • The investment side. Fund managers, analysts and traders who decide what to own.
  • The client side. The people who design products, market them, sell them, and look after clients once they have invested. At Schroders this half is called Client Group [S306]. The internship sits here.

Neither half works without the other. A brilliant fund nobody hears about gathers no money. A brilliant salesperson with poor funds loses clients within three years, because that is how long most professional buyers wait before judging a track record.

5.5 How money moves: flows, fees and margins

AUM changes for three reasons. Markets move the value of what clients already own. Currency moves change the sterling value of overseas assets. And net flows (money in from clients minus money out) show whether clients are choosing the firm. Only the third one is earned by Client Group.

Key terms: gross flows, net flows, NNB

Gross inflows are all new money received. Net flows or net new business (NNB) subtract redemptions. Schroders took £69.3bn of gross inflows in H1 2026 but had net outflows of £8.3bn, most of it one £6.6bn low-margin institutional redemption [S220].

BCG found that more than 80% of the industry's revenue growth in 2025 came from rising markets, not from new money [S154]. That is the uncomfortable truth behind every results statement. It also explains why a firm like Schroders reorganised its sales and marketing: when markets stop rising, only distribution grows the business.

Fee margins differ hugely by product. Schroders publishes them. In 2025 private markets (Schroders Capital) earned 57bp, Cazenove Capital wealth 48bp, equities 45bp, fixed income 34bp, multi-asset 24bp and core solutions 6bp [S300]. So £1bn won in private markets brings in roughly ten times the revenue of £1bn won in core solutions. Salespeople who understand this talk about revenue won, not just assets won.

@@A044@@

5.6 The forces reshaping the industry

Passive investing and fee pressure. Index funds buy every share in an index at very low cost. They held 35% of UK-managed assets in 2024 [S150]. In 2025 UK retail investors put £12.8bn into trackers and pulled £15.1bn from active funds [S152]. Institutional fees are falling about 3% a year [S154].

Fewer, bigger buyers. The Pension Schemes Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026) requires multi-employer DC default funds to reach £25bn by 2030 [S183]. The LGPS now invests through six pools [S185]. A salesperson now has fewer doors to knock on, each far bigger and more expert.

Private markets for everyone. The Mansion House Accord (13 May 2025) saw 17 DC providers aim for at least 10% of default funds in private markets by 2030, with 5% in the UK [S181]. That creates demand for products such as the Long-Term Asset Fund (LTAF). The Bank of England's July 2026 Financial Stability Report is the counterweight: "Redemption requests have been elevated in several retail funds, with some limiting redemptions" [S196].

The retail push. From 6 April 2027 the cash ISA limit falls to £12,000 for under-65s, inside the unchanged £20,000 total [S187]. Targeted support, a new form of help between guidance and advice, went live on 6 April 2026 [S170]. The "Invest for the Future" campaign launched on 23 April 2026, and Schroders is a founding member [S189].

Active ETFs. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) trades on a stock exchange like a share. Most track an index, but an active ETF has a manager picking investments. European active ETFs took $33.9bn in the first eight months of 2026, already beating 2025's full-year record [S251]. Schroders' own range reached $4.3bn by 21 September 2026 [S230].

AI and cost. BCG estimates AI could cut costs by 25 to 35% over three to five years and let one relationship manager cover three to five times more clients [S154].

6.2 How fund selling changed: five steps

  1. The commission era (before 2013). Fund houses paid advisers commission for recommending their funds. Part of sales was about who paid more.
  2. The Retail Distribution Review (RDR), in force 31 December 2012. It banned product providers from paying commission to advisers and raised adviser qualifications. The FCA found in 2014 that "commission is no longer a driving factor in advisers' recommendations" [S177].
  3. The platform rebate ban, 6 April 2014. Platforms had to be paid by explicit charges; legacy payments ended in April 2016 [S178]. This created "clean" share classes and pushed fees down.
  4. Gatekeepers take over. Investment consultants (Mercer, WTW, Aon, LCP, Hymans Robertson) now shortlist managers for pension schemes using their own ratings [S198]. On the wholesale side, central fund-selection teams and model portfolios decide. Platform model portfolios passed £214bn at end-2025 [S193].
  5. The gatekeepers are regulated too. The Competition and Markets Authority's Order, made 10 June 2019, forced pension trustees to tender fiduciary management covering 20% or more of their assets and to set objectives for their consultants [S179].
Say this in the interview

"Since RDR removed commission in 2012, fund houses win on performance, value and service to professional buyers. That's why Consumer Duty and assessments of value matter so much to sales today."

@@A050@@

6.3 How an institutional sale happens

The steps are general industry practice, not a single published Schroders process Inferred. A trustee board sets an objective. Its consultant screens the managers it rates. The shortlisted managers answer a request for proposal (RFP) and a due diligence questionnaire (DDQ) covering team, process, risk, fees, operations and sustainability. Three to six managers present in person (the "beauty parade"). One wins, negotiates fees and signs an investment management agreement. Then the real work starts: reporting, meetings and keeping the client for years.

Where an intern helps

Interns rarely pitch. They make pitching possible: updating pitch books with approved figures, drafting DDQ answers from the library, preparing meeting briefs, logging activity in the CRM (Salesforce at Schroders [S317]), organising events and researching prospects. Every one of these touches a rule described below.

How to rank the workplace scenarios

You will usually see a short workplace situation and four or so responses to rank from most to least effective [S262, S280]. Four rules decide almost every ranking in a client-facing asset management seat Inferred:

  1. Rules first. Anything that sends unapproved, out-of-date or inside information goes to the bottom.
  2. The client gets an honest, timely answer. Acknowledge, give a time, keep promises.
  3. The owner decides. Escalate to the relationship manager or diary owner with options, rather than deciding alone or dumping the problem.
  4. Initiative inside the lines. Doing something useful now beats waiting, as long as rules 1 to 3 hold.

10.3 Reading the room live

  • If they lean in or write fast, you are on something they score. Finish the point cleanly; don't add a second example.
  • If they look at the clock or the grid, compress: result and link, then stop.
  • If they interrupt, answer the new question first, then offer to finish the old one (Part 11.3).
  • If they challenge a fact, check it calmly. "You may well be right; my source was the H1 results. Either way, the point is..."
  • If there are two assessors, answer the person who asked, then include the other with your eyes on the result line.

This is about adapting your communication, which is on the JD, not about managing people. The same honest answer, pitched at the right level.

11.1 The four-step protocol: steady, sort, act, close

StepWhat you doPhrases that execute it
SteadyBreathe out. Restate the core of the problem so they know you heard it."So the situation is..." / "Let me make sure I've got this: ..." / "The key issue seems to be..."
SortName what collides. Apply the priority order."Two things matter here: X and Y." / "The rule comes first, then the client, then speed." / "What I don't know yet is..."
ActSay what you would do, in order, and who you would tell."First I'd..., then I'd..., and I'd tell [owner] by [time]." / "I'd put it in writing so there's a record."

@@A073@@

11.2 The priority order when good things collide

Why this order in a Client Group seat:

  • Rules first. They carry personal and firm-wide consequences: financial promotions, inside information and data [S401, S402, S403].
  • The client's real interest second. For retail outcomes that is itself a rule under Consumer Duty [S161], and trust is what an active manager sells.
  • Fairness and the record third. Client Group is a shared hub, so queue-jumping by rank breaks it.
  • Speed last. Speed is what you flex Inferred.
@@A074@@

11.3 Eight ways assessors break a frame, and the counter to each

Staying calm physically

  • Breathe out first. A long exhale (four seconds) before you speak slows the heart rate more than a deep breath in.
  • Feet flat, hands still and visible. On video, rest forearms on the desk.
  • Slow the first sentence on purpose. The rest follows its pace.
  • Hold a pause. "Let me think about that for a second" is always acceptable. Three seconds of silence feels long to you and normal to them.
  • Anchor on paper. Keep a card with the four protocol words and the one-sentence frame.

General composure practice Inferred; not Schroders guidance.

1. Markets and opinions

JD line: "Shows genuine interest in asset management and current market trends, with the ability to discuss the impact of economic events on investments and form well-reasoned opinions on commercial or financial matters."

What has caught your eye in markets?easy
Setup
The assessor opens with: "What is one market story you have followed recently and why does it matter to our clients?"
Trap
Picking a headline you cannot explain two levels down, or reciting a number with no "so what".
Say
Name one story, one number, one mechanism, one client. "The Bank of England's path on rates matters to a UK DB pension client because it moves gilt yields, which moves the value of their liabilities." Then say what you would watch next.
Why
The JD asks you to "discuss the impact of economic events on investments". A chain from event to client is exactly that skill. [S232]
Twist
"And what would change your mind?"
Hold
Give one data point that would falsify your view. Holding a view and naming its weak point is the frame.
Defend a view on active versus passivemedium
Setup
"Passive is cheaper and usually wins. Why would anyone pay Schroders' fees?"
Trap
Getting defensive, or conceding completely. Both lose.
Say
Concede the fact, then narrow the claim. "On average, after fees, many active funds do lag their index over long periods. [S319, S321] The case for a firm like Schroders sits where indices are weak or absent: private markets, solutions, less efficient markets, and clients who need an outcome, not a benchmark."
Why
You show you know the evidence and you still find the commercial logic. That is a "well-reasoned opinion". [S71]
Twist
"So you're saying active is dead in large-cap US equity?"
Hold
"Harder to justify there, yes. That is why the mix matters more than any single fund." Do not retreat further than the evidence.
The client blames the market on youhard
Setup
Role-play: a client says, "Your fund fell 8% this quarter. Explain yourself."
Trap
Guessing at reasons for the fall, promising a rebound, or apologising for the market.
Say
Acknowledge, clarify, commit. "That is a fair question and you should get a proper answer. Can I check which share class and period you're looking at? I'll come back today with the attribution from the investment team, and I'd like to set up a call with the portfolio manager."
Why
An intern should never improvise performance explanations or forecasts. Communications with clients must be fair, clear and not misleading. [S401] Promises of recovery are exactly what the rules forbid.
Twist
"I don't want the fund manager. I want your view, now."
Hold
"My honest view is that I don't have the full attribution in front of me, and a guess from me wouldn't help you. You'll have it today."

Glossary

85 terms in plain English. Where a term has a date or rule behind it, the source is in the part where it first appears.

Active ETF. An exchange-traded fund run by a manager making active choices.

Active management. Choosing investments by judgement to beat a benchmark or meet an outcome.

Alpha. Return above the benchmark attributable to the manager's skill.

AML. Anti-money laundering: checks that clients and distributors are not moving criminal money.

Assessment of Value (AoV). An annual report in which a UK fund manager judges whether each fund's charges are justified (from 30 Sep 2019).

Asset management. Investing other people's money for a fee.

AUM. Assets under management: the market value of money a manager runs.

AUMA. Assets under management and administration: AUM plus assets held on platforms.

Basis point (bp). One hundredth of one percent. 0.45% = 45bp.

Benchmark. The index or target a fund is measured against.

Beta. Return from market exposure itself.

Buy side. Firms that invest: asset managers, wealth managers, hedge funds, private equity.

Buyout (bulk annuity). A DB pension scheme insuring its pensions with an insurer.

Cazenove Capital. Schroders' UK wealth management and private banking business.

CCI. Consumer Composite Investments: the new UK retail disclosure regime replacing the PRIIPs KID; mandatory from 8 Jun 2027.

Client Group. Schroders' central function for sales, client service, product and marketing.

Compliance. The team that sets and checks the rules; part of the second line of defence.

Consumer Duty. FCA rule requiring firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers (PS22/9).

Core solutions. Schroders' low-margin, large-scale solutions business, including fiduciary management (about 6bp).

Cost:income ratio. Operating costs as a share of income. Schroders: 68% in H1 2026.

CRM. Customer relationship management system. Schroders uses Salesforce.

Custodian. The bank that holds a fund's assets safely.

DB pension. Defined benefit: a promised pension; the employer bears the risk.

DC pension. Defined contribution: a pot built from contributions; the member bears the risk.

DDQ. Due diligence questionnaire covering team, process, risk, operations and ESG.

Default fund. Where DC members' money goes if they make no choice.

Delisting. Removal of a company's shares from a stock exchange. Schroders: 2 Oct 2026.

DFM. Discretionary fund manager: a wealth manager allowed to make decisions without asking each time.

Distribution. Everything that gets a product to clients: sales, marketing, platforms, intermediaries.

ETF. Exchange-traded fund: a fund whose shares trade on a stock exchange.

Fee compression. The long-run fall in fees as passive and competition grow.

Fiduciary management. A pension scheme handing day-to-day investment decisions to one provider.

Financial promotion. Marketing that invites investment; must be fair, clear and not misleading (COBS 4).

Fund selector. A professional who chooses funds for a wealth manager, platform or model portfolio.

Gross inflows. All new money received before withdrawals.

IFA. Independent financial adviser, paid by client fees since the RDR.

Inducement. A payment or benefit from a third party that could bias service; restricted (COBS 2.3A).

Inside information. Precise, non-public information likely to move a price (UK MAR).

Institutional client. A pension scheme, insurer, sovereign fund or similar organisation.

Intermediary. A firm between the manager and end investors: platforms, wealth managers, advisers.

Investment consultant. Adviser to pension trustees on strategy and manager selection; a gatekeeper.

Investment trust. A UK closed-ended fund listed as a company.

JV. Joint venture: a business owned with a partner, such as Schroders' China JVs with Bank of Communications.

KYD. Know your distributor: due diligence on firms that sell your funds.

LDI. Liability-driven investment: matching a DB scheme's assets to its pension promises.

LGPS pool. One of six vehicles that invest local government pension money.

LTAF. Long-Term Asset Fund: a UK open-ended fund for illiquid assets, aimed at DC and wider retail.

Mandate. The instructions under which a manager runs a client's money.

Mansion House Accord. A May 2025 pledge by 17 DC providers to put 10% of default funds in private markets by 2030.

Megafund. A DC default arrangement of at least £25bn, required by 2030 under the Pension Schemes Act 2026.

MiFID II. EU rules (UK-retained) on investment services, including product governance and inducements.

Model portfolio service (MPS). Ready-made portfolios advisers use on platforms.

NAV. Net asset value: a fund's assets minus liabilities, per share.

Net new business (NNB). New money in minus money out; growth that is not from markets.

Net operating revenue. Fee income after costs paid to distributors; Schroders' main revenue line.

OCF. Ongoing charges figure: the yearly cost of a fund as a percentage.

OCIO. Outsourced chief investment officer: a manager running a client's whole portfolio.

OEIC. Open-ended investment company: a UK fund whose shares are created and cancelled at NAV.

Passive management. Copying an index at low cost.

Per se professional client. A client treated as professional by definition, such as a pension fund or central bank (COBS 3.5.2R).

Pitch book. The presentation used in a client or prospect meeting.

Platform. An online service holding investors' funds, ISAs and pensions.

Pooled fund. A fund shared by many investors.

Private markets. Unlisted assets: private equity, private credit, infrastructure, property.

Product governance. Rules requiring every product to have a defined target market (PROD 3).

RDR. Retail Distribution Review: banned commission to advisers from 31 Dec 2012.

Relationship manager (RM). The salesperson who owns a client relationship.

Retail client. An individual investor; the most protected category.

RFP. Request for proposal: a formal tender from a client.

RNS. Regulatory News Service: how UK-listed firms publish results and announcements.

Scheme of arrangement. A court-approved way to take over a company; used by Nuveen for Schroders.

Schroders Capital. Schroders' private markets business (about 57bp margin).

SDR. Sustainability Disclosure Requirements: FCA regime with four fund labels and naming rules.

Segregated account. A portfolio run for one client only.

Sell side. Banks and brokers that sell services to investors.

SM&CR. Senior Managers and Certification Regime: individual accountability in FCA firms.

Stewardship. Voting and engagement with companies on clients' behalf.

Strength (Cappfinity). Something you do well, find energising and use often.

Targeted support. A new FCA regime (6 Apr 2026) for suggestions to groups of similar consumers.

Three lines of defence. Business, then Risk and Compliance, then Internal Audit.

Tracking error. How much a portfolio's return varies from its benchmark.

UCITS. A regulated fund standard for retail investors in the UK and EU.

UK MAR. UK Market Abuse Regulation: bans insider dealing, unlawful disclosure and manipulation.

Value for Money framework. Planned rating of DC default funds on performance, cost and service; first assessments 2028.

Work simulation. A Cappfinity assessment that puts you in realistic tasks and asks you to rank responses.
