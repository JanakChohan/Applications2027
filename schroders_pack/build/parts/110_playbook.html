Part 11

The scenario playbook

A method for answering any "what would you do" question and holding your frame when pushed, then three scenarios for every line of the JD, five hard composure scenarios with decision trees, and a CV grill drill.

11.1 The four-step protocol: steady, sort, act, close

StepWhat you doPhrases that execute it
SteadyBreathe out. Restate the core of the problem so they know you heard it."So the situation is..." / "Let me make sure I've got this: ..." / "The key issue seems to be..."
SortName what collides. Apply the priority order."Two things matter here: X and Y." / "The rule comes first, then the client, then speed." / "What I don't know yet is..."
ActSay what you would do, in order, and who you would tell."First I'd..., then I'd..., and I'd tell [owner] by [time]." / "I'd put it in writing so there's a record."
CloseState the principle and the outcome. Stop talking."That way the client gets it fast and gets it right." / "The principle is I tell people early."

11.2 The priority order when good things collide

Why this order in a Client Group seat:

  • Rules first. They carry personal and firm-wide consequences: financial promotions, inside information and data [S401, S402, S403].
  • The client's real interest second. For retail outcomes that is itself a rule under Consumer Duty [S161], and trust is what an active manager sells.
  • Fairness and the record third. Client Group is a shared hub, so queue-jumping by rank breaks it.
  • Speed last. Speed is what you flex [Inferred].

11.3 Eight ways assessors break a frame, and the counter to each

Staying calm physically

  • Breathe out first. A long exhale (four seconds) before you speak slows the heart rate more than a deep breath in.
  • Feet flat, hands still and visible. On video, rest forearms on the desk.
  • Slow the first sentence on purpose. The rest follows its pace.
  • Hold a pause. "Let me think about that for a second" is always acceptable. Three seconds of silence feels long to you and normal to them.
  • Anchor on paper. Keep a card with the four protocol words and the one-sentence frame.

General composure practice [Inferred]; not Schroders guidance.

11.5 The composure set: five hard scenarios with decision trees

These five come up in some form at almost every client-facing assessment centre. Learn the trees well enough to draw them on a napkin.

1. Two senior people want the same scarce resource

Say this

"I wouldn't pick between them myself. I'd put both requests in writing, with the client, the stage and the deadline, to whoever owns the fund manager's diary, and tell both of them they'll hear within the hour. If there's a way to split the slot or offer a specialist instead, I'd put that in too."

2. A senior person asks you to skip a control

Say this

"I'd want to help, so I'd chase the approval straight away and tell them when it'll clear. I wouldn't send it before then, because anything a client sees has to be fair, clear, not misleading and approved. If it can't be done in time, I'd tell my manager and suggest a holding message to the client."

3. You hear something that might be confidential or inside information

Say this

"I wouldn't repeat it, act on it or forward it. I'd note what I heard, when and from whom, and tell Compliance or my manager the same day. Inside information is 'information of a precise nature which has not been made public' that could move a price. I don't have to decide whether it is. I just have to hand it to the people who do."

4. You made a mistake

Say this

"I'd tell the person who owns it straight away, with the corrected figure and its source, and let them decide how to handle the client. Then I'd log it and change my checking so it doesn't happen again. The worst version of a mistake is the one found later."

5. Incomplete information against a deadline

Say this

"It depends who it's for. If it's going to a client, only verified, approved figures go out, so I'd ask for more time or send it with the gap clearly stated. If it's internal, I'd send what I have on time, with my assumptions in the first line."

11.6 The CV grill

How a three-level attack works.

  • Level one asks what you did. "Tell me about your society role."
  • Level two asks how, with numbers. "How many members? What did you change? How do you know it worked?"
  • Level three asks why, or what else. "Why that approach and not another? What would you do differently? What did someone else do that you didn't?"

Most candidates prepare level one. Assessors score levels two and three.

AttackFrame-holding answer shape
1. "That sounds like a team achievement. What did you do?"Name your two specific actions and one decision only you made. "The team ran it; my part was X and Y, and I decided Z."
2. "Those numbers sound small."Agree with the scale, defend the rate. "It was 40 people; attendance went from 15 to 40 in a term, which is the part I'm proud of."
3. "Why did you leave that role / stop that activity?"Honest reason, forward-looking. No criticism of others.
4. "This bullet has nothing to do with finance."Bridge the skill, not the subject. "It's where I learned to handle unhappy customers, which is half of client service."
5. "What went wrong?"A real failure, your share of it, the fix, the evidence you changed.
6. "What would your manager there say about you?"One strength and one development point, both specific and both true.
7. "You say you're interested in markets. What did the Bank of England do last month?"Know it. For September 2026: held at 3.75%, six to three, three votes to raise; next decision 5 November [S232].
8. "Your grades dipped here."One sentence of context, no excuses, then what you did about it and the result since.
The four-line drill for every CV bullet
  1. What: one sentence, with a number.
  2. How: the two actions you personally took.
  3. Why: why that approach, and one alternative you rejected.
  4. So what for Schroders: the JD skill it proves, quoted.

Write these four lines under each bullet on your CV. If you can't write line 3, that bullet will break at level three.
