Part 3

Why Schroders is built this way

The mechanism behind the structure: why one central Client Group, how teams actually work together, what clients are really buying, and how big and how wired the centre is.

3.1 Why the business is structured this way

Schroders runs three businesses: Public Markets (£533.1bn at end-2025), Schroders Capital, its private markets arm (£72.6bn), and Wealth Management (£123.9bn). Joint ventures add £94.1bn [S71]. A single Client Group sells across them, and a corporate centre (technology, operations, finance, risk and compliance, people, legal, audit, tax) supports all three [S72].

The theory: five reasons for one central Client Group

This argument is the author's reading of the firm's own words [Inferred]. It is not stated in one place.

  1. Concentrate support on a few propositions. The nine "leading capabilities" produce more than 85% of Public Markets revenue [S301]. More than 16% of the fund range is being merged or closed [S71]. A central team can decide where marketing money and sales time go. Sales teams inside each business would each push their own long tail. The JD calls this area Value Proposition.
  2. One client, many capabilities. "By sharing insights and expertise across public and private market capabilities globally, we are able to deliver bespoke solutions" [S72]. A pension scheme may want fiduciary management, private credit and global equities at once. One relationship owner stops three business lines calling the same chief investment officer. The Mercer LTAF and the Apollo partnership are cross-capability products [S71, S96].
  3. Global centre, local execution. "We engage with clients through specialist teams based in regional hubs around the world ... delivering dedicated support through Client Service Hubs" [S72]. The centre designs; the regions sell. The JD calls this Sales Activation.
  4. Scale in the plumbing. The CRM, data and marketing technology are shared. The sales enablement team sits "in a virtual network with Distribution & Marketing, Technology, Finance, Product Management" and drives "consistent adoption of CRM (Salesforce) across Distribution teams" [S317]. The JD calls this Delivery.
  5. Cost. The 2025 strategy named "focused sales and marketing" and removing "duplication and overlapping processes" as sources of the £150m saving [S301]. Adjusted cost:income fell from 75% to 68% [S71, S70].

What the structure costs

  • Distance from complex products. Schroders made an exception for private markets. It built a dedicated Schroders Capital team of "40 sales specialists" [S71]. That concedes a generalist salesforce is not enough for complex products. Whether that team reports into Client Group is not confirmed.
  • Wealth runs its own client model. Cazenove's advisers own their client relationships [S72] [Inferred].
  • Disruption. Job cuts, market exits and fund closures mean some clients lose products and some staff lose roles [S88, S71].
  • A single point of failure. If the centre picks the wrong propositions, every region sells the wrong thing [Inferred].

Why combine asset management, private markets and wealth

The margin table in Part 2 is the answer in numbers. Private markets earn about 57bp and Cazenove about 48bp, against 6bp in core solutions [S71]. Wealth adjusted operating profit grew at a compound 15% a year for three years to £198.3m [S71]. Schroders Capital's strategy is to "enhance access to the Wealth segment" with evergreen and semi-liquid funds [S301]. So wealth gives stable, high-margin revenue and a captive route to market for private assets, while public markets gives scale [Inferred].

Honest caveat

Wealth net new business was 2.7% in 2025, against a 5 to 7% target [S71]. Selling Benchmark narrows Wealth to Cazenove in the UK and Schroders Wealth Management internationally [S70]. The combination works best at the high-net-worth end, not through adviser platforms.

3.3 What clients actually buy

Who they are (end-2025, by AUM) [S72]:

  • By channel: institutional 64%, intermediary 19%, wealth 17%.
  • By region: UK 47%, Asia Pacific 25%, Europe, Middle East and Africa 16%, Americas 12%.
  • By asset class: equities 31%, wealth 17%, core solutions 16%, multi-asset 14%, fixed income 12%, private markets 10%.

What they are buying.

  1. Active returns in chosen areas. "We are unashamed advocates of the power of active management to address our clients' complex needs" [S301].
  2. Solutions. Core solutions took £8.1bn of net inflows in 2025, "underpinned by continued success in partnering with pension clients on outsourced CIO and fiduciary management solutions" [S71]. This business was scaled by buying River & Mercantile's solutions arm [S106].
  3. Access to private markets. Schroders Capital raised £10.9bn in 2025 and £5.5bn in H1 2026 [S71, S70].
  4. Sustainability tools and stewardship. Proprietary tools such as SustainEx and CONTEXT; £42.9bn in sustainability-focused portfolios; votes at 7,042 companies. The £3.3bn PGGM mandate was won "leveraging our proprietary research platform, including SustainEx" [S72].
  5. Research and insight. "More than 11,000 company research pieces a year" [S72].
  6. Brand and trust. Ranked fifth of 2,770 asset managers in NMG Consulting's brand study and the top non-US firm, for seven years running [S72].

Why they accept the fees: Schroders' evidence. 71% of assets beat their comparator over three years at end-2025, up from 58% a year earlier. The figure was 71% at H1 2026 [S71, S70]. Read the method: the figures are internal, mostly before fees, and exclude £86.9bn of assets. Only 70% of the measured assets are compared with a benchmark; the rest are compared with peers, absolute targets or cash [S71].

The counter-case, with numbers.

  • Morningstar's European Active/Passive Barometer (2024 data): 29.1% of active equity funds survived and beat passive peers over one year, and 14.2% over ten years. Bond managers did better: 53.5% and 26.0% [S319].
  • S&P's SPIVA reportedly found 88% of broad UK equity funds underperformed in 2025 [S321] [Reported], search summary only.

What follows. Schroders' case is strongest where active has an edge: credit, emerging markets, smaller companies, private markets and solutions. It is weakest in large developed equity markets. That matches the mix the firm is steering towards [Inferred].

Your opinion goes here

Is active worth paying for? For: dispersion and concentration are high, and a third of a US index tracker is a handful of tech stocks [S241]. Schroders' own survey finds institutions adding active to reduce concentration [S244] [Reported]. Against: the ten-year base rate for active equity is poor [S319]. Model view: "Active is worth paying for where the index is a poor answer to the client's problem, which is why solutions, credit and private markets matter more to Schroders than a UK large-cap fund."

Against peers, Schroders runs about £150m of assets per employee (estimate), more than wealth-heavy firms and far less than passive giants (Part 4). Part of the gap is the product mix. Active and wealth businesses need more people per pound managed than index funds do [Inferred].

How the centre is wired to the front line. Postings show four kinds of connection:

  • Embedded roles. Product managers attached to strategies [S4].
  • Connective roles. Relationship managers, sales support specialists and the consultant database team, "coordinating distribution through the relevant sales representative" [S7, S9].
  • Utility roles. The Salesforce platform, marketing hubs and Business Development Services [S5, S10].
  • Control roles. Distribution Control in Luxembourg does "initial and ongoing AML due diligence on intermediary's business partners" [S11].
