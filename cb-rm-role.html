Start from zero: what a bank actually does for a business

Forget banking for a second. Picture a company — say, a furniture maker in Manchester with 80 staff. It has money problems that have nothing to do with being badly run; they're just what running a business is:

  • It pays for wood from Poland today, but its customers pay for the finished furniture 90 days later. For three months, cash is going out faster than it comes in.
  • It wants a new £2 million cutting machine but doesn't have £2 million sitting around.
  • It sells to a buyer in Dubai it has never met. Ship the furniture first and hope to be paid? Or demand payment first and lose the sale?
  • It earns dollars from American customers but pays its staff in pounds. If the dollar weakens, its profit shrinks through no fault of its own.
  • Every week it needs to pay 200 suppliers, in several currencies, without mistakes.

A commercial bank is the business that solves exactly these problems, for a fee. Each problem has a named product:

OFFICIAL HSBC's own one-line version of this: "We provide expert advice and essential services dependent on business needs… trade finance, global payments, multi-currency accounts, commercial cards and insurance, alongside many others." [1] Now you can read that sentence and see the five problems hiding behind it.

Who the clients are: OFFICIAL "businesses of all sizes… from small enterprises to large multinationals." [1] In practice that's a ladder — a family-run firm with £2m of sales, a mid-market company with £50m, a corporate with £500m operating in ten countries. The job is the same at every rung; what changes is complexity: more countries, more currencies, more products, bigger loans, more people involved in every decision. INFERRED

What a Relationship Manager actually is

Here is the key idea, and it's worth slowing down for.

The bank has all those products, each run by deep specialists — trade-finance experts, payments experts, foreign-exchange desks, lending teams. But the furniture maker's finance director does not want seven phone numbers. They want one person at the bank who knows their business — someone who understands that the Dubai order is why they suddenly need trade finance and currency hedging and a bigger overdraft, because those three needs are one story.

That one person is the Relationship Manager (RM).

INFERRED The best everyday analogy: an RM is like a general practitioner (GP) doctor. The GP doesn't perform surgery — but they know your history, spot what you need, refer you to the right specialist, and stay responsible for you across every visit. The specialists change; the GP is constant. Swap "patient" for "business client" and "surgeons" for "product specialists" and you have the job:

OFFICIAL Match this against the posting's own words: "you'll work with Relationship Managers and specialist teams… help build an understanding of each client's business, goals and challenges, and see how HSBC brings together the right expertise to respond." [1] And the guardians at the bottom are there too: "you'll also work with colleagues across Legal, Credit and Risk." [1]

Why this role exists commercially INFERRED: the specialists each see one product; the RM sees the whole client, so the RM is the one who notices the Dubai expansion means three new products, not one. Banks call this cross-selling; clients, when it's done well, call it being understood. It's also the moat — a competitor can match a loan price in an afternoon, but not ten years of earned trust.

The job description, decoded line by line

The "What you'll do" paragraph is dense. Here is each phrase in plain English, with what you'd physically be doing at your desk — the quoted phrases are OFFICIAL, the decode is INFERRED:

  • "Support client presentations, reports" — building PowerPoint decks and Word documents for client meetings: a page on how the client's industry is moving, a summary of their accounts with the bank, a proposal for what HSBC could do next. You draft; your RM edits; the client sees the polished version.
  • "…and credit papers" — the big one; it gets its own section below.
  • "Help with strategic reviews" — once a year (or when something changes) the team steps back and asks: what is this client trying to become in five years, and what will they need from a bank to get there? You'd pull the client's published accounts, news about them, industry data, and help turn it into that forward-looking view.
  • "…and pitches" — the same work, aimed at companies that aren't HSBC clients yet: researching a target company and helping build the argument for why they should switch banks.
  • "Carry out financial analysis and risk assessments on portfolios and transactions" — a portfolio just means "all the clients this team looks after." You'd work in Excel on their numbers: are revenues growing, is debt rising, can each company comfortably pay its interest, which clients are drifting toward trouble. "Transactions" means doing that same analysis for one specific proposed deal.
  • "Work with colleagues across Legal, Credit and Risk" — shepherding your team's proposals through the bank's internal checks, and learning why those checks exist.

The credit paper — the document the whole job revolves around

This phrase will mean nothing to you now and everything later, so let's define it properly.

INFERRED — standard banking practice, consistent with the JD A credit paper is the written argument for why the bank should lend money to a particular client. When the furniture maker asks for £2 million, the RM's team cannot just say yes — the bank's money is at risk. So the team writes a structured document that typically covers:

  1. The business — what the company does, who runs it, who its customers are.
  2. The numbers — revenues, profits, existing debts, and above all cash flow (the actual money moving in and out — because loans are repaid with cash, not with accounting profits).
  3. The ask — how much, for how long, for what purpose.
  4. Repayment — exactly where the money to pay the bank back will come from, and what happens if the plan goes wrong.
  5. Security (also called collateral) — what the bank can claim if the client can't pay (property, machinery, invoices owed to the client).
  6. The risks, stated honestly — what could sink this (losing a big customer, raw-material costs, the economy) and why the bank should still be comfortable.
  7. The recommendation — lend / don't lend / lend with conditions.

That paper then goes to the Credit team — professional sceptics whose entire job is to challenge it. They don't know the client and don't want to; their independence is the point. The RM team argues for the client; Credit stress-tests the argument; the tension between them is how the bank makes good loans.

The balancing act the whole career rests on

OFFICIAL Buried in the "What you'll learn" paragraph is the most important sentence in the posting: you'll learn "how client needs, profitability and risk are balanced in real situations." [1] That triangle is relationship management:

Decode the corners INFERRED:

  • Client needs — what actually helps the business. Sometimes that's a product the bank earns little on; sometimes it's advising them not to borrow.
  • Profitability — the bank earns the gap between what it pays for money and what it charges for it (the margin), plus fees. Every client relationship has a cost (people's time, capital set aside against the loans), so each relationship must earn more than it costs.
  • Risk — mostly credit risk: the chance the client can't pay the money back. A loan that defaults can wipe out the profit from fifty good loans, which is why the Credit team gets a veto and why the posting mentions Risk colleagues twice.

The reason relationship banking is a career rather than a sales job: the triangle only balances over years. Trust lets the RM say "no, and here's why" without losing the client — and a client who's heard an honest no believes the next yes.

What you'd actually do day to day

The posting gives the ingredients; here's the realistic week they cook into. INFERRED — built from the JD's own task list plus intern accounts; the shape is reliable, the proportions vary by team

  • Morning: scan news on the portfolio's clients and their industries. A client in the news (won a contract, lost a CEO, hit by tariffs) changes what the team does that day.
  • Most hours, honestly: Excel and PowerPoint. Updating portfolio spreadsheets, spreading client accounts (banker-speak for typing a company's financial statements into the bank's analysis format), drafting slides for reviews and pitches, assembling inputs for credit papers. This is not glamorous and it is exactly where you learn how businesses work from the inside.
  • Meetings you shadow: client calls and visits with your RM (mostly listening, taking notes, owning the follow-ups), internal huddles with product specialists, credit discussions where a paper is argued.
  • The long-running project: interns typically carry one substantial piece of work across the ten weeks — a portfolio review, a sector study, a prospect pitch — presented to the team at the end. CORROBORATED HSBC internship job descriptions describe exactly this project work and final assessments. [4]
  • ANECDOTAL A past HSBC corporate-banking intern's own summary of the role, which matches the JD almost word for word: "Creating and updating documents regarding the bank's client relations. Participated in client calls, researched clients and kept up to date with their news." [5]

What you would not do: lend anyone money, give a client advice, or sign anything. Ten-week interns (and first-year graduates) are the analytical engine behind the people who do. The value of the seat is proximity — you watch trust being built in real meetings, years before you're the one building it.

The skills — ranked, with the most important one named

You asked for the single most important skill. Here it is, then the supporting cast.

#1 — Turning mess into a clear, accurate document someone senior can rely on. INFERRED — my call, and I'd defend it Every task in the JD — credit papers, presentations, reports, analysis — is the same underlying act: take scattered raw material (accounts, news, a client conversation, a spreadsheet) and produce something short, correct and decision-ready. For the intern/graduate stage this beats charisma, beats maths, beats market knowledge. An RM who trusts your documents gives you client exposure; one who has to re-check your numbers doesn't. Note that accuracy is the trust currency inside the bank exactly as reliability is the trust currency with clients — same skill, two audiences.

How you're measured

Two different scoreboards — one for you as an intern/graduate, one for the RMs you'd be supporting. Knowing both makes you sound five years older in interviews.

Scoreboard 1: you. CORROBORATED HSBC internships are explicit conversion machines: the programme "is considered a conversion route to the Commercial Banking Graduate Programme" [1], and intern JDs describe "structured assessments aligned to securing a graduate role" — project work, presentations, interviews and skills-based evaluations across the ten weeks. [4] In practice that means you're assessed on: the quality and accuracy of what you produce (skill #1 again), how you behave against HSBC's four values (value difference, succeed together, take responsibility, get it done), the final project presentation, and — unofficially but decisively — whether the team would want you back at the desk next year. INFERRED On the graduate scheme the same logic runs for 24 months, ending in a permanent client-facing seat. [2]

Scoreboard 2: the RMs around you. INFERRED — standard industry practice; HSBC doesn't publish its scorecard A working RM is typically measured on:

  • Portfolio income — total revenue from their clients across all products (so the payments and FX referrals count, not just loans — this is why the quarterback model is also the incentive model).
  • Credit quality — do their clients repay? A book of loans going bad erases years of revenue; prudence is paid for.
  • Client growth and retention — new clients won, existing ones kept and deepened.
  • Risk and compliance hygiene — client files current, KYC (know your customer — the identity and legitimacy checks regulators require) up to date, no corners cut. At HSBC, which operates under the memory of its 2012 money-laundering settlement, this is a first-class metric, not paperwork. [7]

Notice what the two scoreboards share: nothing on either rewards flash. Both pay reliability, accuracy and trust — compounding assets, which is rather the theme of this bank.

Glossary — every term in one place

  • Commercial banking — the part of a bank that serves businesses (not individuals, not giant institutional investors).
  • Relationship Manager (RM) — the single named banker who owns a client relationship and coordinates all the bank's specialists for them.
  • Portfolio — all the clients (or all the loans) one team or banker looks after.
  • Coverage — banker-speak for the client-facing, relationship-owning side of the bank (as opposed to product teams). The graduate scheme's "coverage placement" means sitting with RM teams.
  • Credit — lending money; also the name of the independent team that approves or rejects lending.
  • Credit paper — the written case for a specific loan: the business, the numbers, the repayment plan, the risks, the recommendation.
  • Cash flow — actual money moving in and out of a business; loans are repaid from this, not from paper profits.
  • Collateral / security — assets the bank can claim if a loan isn't repaid.
  • Working capital — the money tied up in running a business day to day (stock bought, invoices not yet paid); working-capital finance bridges that gap.
  • Trade finance — bank products that let strangers trade internationally by having the bank guarantee payment and delivery.
  • Foreign exchange (FX) — converting one currency to another; hedging means locking an exchange rate now to remove uncertainty later.
  • Margin — the gap between the bank's cost of money and what it charges — the core of lending profit.
  • Default — a borrower failing to repay.
  • KYC (know your customer) — the regulator-required checks that a client is who they claim to be and their money is legitimate.
  • Pitch / prospect — a presentation to win a company that isn't yet a client / that target company.
  • 2:1 — UK degree classification, upper second class; HSBC's minimum everywhere.
  • Penultimate year — second-to-last year of your degree. The internship's eligibility gate, and the reason this particular posting isn't yours.
