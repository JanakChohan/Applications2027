0.2 The 10-minute emergency version

What Millennium is (three sentences)

Millennium is a private multi-manager hedge fund founded in 1989 by Izzy Englander, managing about $92bn (its own website, September 2026) with 6,700 to 6,900 staff and over 350 independent investment teams [S71][S70]. It allocates investor capital to those teams under hard loss limits (reported: lose 5%, capital halved; 7.5%, out [S104][S113]), passes its costs through to investors, and sells them a steady return that barely moves with markets: about 14% a year with one losing year since 1989 [S111][S115]. In November 2025 it sold its first outside stake, 15% for about $2bn at a $14bn valuation, and in 2026 it is raising a further $10 to 20bn while Citadel returns capital [S82][S221].

Why this role exists (two sentences)

Hundreds of independent PM teams all want time with company management, and since 2014 in the UK and 2018 across the EU that time has been a priced, rationed, compliance-controlled service rather than a free perk of trading with a bank [S180][S185]. A central desk that pools demand, works both the broker channel and the direct-to-company channel, allocates scarce slots, and proves with data which access was worth its cost is cheaper and better than 350 pods fending for themselves.

Five talking points

  1. Two channels. "Sell-side broker sourced" and "Millennium originated" are two supply lines. The Services desk centralises the broker channel; a sectorised Origination team goes direct to IR teams [S42].
  2. Internal clients. The desk markets access to PMs, allocates contested slots, and reports consumption. The consumption report feeds a semi-annual broker vote [S43].
  3. Compliance is the bright line. Meetings are logged because MAR treats analysts as "not the public" and private issuer calls as "dissemination" [S21][S22]. Einhorn was fined £7.3m over one broker-arranged call [S19].
  4. Regulation, precisely. FCA banned paying for access with commission on 2 June 2014; MiFID II unbundled research on 3 January 2018; the FCA re-allowed bundled research payments from 1 August 2024 and for funds from 9 May 2025, but ruled corporate access "out of scope" [S180][S181][S188]. The EU followed from 6 June 2026.
  5. Right now. Millennium is up 8.2% to August against an S&P 500 up about 19%; that is the low-volatility product working as designed, and it makes the fee debate louder [S220][S249]. London equities are being built out, which is your client base growing [S231].

Three questions to ask

"Roughly what share of EMEA meetings is broker-sourced versus originated, and is that shifting?" "When several teams want the same 1x1, is there a written allocation rule?" "What does a strong Business Services intern look like by week ten, and what are the next steps?"

Three things not to say

Do not quote a precise AUM, fee or expense ratio as fact. Do not say "edge" next to "management meeting". Do not imply you plan to move to the investing side.

The role, explained from zero

What the job description actually says, what a corporate access team does all day, and a reconstruction of the team you would be joining.

1.1 First, the two words in the job title

Key term: corporate access

Arranging contact between investors and the people who run listed companies. The UK regulator's legal definition is "a service of arranging or bringing about contact between an investment manager and an issuer or potential issuer" [S2] Confirmed. An issuer is a company that has issued shares. The contact can be a private 30-minute meeting, a group lunch, a slot at a bank conference, a factory visit, or a video call.

Key term: buy side and sell side

The buy side is anyone who invests money: hedge funds, pension funds, asset managers. Millennium is buy side. The sell side is the investment banks and brokers who sell them services: trading, research, and access to company management. A "broker" in this guide always means a sell-side firm.

Three parties want this contact. Portfolio managers (PMs) and analysts want time with management to inform investment decisions. Banks arrange it because it pleases both their corporate clients and their investor clients. Company Investor Relations (IR) teams manage it because it is how the company tells its story to the people who own it [S1]. The person in the corporate access seat sits in the middle of all three.

1.3 The two channels: broker-sourced versus Millennium-originated

Channel A: consuming what the bank arranged

A bank's corporate access desk builds an event: a sector conference with 30 to 300 presenting companies, a non-deal roadshow (NDR: management tours investors' offices when no money is being raised), or a post-results tour. It then markets slots to its buy-side clients. Millennium is one of many asking. An academic study of over 60,000 NDRs from 2013 to 2018 found about 65% were broker-sponsored, and that the sponsoring bank's commission revenue "increases substantially" in the roadshow week [S38] Confirmed. That is the bank's motive: access buys trading flow and goodwill.

The catch: banks ration their scarcest slots to the clients they value most, and since 2014 in the UK (and 2018 across the EU) corporate access has been a priced, separately paid service rather than something quietly bundled into trading commissions [S2][S13]. Part 5 covers that history. The practical result is that a buy-side firm cannot simply assume it will get the CFO slot it wants.

Channel B: going direct

The alternative is to contact the company's IR team yourself and host the meeting at Berkeley Street or on video. This is "origination". At Millennium it is run by a sectorised Sector Specialist Origination team; a London intern posting for that team asks candidates to "centralize and execute originated corporate access activity, including in-house meetings, company visits, conferences and conference calls" [S47] Confirmed. The Corporate Access Services desk (your team) handles the broker channel and the shared machinery for both.

Why build origination at all? Four reasons the sources support:

  • It is what companies prefer. An IR Magazine survey found nearly three-quarters of buy-siders are more likely to take a meeting arranged by direct contact, and 84% of IR officers rate direct access eight or more out of ten [S4] Confirmed. The FCA's own 2019 review noted "a general increase in the proportion of corporate access meetings arranged directly between an issuer and asset manager without broker intermediation" [S16].
  • Control. AllianceBernstein's head of corporate access: "we have a no broker policy, so brokers aren't in the meetings, and that's by design" [S8].
  • Cost. Paying a bank to intermediate a meeting the company would happily take directly is wasted budget once access is a priced service [S2][S13].
  • Reach. Brokers have withdrawn from small and mid caps. A 2025 German and Austrian IR survey found 78% of smaller companies saw reduced sell-side coverage after MiFID II and 56% said investor access got harder, while 83% of large caps reported more direct investor contact [S34] Confirmed. Origination matters most exactly where brokers have left.

What origination requires: relationships with IR teams, credibility (a hedge fund has to overcome what IR Magazine in 2016 called "a negative perception among corporate leadership" [S9]), scale (a large fund has "the clout to get the attention of many large caps" [S9]), and a reason for the company to say yes: well-prepared PMs who ask good questions, and a feedback loop.

Your opinion goes here

Which channel is harder and which is more valuable? The author's view: broker-sourced gives volume cheaply; originated gives control and reach but costs relationship time. The right mix depends on market cap and region. Decide whether you agree, and be ready to say why a London desk covering European mid-caps might lean more on origination than a New York desk covering US large caps.

1.5 Conference allocation: the judgement problem hiding in the JD

Every conference has more demand than supply for the best companies. The bank allocates its slots by "conference economics", meaning commission relationships and fit between investor and company [S30]. IR advisers tell companies to give the bank a "must see" list and "approve priority accounts first; hold remaining slots for the last 48 to 72 hours of booking" [S30]. So Millennium competes externally for slots, then must allocate internally.

Inside a pod shop this is harder than at a long-only manager. Pods are independent. Two pods can be on opposite sides of the same stock. Neither will share notes. A multi-manager corporate access head told an IR adviser that the fund receives "1,000+ headquarters meeting invitations annually" and that analysts engage "through carefully coordinated quarterly plans rather than ad hoc conference attendance" [S10] Confirmed. The desk's job is to turn hundreds of raw requests into one ranked list the bank can act on, then to allocate fairly and explain the outcome.

If asked how you would allocate a contested slot

Give a rule, not a feeling. Candidate rules: position size or relevance to the pod's book; who requested first; who has not met the company recently; whether a group format can serve several pods; escalate genuinely contested cases to the desk head. Then say you would record the decision so the consumption report shows who got what and why.

1.6 Consumption data, the broker vote, and why the numbers matter

Key term: broker vote

A periodic scorecard in which a buy-side firm's investors rate each broker on the services they received: research, analyst calls, conferences and, above all, company meetings. The scores drive how much the firm pays or values each broker. A vendor whitepaper citing a Greenwich Associates survey says the most tracked services are "one-on-one company meetings (82% of respondents), analyst visits (68%), and invitations to conferences (58%)" [S39] Reported.

Consumption data is the raw material for that vote. If the desk does not log which PM attended which meeting via which broker, the vote is guesswork. Millennium's Tokyo Associate posting says the role will "manage the semi-annual Corporate Access Broker Vote process, including collecting feedback on broker access quality, meeting quality, and overall effectiveness" [S43]. The consumption report in your JD is the London side of the same machine. It also serves four other purposes:

  • Resource allocation. Which conferences are worth sending people to, and which brokers deliver.
  • Justifying spend. Corporate access is a cost passed through to investors (Part 2). The desk has to show it earns its keep.
  • Spotting under-served PMs. A pod that never requests meetings may not know the service exists. The Tokyo posting includes onboarding new investment professionals to "Millennium's corporate access offering, process, and tools" [S43].
  • Compliance evidence. Regulators expect records of who met whom and when (see 1.8).

1.7 Tools, and what the proprietary portal has to do

The vendor market exists: early aggregators (ingage, WeConvene, A2 Access, later bought by Dealogic) let brokers upload event calendars tagged by sector, geography and event type [S31][S32]; Bloomberg added corporate access functions to the Terminal [S33]; buy-side workflow tools now offer one calendar across "10 to 30 sell-side partners", ranked requests and notes routed into a CRM [S28] Reported. Millennium chose to build. Inferred from the JD and peer descriptions, its portal must:

  1. Hold a forward calendar of broker events and originated events, tagged by sector, region and ticker.
  2. Capture requests with the pod's identity and priority.
  3. Record the allocation: who got which slot.
  4. Log attendance and consumption by PM, broker, company and format.
  5. Carry compliance fields: restricted-list check, chaperone, note retention.
  6. Produce reports for the broker vote and PM strategy sessions.

Your Excel work is items 4 to 6.

1.8 The compliance dimension: the bright line

Corporate access sits close to a line it must never cross. A management meeting is valuable because it gives colour, tone and detail. It becomes illegal the moment it delivers inside information. Understanding this unprompted is a strong differentiator for an intern candidate.

Key terms

MNPI, material non-public information: facts not yet public that a reasonable investor would consider important to the price. UK MAR, the UK Market Abuse Regulation: the law that defines inside information (Article 7), bans insider dealing (Article 8) and unlawful disclosure (Article 10), and applies to anyone who receives such information, including a PM in a meeting [S21][S22]. Regulation FD (US, 2000): if a US company tells MNPI to investors or analysts privately, it must publish it, simultaneously if intentional and within 24 hours if not [S17] Confirmed. Reg FD binds the company; the fund's exposure is insider-trading law.

Three regulator statements shape how a UK desk behaves. The FCA says analysts "are not 'the public'" for MAR purposes and that it has opened "numerous enquiries into suspected unlawful disclosures by issuers' senior executives in analyst briefings" [S21]. It says "communications between issuers and shareholders in private forums or channels is considered dissemination for the purposes of MAR", recommends companies avoid private calls during closed periods, and cites the 2022 £80,000 penalty on the chairman of ConvaTec for unlawful disclosure [S22] Confirmed. And its 2015 review of asset managers praised a firm whose fund managers avoided meetings with investee companies during close periods, and criticised firms whose managers thought it was the company's job to judge what was inside information [S20].

The case every corporate access person knows

In 2012 the UK regulator fined David Einhorn and Greenlight Capital a combined £7.29m over a June 2009 conference call, arranged by Punch Taverns' broker, in which the CEO signalled an imminent equity raise. Einhorn had declined to be "wall-crossed" (declined a confidentiality agreement), and Greenlight sold shares afterwards. The regulator rejected the argument that the discussion was only "at a conceptual level": "reasonable investors are expected to interpret comments made to them in an appropriate manner" [S19] Confirmed. Lesson for the desk: refusing to be wall-crossed is not a shield. Meetings that could carry inside information go through Compliance.

Practical controls a buy-side desk runs, inferred from these sources and from expert-network practice after the Galleon prosecutions: pre-clearance of meetings against a restricted list, attendee logs, chaperoning where needed, note retention, a designated first contact for "market soundings" (approaches about deals) so that a PM is not the gatekeeper [S20][S23][S24]. The FCA's Market Watch 75 explicitly recommends "Gatekeeper" arrangements [S23]. The desk's databases and reports are compliance artefacts as much as management tools.

Say this in the interview

"I understand this is a controlled function. A meeting log is not just for the broker vote; it is evidence of who received what information and when. If a PM came back from a meeting with a number that was not in the company's public releases, my job would be to flag it to Compliance, not forward it."

Don't say this

"Corporate access is about getting an edge from management." The word "edge" next to "management meeting" is what a compliance officer hears as insider trading. Say "colour", "context", "quality of decision".

1.11 EMEA specifics

  • Reporting rhythm. European full-year results cluster in February and March, first quarter in April and May, half-year in July and August, third quarter in October and November. Many mid-caps report only half-yearly. The weeks after results are the peak demand window for access Inferred.
  • Closed periods. Under MAR Article 19(11) senior managers may not deal in their own shares for 30 days before results. That restricts dealing, not meetings, but the FCA recommends companies avoid private investor calls in closed periods [S22], so blackout calendars shape when EMEA management is available.
  • UK corporate broking. Unique to the UK: a listed company retains a "corporate broker" (AIM companies must have a nominated adviser). The house broker runs the company's roadshow calendar and gathers post-meeting feedback for the board [S36][S37] Reported. To reach a UK plc, a desk usually works through both IR and the house broker, and PM conduct in the room gets reported back.
  • Time zones. EMEA spans UTC-1 to UTC+4. London serves PMs in Dubai and Paris, and US PMs who want European management in their morning.
  • Regimes. UK MAR, EU MAR, and the UK's payment-optionality rules (Part 5) differ from the EU's. A London desk lives in two rulebooks.

2.2 The shop analogy

@@f10@@
Figure 10. Millennium as a department store renting stalls to independent traders. The three places the analogy breaks are on the right. Basis: Millennium's own model description [S74][S77], reported risk rules [S104][S113], fee reports [S87]. Author's analogy, 4 Sep 2026.

The analogy, in words. Millennium is a department store. The customers walking in are pension funds, sovereign funds and endowments. They do not buy from any one stall; they buy the store's overall basket, a steady return with low volatility. The stalls are PM pods: each rents space, sells its own product (European industrials long/short, rates relative value, commodity spreads), keeps a share of its own takings, and is evicted if it loses more than a set amount. Store management is the central platform: landlord, security guard (risk), buyer (business development, which recruits new stalls), treasury (which borrows to stock the shelves), and the concierge desk, which is corporate access: it does not sell to customers, it makes the stalls better at their jobs. Customers pay the store's running costs plus a share of profits.

Where it breaks, in words. First, the stalls share one balance sheet, so one bad stall can hurt the whole store: this is netting risk. Second, the landlord lends the stalls money to hold far more stock than they paid for: leverage, which real landlords do not provide. Third, customers cannot choose stalls; they buy the whole store blind, and rely on management to keep the stalls honest. Saying all three unprompted is what turns a memorised line into understanding.

The hedge fund industry from zero

Foundations, the structural shifts of the last five years, who gives hedge funds money and why, and where the industry might go next. Nothing here assumes prior knowledge.

4.1 Foundations

Key term: hedge fund

The US regulator's own definition: "Hedge funds pool investors' money and invest the money in an effort to make a positive return. Hedge funds typically have more flexible investment strategies than, for example, mutual funds. Many hedge funds seek to profit in all kinds of markets by using leverage (in other words, borrowing to increase investment exposure as well as risk), short-selling and other speculative investment practices" [S151] Confirmed. Only institutions and wealthy individuals may invest.

The people

Limited partners (LPs) are the investors; their liability is limited to what they put in. The general partner (GP), or investment adviser, runs the money and takes the fees. Millennium Management is the GP; a pension fund is an LP [S151].

The size

AUM, assets under management, is the total capital run for clients. Providers measure it differently, and for private firms every figure is a press estimate that moves month to month [S150a].

The positions

Long means you own a share and profit if it rises. Short means you borrow a share, sell it, and profit if it falls. Net exposure is longs minus shorts: how much you move with the market. Gross exposure is longs plus shorts: how much capital is at work. Eighty percent long and twenty percent short is 100% gross and 60% net [S176b]. Market neutral keeps net exposure "typically -10% to 10%", which is why such funds "may be managed with gross exposures in excess of 400%" [S175] Confirmed.

The return

Alpha is return from skill; beta is return from simply being exposed to the market. Investors "are understandably reluctant to pay high fees for beta" [S175]. The Sharpe ratio is return above cash divided by volatility: return per unit of risk. A Sharpe of 2 is excellent. Multi-manager funds chase high Sharpe, not high raw return.

The risk controls

A drawdown is the fall from a peak to a trough. Platforms enforce "drawdown triggers" on each pod [S175]; press reports that Millennium PMs "face termination if their losses exceed 7.5%" [S160a] Reported. A high-water mark means performance fees are charged only on gains above the previous peak, so investors never pay twice for recovering a loss [S176d]. A hurdle is a minimum return before fees apply. Texas Teachers' Joel Hinkhouse: "If you are a hedge fund and return 5.25%, you have not made us alpha. So, you should not take a carry for that" [S167a].

The exit

Funds "typically limit opportunities to redeem" to monthly, quarterly or annually and "often impose a 'lock-up' period of one year or more" [S151]. A gate caps how much can leave per period. Millennium's newest fund has a two-year commitment and a five-year full exit [S173a].

The plumbing

A prime broker is a bank unit that lends the fund cash and securities (so it can lever up and short), holds its assets, and clears its trades [S176c]. This lending sits on the bank's balance sheet and consumes regulatory capital, so banks ration it. In the Bank of England's stress exercise, "Most banks reported that they would prioritise supporting their larger and preferred clients" [S154d] Confirmed. Big platforms run "central treasury desks" across "three to five plus" prime brokers [S175]. A fund administrator independently values the fund and keeps the investor register.

Key term: 2-and-20 versus pass-through

The classic fee is 2% of assets per year plus 20% of profits. Reality is lower: HFR's industry averages in early 2026 were 1.32% and 15.78% [S150d]. Under pass-through, investors instead pay the fund's actual operating costs, mostly PM pay, with no cap, plus a performance fee. Bloomberg's analysis of regulatory filings found pass-through expenses "typically range from 3% to 10% of net assets"; Point72 states there is "no limit" [S156][S178b] Reported. Investors "assume the 'netting risk' of underperforming PMs" [S175].

4.6 Sell side and buy side: the four flows between a bank and a hedge fund

The buy side "purchase[s] securities" (investment managers, pension funds, hedge funds). The sell side "issue[s], sell[s], or trade[s] securities" (investment banks, brokers) and "earn[s] their way through fees and commissions" [S176a]. A bank does four things for a fund like Millennium:

  1. Prime brokerage. Lends cash and securities, holds custody, clears trades. Earns financing spreads and stock-loan fees. The most important flow, because it is the source of leverage [S176c].
  2. Execution. Trades the fund's orders. Paid by commission or spread.
  3. Research. Analysts' reports and models. Since MiFID II in 2018 paid separately from execution; since the UK's PS24/9 in 2024 there is a "joint payments" option again under conditions [S179a]. Part 5.
  4. Corporate access. Meetings with company management. Under UK rules not research and not payable from client commission, so the fund pays from its own resources or values it in the broker vote. This is your seat.

An analyst on the sell side covers a sector and publishes for many clients, ranked by broker votes. An analyst on the buy side works for one pod, builds private models, and is paid on the pod's profit [S175][S176a].

If asked "why a hedge fund and not a bank?"

Structure: on the sell side, corporate access is a product sold to many clients. On the buy side it is procured, allocated and measured for internal clients who are paid on results. You are the client side of every broker relationship. At a multi-manager the allocation problem is hardest, because hundreds of independent teams compete for the same slot. Then add your own reason. 5.1 Era 1: bundled commissions

For decades, a fund manager paid a broker a commission per trade, out of the client's money, and the broker "gave back" research, analyst calls and company meetings. In the US this is "soft dollars", protected by a 1975 safe harbour. In the UK it was "dealing commission". Nobody priced the meeting; it came free with the trading. The regulator's objection was simple: clients paid for services they never saw, and managers chose brokers for the perks rather than for execution quality Inferred framing.

The UK moved first. A 2012 regulator review found "the majority of investment managers had inadequate controls and oversight when acquiring research goods and services from brokers" and, specifically on corporate access: "None of the investment managers we visited could justify to us how Corporate Access met the evidential criteria for research under our rules to allow them to pay for it with dealing commissions" [S180] Confirmed. The industry's own numbers explain why the regulator cared: a Thomson Reuters Extel survey around 2012 reportedly found the share of commission allocated to corporate access had nearly doubled in three years to 27% (snippet only, not relied on), and the regulator estimated roughly £500m a year of UK commission was spent on access [S3] Reported.

The 2014 rule that created your job

FCA Policy Statement PS14/7, May 2014, effective 2 June 2014. It inserted a legal definition of a "corporate access service" into the rulebook, added corporate access to the list of things that may not be bought with dealing commission, and told managers who attend bundled events like conferences to "disaggregate the discrete element of substantive research" from the access and pay only for the research part with commission [S180] Confirmed. Paying for access stayed legal, from the firm's own money: a manager "may decide to pay this fee from their own resources". From this date, corporate access in the UK is a distinct, separately paid product. That is the origin of buy-side corporate access desks as budget-owning functions.

5.2 Era 2: MiFID II unbundling (3 January 2018)

MiFID II extended the logic across the EU and to all research. "Firms receiving research were required to either pay for research themselves from their own resources (P&L model) or agree a separate research charge with their clients (research payment account, or RPA model)" [S181] Confirmed. In the UK the rules live in COBS 2.3A (inducements) and COBS 2.3B (research payments).

On corporate access, ESMA's 2017 Q&A is the key text: "ESMA does not consider that arranging meetings is research"; corporate access "should be considered a discrete service"; a firm "can also treat corporate access as a commercial service and pay for it appropriately from its own resources"; and a firm wishing to meet a company "has the option to approach them directly and/or pay for a third party corporate access service provider" [S185][S13] Confirmed. That last sentence is the regulatory licence for in-house buy-side desks.

What unbundling did

  • Budgets collapsed. The FCA's 2019 review of 40 firms found research budgets down "20% to 30%", about £70m saved for investors in the first half of 2018 alone and "nearly £1 billion over 5 years" [S191] Confirmed. A CFA Institute survey found 54% of sell-side respondents saw fewer analysts and around half saw reduced small and mid-cap coverage [S192].
  • Brain drain. Substantive Research estimates the sell side lost "7,500 years of net experience" between 2018 and 2021 [S205] Reported.
  • Access went direct. The same FCA review recorded "a general increase in the proportion of corporate access meetings arranged directly between an issuer and asset manager without broker intermediation" [S191]. Once a broker meeting had a price, companies and investors cut out the middleman.
  • Small caps suffered. In a 2025 German and Austrian survey, 78% of smaller companies saw reduced sell-side coverage and 56% said investor access got harder [S187]. The UK's partial fix in 2022: research and corporate access on companies under £200m market cap became "acceptable minor non-monetary benefits", meaning brokers can provide them free without breaching inducement rules [S190][S15] Confirmed.
  • US friction. US brokers had relied on SEC no-action relief to accept cash for research without being treated as investment advisers. The SEC let it lapse on 3 July 2023 [S193][S196]. European managers now report a "competitive disadvantage" versus US peers, including "the ability to meet with the corporates they need to meet with" [S207].

The candidate wants to defend opinions, not recite facts. This part lays out the arguments on both sides of the six questions most likely to come up, and a method for building a view you can hold under pressure.

7.1 The method: claim, evidence, limit

A defensible opinion in a 30-minute interview has three parts and takes 30 seconds. Claim: one sentence. Evidence: one or two sourced facts, with dates. Limit: the one thing that would change your mind. Interviewers push on the limit; if you name it first, the push becomes a conversation instead of a correction. Never claim more precision than the source has. "Press estimates put it at" beats a number stated as fact.

1. Claim"The pod model has peaked for mid-tier platforms, not for the top four."
2. EvidenceEisler closed after -14.3% driven by pass-through costs (Jan 2026); $39bn of $44.5bn Q1 2026 inflows went to $5bn+ firms.
3. Limit"If Millennium's 2026 ends below cash after fees, allocators' hurdle demands could bite even at the top."

Q1. Is the multi-manager model past its peak?

Peaked for the middle, not the top. The number of platforms that can afford the model has shrunk; the top four or five are consolidating the talent and the capital. Limit: a year in which the top platforms return less than cash after fees.

Q2. Is pass-through fair?

Fair

  • Capacity is scarce; waiting lists exist [S111]

Unfair

  • Investors bear netting risk: winning pods are paid while losers are not refunded [S175][S104]

  • Sixty-plus asset owners demand cash hurdles; most of Texas Teachers' managers agreed [S167a][S157]
Author's view

Fair while net returns beat what allocators can get elsewhere for the same volatility, and only then. Which means every cost centre, corporate access included, has to show its keep. Limit: a multi-year stretch below cash plus a margin.

No. The buy-side desk's value depends on access being priced and tracked explicitly. Limit: evidence that UK managers are losing meetings to US peers because of the rule, rather than because of budget choices.

No replacement; a rebalancing. Brokers keep conferences and large-cap volume; origination wins the bespoke, mid-cap and hard-to-reach meetings. The desk's skill is knowing which channel to use for which request. Limit: a collapse in bank conference economics.

Quality over count, which is exactly why the JD says "quantity and quality" and why consumption reports exist. Limit: a pod strategy where breadth of coverage genuinely is the edge.

Author's view

The constraint is coming, via banks' balance sheets and minimum repo margins rather than a leverage cap. The largest platforms with the deepest prime-broker relationships are best placed, which is another reason capital concentrates. Part 10

Glossary

Plain-English definitions of every term used in this guide. Read this part first if the jargon in any section slows you down.

Alpha
Return that comes from skill rather than from the market moving. If the market is up 10% and your fund is up 12% with the same risk, alpha is roughly the extra 2%.
Allocation (conference)
Deciding who gets scarce meeting slots: the bank allocates among its clients; the buy-side desk allocates among its PMs.
Allocator
An institution that decides where to invest large pools of money: a pension fund, endowment or sovereign wealth fund. Hedge funds' customers.
Analyst (buy side)
A researcher inside a fund who supports a PM by studying companies and building models.
Analyst (sell side)
A researcher at a bank who publishes reports on companies for the bank's investor clients.
AUM, assets under management
The total value of the money a firm manages for investors. The standard measure of size. Not the same as the firm's own revenue or profit.
Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of one percent. 50bp = 0.5%.
Beta
Return that comes from simply being exposed to the market. A fund with high beta rises and falls with the index.
Broker
In this guide, a sell-side firm (investment bank or brokerage) that executes trades and sells research and access.
Broker vote
A periodic scorecard in which a fund's investors rate each broker's services. Drives how much the fund pays or values each broker.
Buy side
Firms that invest money: hedge funds, asset managers, pension funds. Millennium is buy side.
Capital markets day
A company-hosted event where management presents strategy to all analysts and investors at once.
Closed period
Under UK and EU market abuse rules, the 30 days before results when senior managers may not deal in their company's shares. Companies also often avoid private investor meetings then.
Commission
The fee a fund pays a broker per trade. Historically it also quietly paid for research and access ("bundling").
Commission sharing arrangement (CSA)
A mechanism where part of trading commission is set aside to pay for research from any provider. Common between 2006 and 2018.
Consumption report
A record of who at the fund used which broker's services, especially company meetings. Feeds the broker vote.
Corporate access
Arranging contact between investors and the management of listed companies.
Corporate broker
UK-specific: a bank or broker a listed company retains to advise it and manage its relationship with shareholders. Runs the company's roadshow calendar.
Correlation
How much two things move together. Uncorrelated returns are the whole point of a multi-manager fund.
Crowding
Many funds holding the same positions. Dangerous because they all try to sell at once in a shock.
De-grossing
Cutting both long and short positions to reduce risk, usually fast and under pressure. When many pods do it at once, prices move sharply.
Drawdown
The fall from a peak to a trough in a fund's or PM's value. Millennium is reported to cut a PM's capital at a set drawdown (see Part 2).
EMEA
Europe, the Middle East and Africa. The region the London desk serves.
Expert network
A firm (GLG, AlphaSights, Third Bridge, Guidepoint) that connects investors with paid industry experts for calls. Adjacent to corporate access, tightly controlled by compliance.
FCA
Financial Conduct Authority, the UK regulator that authorises Millennium Capital Partners LLP and writes the rules on research payments and market abuse.
Form ADV
The registration document a US investment adviser files with the SEC. Public. The best primary source on a private fund manager's size and structure.
13F
A quarterly public filing listing a US manager's holdings of US-listed shares. Shows long positions only, with a delay.
Gross exposure
Longs plus shorts as a share of capital. A pod with 100 long and 100 short on 50 of capital has 400% gross exposure.
Guaranteed package
A signing deal that promises a PM a minimum payout regardless of performance. A feature of the talent war between platforms.
Hedge fund
A private investment fund, open only to institutions and wealthy individuals, that can go long and short, use leverage, and charges performance fees.
High-water mark
The rule that a fund only earns performance fees on gains above its previous peak. Stops it charging twice for recovering losses.
Hurdle rate
A minimum return a fund must beat before performance fees apply. Not all funds have one.
Inducement
Regulatory term for a benefit a broker gives a fund that could bias its behaviour. MiFID II restricts what counts as acceptable.
Inside information / MNPI
Precise, non-public, price-sensitive information. Trading on it is a crime. Corporate access must never be a channel for it.
Investor Relations (IR)
The team inside a listed company that communicates with shareholders and analysts. The desk's main external counterparty on the company side.
IP, investment professional
Millennium's term for PMs and analysts, the desk's internal clients.
Leverage
Borrowing, or using derivatives, to hold positions bigger than your capital. Amplifies both gains and losses.
Limited partner (LP) / general partner (GP)
LPs are the investors in a fund; the GP is the manager that runs it. Millennium Management is the GP; pension funds are LPs.
Lock-up
A period during which an investor cannot withdraw money. Millennium moved most investors to multi-year terms (Part 2).
Long / short
Long = own a share, profit if it rises. Short = borrow and sell a share, profit if it falls.
Long-only manager
An asset manager (BlackRock, Fidelity) that only buys, rarely shorts, and is judged against an index.
MAR, Market Abuse Regulation
The UK and EU law defining inside information, insider dealing and unlawful disclosure.
Market neutral
A portfolio built so that market moves roughly cancel out, leaving only the return from stock selection.
MiFID II
EU rules effective 3 January 2018 that, among much else, forced funds to pay for research and corporate access separately from trading.
Multi-manager / multi-strategy / pod shop / platform
A hedge fund made of many independent PM teams (pods) under one risk and capital framework. Millennium, Citadel, Point72, Balyasny.
Net exposure
Longs minus shorts. Near zero means market neutral.
Netting risk
The platform pays winning pods a share of their profit even in a year when losing pods drag the total down. The firm bears the gap.
Non-deal roadshow (NDR)
Management tours investor offices when no capital is being raised. Usually broker-organised.
Origination
The buy-side firm sourcing meetings directly from companies rather than through a broker.
Pass-through fees
A fee model where investors pay the fund's actual operating costs (pay, technology, offices) plus a performance fee, instead of a flat management fee.
Payment optionality
The UK's 2024 rule change letting fund managers once again pay for research bundled with trading commissions, subject to guardrails. See Part 5.
Performance fee
A share of profits (often 20%) paid to the manager.
Pod
A PM plus their analysts, running an independent book inside a platform.
Portfolio manager (PM)
The person who decides what a book buys and sells and is paid on its profit.
Prime broker
A bank that lends a hedge fund money and securities, holds its assets and clears its trades. The source of leverage.
Quiet period
A company's self-imposed silence before results, often two to four weeks. Wider than the legal closed period.
Regulation FD
US rule (2000) that a company must publish any material information it gives privately to investors or analysts.
Research payment account (RPA)
A client-funded pot, created under MiFID II, from which a fund pays for research.
Restricted list
Compliance's list of companies employees may not trade or discuss, often because the firm holds inside information.
Sell side
Investment banks and brokers who sell trading, research and access to investors.
Sharpe ratio
Return per unit of risk. A Sharpe of 2 is excellent; multi-manager funds target high Sharpe rather than high raw return.
Site visit / field trip
Investors visiting a company's plant, store or HQ.
Soft dollars
US term for paying for research with trading commissions rather than cash.
Unbundling
Separating payment for research and access from payment for trading. The core of MiFID II's research rules.
Wall-crossing
Formally telling an investor inside information (usually about a deal) after they agree to be restricted from trading. Refusing to be wall-crossed is not a defence if you receive the information anyway.
Webex
Cisco's video meeting software, used for your interview. Test it beforehand.
