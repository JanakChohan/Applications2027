Part 6

The role's own industry and its regulation

Consulting needs no licence. A Deloitte consultant is still boxed in by four sets of rules: audit independence, public procurement, data and AI law, and the financial-services rules that clients hire consultants to implement. This part gives each rule with its exact date, then the competitive map.

Why a lightly regulated industry still has rules that bite

Consulting itself has no statutory regulator and no licence to practise [Inferred from S180, S205]. Anyone can call themselves a management consultant. The voluntary standards come from the Management Consultancies Association (MCA), which runs the "Consulting Excellence" quality hallmark and the Chartered Management Consultant (ChMC) title, held by 2,800+ people in its 2026 report [S205, S150] [Reported].

A consultant at Deloitte faces a different reality, for one reason: Deloitte also audits. Audit is tightly regulated, and the rules that protect auditor independence reach into what the consulting side may sell. Add the government's own buying rules, data and AI law, and the regulation that drives bank and insurer spending, and you have the rulebook that shapes a graduate's day-to-day work.

Rule set 1: auditor independence

KEY TERM

Auditor independence: the principle that an auditor must be, and be seen to be, free of interests that could bias its opinion. Selling large consulting projects to a company you audit creates a self-review threat (you would audit your own work) and a financial interest in keeping the client happy. Public interest entity (PIE): broadly, a company with securities listed on a regulated market, a bank or an insurer.

The EU Audit Regulation (applies from 17 June 2016)

Regulation (EU) No 537/2014 was adopted on 16 April 2014 and applied from 17 June 2016 [S182] [Confirmed]. It did three things that still matter [S182, S180] [Confirmed]:

  • A 70% fee cap. Non-audit fees from a PIE audit client cannot exceed 70% of the average statutory audit fee over the previous three years.
  • A list of banned non-audit services (Article 5).
  • Mandatory audit firm rotation (Article 17): PIEs must change auditor after a set period.

The rules are retained in UK law after Brexit [Inferred; not separately verified]. The FRC's Revised Ethical Standard 2016 gave effect to them in the UK [S179, S180] [Confirmed].

FRC Revised Ethical Standard 2019 (issued 17 December 2019, in force 15 March 2020)

The FRC (Financial Reporting Council) is the UK audit regulator. Its 2019 standard was released on 17 December 2019 and took effect on 15 March 2020 [S180] [Confirmed]. Per Deloitte's own February 2020 briefing, the key changes were [S180, S181] [Confirmed]:

  • Blacklist to whitelist. Instead of listing what is banned, the FRC now lists what is allowed for UK PIE audit clients. Permitted services are largely those required by law and regulation, loan covenant reporting, other assurance closely linked to the audit, and reporting accountant work.
  • Bans for all audited entities (not only PIEs) on internal audit services, secondments of staff, and contingent fees.
  • Reach across the network. All services by the firm and its network to UK PIEs, their UK parents and their worldwide subsidiaries fall under the whitelist.
  • A new category, Other Entities of Public Interest (OEPI): large AIM companies, Lloyd's syndicates, the largest pension schemes, and the largest private companies (more than 2,000 employees, or turnover over £200m and balance sheet over £2bn). The whitelist applies to OEPIs for periods from 15 December 2020, but the 70% cap does not.
  • A tougher test: would an "objective, reasonable and informed third party" conclude that independence is compromised?

FRC Revised Ethical Standard 2024 (issued 15 January 2024, in force 15 December 2024)

The current rulebook. Published 15 January 2024, effective 15 December 2024. It simplified the 2019 text, aligned it with the international IESBA Code, and added a restriction on fees from entities related through a single controlling party. The FRC's Mark Babington: "The revised ethical standard has been simplified to ensure auditors are clear as to the high ethical standards expected" [S178] [Confirmed].

What this means on a Monday morning. Before a Deloitte consulting team can start work, the firm checks whether any Deloitte network firm audits that company or a related entity. If it is a PIE audit client, most consulting is simply not allowed [S180] [Inferred from Confirmed rules]. Deloitte UK has a whole internal team for this: Quality, Risk & Security Services, with sub-teams for Independence and conflict checks [S330] [Confirmed]. In practice, Deloitte's T&T business cannot sell an ERP implementation to a FTSE company Deloitte audits; that client's technology work goes to Accenture, PwC, EY or KPMG [S314] [Inferred]. Challengers such as Unity Advisory sell "no conflicts" as a feature for exactly this reason [S167] [Confirmed].

SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

"Because Deloitte audits, the FRC's Ethical Standard means Deloitte consulting can only sell a short whitelist of mostly audit-related services to UK public interest entities it audits, and even those are capped at 70% of the average audit fee. So a large share of the integrated, strategy-to-delivery work happens at clients audited by someone else, in the public sector and in private companies. I'd expect independence checks to be a routine part of starting any engagement."

Rule set 2: operational separation of audit

On 6 July 2020 the FRC published principles requiring the Big Four (Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC) to operationally separate their UK audit practices. Implementation plans were due by 23 October 2020 and full implementation by 30 June 2024 [S183] [Confirmed]. The required outcomes [S183] [Confirmed]:

  • Audit governance that protects auditors from the rest of the firm.
  • Audit partners' profit share should not persistently exceed the audit practice's own contribution, so no cross-subsidy.
  • A culture of challenge and scepticism, with auditors acting in the public interest.

Press reports of the principles add that dealings between audit and the rest of the firm must be on arm's-length terms, and that the audit practice may not be paid for introducing work to other parts of the firm [S312] [Reported]. On 10 October 2024 the FRC announced that all four firms had completed the transition, with independent audit boards chaired by Audit Non-Executives [S184] [Confirmed]. Sarah Rapson of the FRC: "The FRC is pleased with the spirit in which the firms have embraced operational separation and the progress achieved to date" [S184].

The point for you: this is separation inside one firm, not a legal split. Deloitte remains one LLP, registered OC303675 [S407] [Confirmed]. Consulting must stand on its own economics and cannot lean on audit as a door-opener [Inferred].

DON'T SAY THIS

"ARGA is coming." It was dropped on 20 January 2026 [S185].

"Consultants at Deloitte are regulated by the FRC." The FRC regulates audit. Consulting is caught only through the independence rules that apply to the firm as auditor.

"Deloitte can't work for any FTSE company." It can work for companies it does not audit, and for audit clients within the whitelist and the cap.

"The UK forced the Big Four to split." Separation is operational, inside each firm. EY's own voluntary attempt to split ("Project Everest") was abandoned on 12 April 2023 [S141] [Reported].

"The EU AI Act high-risk rules apply from August 2026." The Digital Omnibus moved them (see below).

Rule set 4: how the government buys consultancy

The Procurement Act 2023

The Procurement Act 2023 and the Procurement Regulations 2024 came into force on 24 February 2025, after a delay from October 2024. Procurements started before that date stay under the old Public Contracts Regulations 2015. A new National Procurement Policy Statement was laid on 13 February 2025 [S190] [Confirmed]. The Act brings more transparency, including published notices and contract information [S175] [Reported]. Note: a September 2024 City AM article still gives the original October 2024 start date [S175]; the delayed date is the correct one.

On 1 April 2026 the Crown Commercial Service (CCS) became the Government Commercial Agency (GCA) [S190] [Confirmed]. Use the new name.

MCF4: the main route to central government consulting

The Management Consultancy Framework Four (MCF4, reference RM6309) runs from 29 July 2025 to 28 July 2027, with 219 suppliers across 10 lots. It allows time-and-materials, fixed-price or risk-and-reward pricing [S117] [Confirmed]. It replaces MCF3 (RM6187) and the restructuring and insolvency framework (RM6269), and is valued at around £1.7bn. The lots: business; strategy and policy; complex and transformation; finance; HR; procurement and supply chain; health, social care and community; infrastructure; environment and sustainability; restructuring and insolvency [S191] [Reported]. Whether Deloitte holds a place on each lot was not verified.

Rule set 5: data and AI

UK data protection

The base regime is the UK GDPR plus the Data Protection Act 2018 [Inferred]. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 amends it. Timeline [S194, S195] [Reported, two sources agree on Royal Assent]:

  • 19 June 2025: Royal Assent; some provisions in force immediately.
  • 20 August 2025: first commencement regulations.
  • 5 February 2026: most data protection changes in force.
  • 19 June 2026: new complaints duty (organisations must acknowledge data protection complaints within 30 days).

The EU AI Act, and the Digital Omnibus delay

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the EU's law on AI, with tighter rules for "high-risk" uses such as hiring, credit scoring, education and biometrics. It matters to UK consultants because UK clients that sell into the EU are caught, and because it creates advisory work. Original timeline [S193] [Confirmed]: in force 1 August 2024; prohibitions and AI-literacy duties from 2 February 2025; general-purpose AI (GPAI) obligations from 2 August 2025; most other provisions from 2 August 2026.

The Digital Omnibus on AI then delayed the high-risk rules. Provisional agreement 7 May 2026; Parliament approval 16 June 2026; Council adoption 29 June 2026; published in the Official Journal in late July 2026 [S192] [Reported]. New deadlines per Orrick [S192] and Morgan Lewis [S234]:

  • 2 December 2026: Article 50 transparency for synthetic (AI-generated) content; new bans on AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material [S192] [Reported].
  • 2 December 2027: stand-alone high-risk systems (Annex III) [S192, S234] [Reported].
  • 2 August 2028: AI built into regulated products (Annex I) [S192, S234].
  • 2 August 2030: public authority systems [S192]. Relief for "small mid-caps" with under 750 staff [S192].
SOURCE UNCERTAIN

Annex III high-risk date. Orrick (written after publication) and Morgan Lewis say 2 December 2027 [S192, S234]. The artificialintelligenceact.eu timeline lists 2 August 2027 [S193]. Entry into force of the omnibus: Orrick says 29 July 2026; another summary says 27 July 2026 [S192]. Neither was checked against the Official Journal. In an interview, say "the high-risk deadline moved into late 2027" and, if pressed, "2 December 2027 on the law-firm readings".

The UK's own approach to AI

The UK has no AI bill. The AI Opportunities Action Plan (13 January 2025, 50 recommendations) set a pro-innovation stance [S203] [Reported]. The King's Speech on 13 May 2026 had no AI bill. It did include a Regulating for Growth Bill, with a stronger statutory growth duty for regulators and "cross-cutting AI sandboxes", and a Cyber Security and Resilience Bill [S188] [Confirmed]. A sandbox is a controlled space where firms test new products under a regulator's eye with some rules relaxed. So a client operating in both the UK and the EU faces two very different AI regimes, which is exactly where advisers add value [Inferred].

The competitive map of UK suppliers

Who Deloitte competes with depends on the job. On a bank's Basel 3.1 programme it faces PwC, EY, KPMG and specialists. On an SAP rollout it faces Accenture, IBM and Capgemini. On a government framework it faces PA Consulting and the mid-tier. On a board strategy question it faces McKinsey, BCG and Bain. What clients value today

  • Help with AI that works safely. In an MCA client survey of 350+ leaders (May 2026), two in five organisations sought consulting help with digital and technology transformation. Top AI barriers: data security and privacy (40%), lack of skilled internal teams (34%), governance and roadmap problems (28%) [S240] [Reported].
  • Judgement and trust. MCA chief executive Tamzen Isacsson: "The competitive edge now lies in being able to provide judgment, sector knowledge and trust that is earned through outcomes" [S240].
  • Expertise plus partnerships. Source's 2026 outlook says expertise paired with strategic partnerships can be decisive in winning work, and that firms must address client worries about AI to build trust [S158] [Reported].
  • Cyber. The fastest-growing service globally (about 9%) and the top area where clients say they will bring in outside help [S156] [Reported].
  • More outside help, not less, in technology. 84% of tech buyers plan upgrades in the next year and 81% intend to rely more on external consultants [S157] [Reported].
  • Value for money, provably. Government buyers must "demonstrate the value-for-money case" (Reeves) [S176] [Confirmed]; challengers sell low cost and no conflicts [S167].
