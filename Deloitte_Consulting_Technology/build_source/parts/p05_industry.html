Part 5

The industry from zero

What consulting sells, who buys it, how a firm turns a working day into profit, how big the market is, and the forces reshaping it. Read this before any of the opinion sections: every argument later in the pack rests on the mechanics here.

What consulting sells, and who buys it

A consultancy sells people's time and judgement to an organisation with a problem it cannot, or will not, solve alone. Strip away the branding and a client is buying one or more of five things [Inferred]:

What the client buysWhat it means in practiceExample
ExpertiseKnowledge the client does not have in house.How to move a bank's core systems to the cloud; how to meet a new regulation.
CapacityExtra hands for a fixed period. The client may know what to do but lack the people. A lot of "consulting" spend is this.Around 900 to 1,035 Deloitte consultants worked on Test and Trace at peak [S332, S333] [Reported].
IndependenceAn outside view not tied to internal politics.A CEO quoted in HBR: when unsure of the question, "I'm more tempted to use a big brand" [S160] [Confirmed].
LegitimacyA known firm's name helps a board or minister defend a decision."No. 1 consulting provider by revenue" is a ranking a buyer can cite [S125] [Reported].
ImplementationBuilding and running the change: systems, processes, training. This is where most of the money now flows.Source says global technology consulting will pass $400bn in 2026 [S157] [Reported].

Who signs the cheque. In large companies the buyer is usually a C-suite executive: the chief financial officer (CFO) for finance and cost work, the chief information or technology officer (CIO, CTO) for technology, the chief operating officer (COO) for operations [Inferred]. Source Global Research, a consulting market analyst, says digital transformation projects "are most likely to be commissioned by CxOs, so companies are prepared to pay more for them" [S157] [Reported]. Banks and insurers buy heavily because regulation forces change on them (Part 6). The public sector (central government, the NHS, local government, defence) is the other big buyer. Old MCA data put financial services at about a third of UK fee income and the public sector at about a fifth, but that data set dates from around 2016 to 2018, so treat the shares as indicative only [S159] [Reported].

KEY TERM

Engagement (or project): one piece of paid work for one client, with a start, an end and a contract. SOW (statement of work): the contract document that sets out what will be delivered, by when, by whom and for how much. When a Deloitte analyst says "I'm on an engagement", this is what they mean.

DON'T SAY THIS

"Consulting is basically like investment banking but for operations." The payment model is the giveaway: banks earn mainly when a deal completes; consultancies earn mainly for time or a defined deliverable, and the hard part is getting change to stick after the deal or decision.

How a consulting firm makes money

Every consulting firm runs on the same four levers. Learn them and the industry's news makes sense: AI, graduate cuts, offshore centres and outcome pricing all act on one or more of them.

KEY TERM

Day rate (rate card): the price per day the firm charges for one person of a given grade. A rate card is the list of rates by grade agreed with a client. Rates disclosed to Parliament for Deloitte's Test and Trace work averaged about £1,000 a day per person, with some senior consultants up to £6,624 a day [S332] [Confirmed].

KEY TERM

Utilisation: the share of a person's available working time that is billed to clients. Chargeable hours are the hours actually billed. Juniors are expected to be highly chargeable; partners spend more time selling and managing [S93] [Inferred]. Deloitte does not publish its utilisation targets.

KEY TERM

Leverage: how many staff work under each partner. More juniors per partner means each partner's profit share draws on more billed hours. At Deloitte UK in FY26 that was roughly 30 staff per equity partner and about 16.5 per partner of any kind (my arithmetic from 23,970 average FTE staff, 799 FTE equity partners and 1,453 partners) [S88, S93] [Inferred]. Realisation: the share of the standard value of time that is actually billed and collected after discounts and write-offs [S93].

KEY TERM

The pyramid: the staffing shape that leverage produces. A few partners and directors at the top; more managers; many consultants and analysts at the base. Equity partner: an owner of the partnership who shares the profit. Deloitte UK had 784 equity partners at year end FY26 [S407] [Confirmed].

Put together, the arithmetic is simple. Revenue per person is billed days times day rate. Billed days are available days times utilisation. Leverage multiplies that across the team under each partner. What is left after pay, offices, technology and network fees is the profit pool that equity partners share. Deloitte's own accounts show that even fixed-fee work is tracked "via timesheet reporting" at charge-out rates [S93] [Confirmed], so these levers still govern a project priced as a lump sum.

Two live numbers show the levers at work. Deloitte UK's average fee-earning staff fell about 5% (21,471 to 20,346 FTE) in FY26 while revenue rose 2% and distributable profit rose 14% to £899m [S93, S407] [Confirmed]. More revenue from fewer fee-earners is leverage and productivity moving in partners' favour [Inferred]. That is also exactly why AI is such a sensitive topic: it acts on the base of the pyramid.

Core vocabulary in one place

Bench: consultants not currently on a client project. Long bench time hurts utilisation.

Pitch / RFP: the competitive bid to win work. RFP means "request for proposal", the buyer's formal invitation to bid.

Framework agreement: a pre-approved list of suppliers a buyer (often government) can call work off without a full tender each time. Example: the UK government's Management Consultancy Framework Four (MCF4), with 219 suppliers across 10 lots [S117] [Confirmed].

Managed services: the consultancy runs a process or system for the client for years. Revenue recurs. Deloitte calls its offer "Operate" [S120].

Time and materials (T&M): the client pays for days worked. Fixed fee: one price for a defined deliverable. Outcome-based (risk and reward): some fee depends on results. MCF4 allows all three [S117] [Confirmed].

Offshore / nearshore delivery: doing work in lower-cost locations (India, Malaysia, the Philippines are named in reporting on the Big Four) or nearby cheaper countries [S162] [Reported]. Onshore regional centres: lower-cost UK cities. Deloitte opened centres in Belfast, Cardiff, Manchester and Newcastle from June 2025 [S254] [Confirmed].

Alliance partners: software vendors (SAP, Salesforce, ServiceNow, Workday, AWS and others) whose products the consultancy implements. Deloitte UK lists 14 [S319] [Confirmed].

Systems integrator (SI): a firm that makes several software products work together inside a client. ERP (enterprise resource planning): core finance, HR and supply chain software such as SAP S/4HANA. Big, long and risky projects.

PMO (programme management office): the team that runs plans, governance and reporting on a large programme. TOM (target operating model): a design of how an organisation should work in future. Change management: helping people actually adopt the new way of working.

How big is the market?

Two respected bodies measure the UK market and get very different answers. Neither is wrong. They count different things.

£21.8bnUK consulting fee income 2025, +3% (MCA / Oxford Economics, July 2026) [S150, S151]
£14.9bnUK market 2024 on Source Global Research's narrower definition [S154]
$275bnGlobal consulting market 2025, +6% (Source, "big consulting") [S156]
>$400bnGlobal technology consulting, 2026 forecast, broader definition (Source) [S157]

MCA. The Management Consultancies Association, the UK trade body, publishes an annual report with Oxford Economics. It estimates the whole UK industry. Fee income rose 3% to £21.8bn in 2025; MCA members alone earned £10.9bn; exports rose 9% and are now 31% of fee income [S150, S151] [Confirmed]. The headline hides a split: 52% of firms grew and 46% shrank. The report says the 3% "masks a mixed performance across the sector" [S151] [Reported]. Forecasts have been cut again and again: 2026 growth was predicted at 8.7% (January 2025), then 7.8% (mid-2025), then 5.7% (early 2026); the July 2026 report says 6% for 2026 and 8% for 2027 [S154, S150] [Reported].

Source Global Research. Source counts only "big consulting": firms with 50 or more consultants, and it excludes outsourcing and managed services [S157] [Reported]. On that basis the UK market was £14.9bn in 2024, down from £15.4bn [S154, S241] [Reported]. Source forecasts 6% UK growth in 2026 from a survey of 200 senior UK buyers. Its head of market trends, Nick Jotischky: "There's a cautious optimism that there's going to be growth in the market" [S155] [Confirmed].

Global. Source sized global consulting at $275bn in 2025, 6% growth after "a disappointing 2024". Cybersecurity was the fastest-growing service at about 9% (about $36bn); technology and innovation was $69.2bn (+5%); strategy just over $60bn (+6%); risk and financial management $58.7bn (+7%) [S156] [Reported]. Its separate $400bn+ figure for technology consulting uses a broader definition. Do not add the two together [S157].

SOURCE UNCERTAIN

The MCA's 2025 report gave £20.4bn for 2024 [S152, S153]. The 2026 report says 2025 grew 3% to £21.8bn [S150]. £21.8bn is about 7% above £20.4bn, so the 2024 base was probably revised upward to around £21.2bn [Inferred]. If you quote growth, quote the MCA's own 3%, not a figure computed across the two reports.

SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

"The UK market is roughly £22bn on the MCA's measure and grew only 3% last year, with nearly half of firms shrinking. The forecast recovery, 6% then 8%, depends on clients moving from planning to implementation. That suits a firm that can deliver, not only advise."

The forces reshaping the industry now

Eight forces are pushing on the consulting model at once. Figure 24 maps each force to what it changes and who gains or loses. The text below gives the numbers.

1. Generative and agentic AI, and the pyramid

If AI drafts research, slides and code, firms need fewer analysts at the base. A recruiter told City AM: "The Big Four are looking at AI very seriously to replicate junior work more cost-effectively" [S162] [Reported]. A summary of a May 2026 FT Big Read (paywalled, not read) quotes KPMG UK's advisory head Lisa Fernihough: "I want this organisation, us, to still exist … That's how disruptive I believe AI will be" [S164] [Reported]. Mark Bunker, formerly of Deloitte, who founded Queen's Tower Advisory, reportedly targets teams that are "20 per cent humans, 80 per cent AI agents" [S164] [Reported]. Agentic AI means AI systems that take actions (book, file, update records) rather than only answer questions. Deloitte's own Tech Trends 2026 says only 11% of organisations have such systems in production [S244] [Confirmed].

2. Pricing moves from days to outcomes

McKinsey is reported to link about a quarter to a third of fees to outcomes; sources disagree on the figure [S163, S164] [Reported]. In 2013 a partner quoted in HBR expected value-based pricing to go "from the high single digits to a third of the business within 20 years" [S160] [Confirmed]. A commentator writing in January 2026 called the shift "sluggish" [S163] [Reported].

3. Graduate intake cuts and low attrition

Reported UK graduate intakes fell at all of the Big Four between 2023 and 2024: KPMG 1,399 to 942 (-29%), Deloitte about 1,700 to 1,400 (-18%), EY -11%, PwC -6% [S162, S143] [Reported]. Graduate accountancy adverts fell 44% year on year [S162] [Reported]. Attrition is the share of staff who leave each year. Big Four firms rely on it to make room for promotions. When fewer leave, firms pay people to go: Deloitte offered voluntary redundancy to up to about 175 audit staff in June 2026, citing "low rates of attrition" [S227] [Confirmed]. The wider market is mixed: MCA members raised graduate hiring 10% in 2025 [S150] [Reported].

4. New entrants

OpenAI and Anthropic launched consulting ventures in May 2026, and OpenAI bought UK consultancy Tomoro AI (about 150 staff, clients including Tesco and Virgin Atlantic). An industry insider called it "definitely a direct play against the traditional consulting firms" [S166] [Reported]. Unity Advisory launched in April 2025 with up to $300m from private equity firm Warburg Pincus, led by ex-EY UK chair Steve Varley. It offers no audit, so it has no audit conflicts [S167] [Confirmed].

5. Private equity enters professional services

Private equity (PE) firms buy companies with investors' money and sell them later. Cinven completed a buyout of Grant Thornton UK in 2025, reportedly valuing it around £1.5bn; the average partner payout was reported at £682,000 [S168] [Reported]. In the US, New Mountain Capital took about 60% of Grant Thornton's non-audit business in 2024 [S144] [Reported]. None of the Big Four has taken outside capital [S144, S120] [Inferred].

6. UK government spend controls

In October 2023 Rachel Reeves, then shadow Chancellor, said Labour "will aim to cut consultancy spending in half over the next parliament" [S176] [Confirmed]. In government the target became: stop non-essential consultancy, halve it in 2025-26, save £550m in 2024-25 rising to £700m a year by 2028-29 [S173, S177] [Confirmed]. The data conflict sharply; Part 6 sets them side by side. Deloitte matters here because it was the largest UK government consultancy supplier by contract value in Labour's first year, at £431m [S174] [Reported].

7. The US DOGE cuts

In 2025 the US Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) drive cancelled federal contracts. The Pentagon cancelled $5.1bn of contracts in April 2025 (claimed savings $4bn), naming Accenture, Booz Allen Hamilton and Deloitte [S169] [Confirmed]. Fortune reported more than 120 Deloitte contracts eliminated, more than twice any other consultancy [S170] [Reported]. This hit Deloitte's US firm, a legally separate member firm from Deloitte UK, but it shows the risk of depending on government buyers [Inferred].

8. AI quality failure: Deloitte Australia

In October 2025 Deloitte Australia agreed to partially refund the A$440,000 paid for a 237-page government report that contained a fabricated court quote and references to non-existent papers. The revised version disclosed use of "a generative AI large language model (Azure OpenAI GPT-4o)" [S316, S171] [Confirmed]. It happened in a separate member firm, yet the story ran worldwide under the Deloitte name [S316] [Inferred]. It is the clearest public example of why human review of AI output is now part of the product.

YOUR OPINION GOES HERE

Prompt: "Where will consulting be in five years?" Pick a side and give a mechanism.

Case for disruption: AI removes the junior hours that pay for the pyramid; AI labs and PE-backed challengers attack on price; SME card data already show consulting spend falling 1.9% while AI subscriptions rise [S239] [Reported].

Case for resilience: clients' main AI barriers are data security (40%), skills (34%) and governance (28%), which are human and organisational problems [S240] [Reported]; MCA and Source both forecast growth [S150, S155].

Suggested stance: "I think demand for implementation grows while demand for junior analytical hours shrinks. The winners will be firms with delivery depth, trusted brands in regulated sectors and AI alliances, provided they change how they price and how they train juniors. So the pyramid becomes narrower at the base, not flat." Then name one fact: Deloitte UK grew profit 14% on 2% revenue growth while fee-earners fell about 5% [S407, S93].
