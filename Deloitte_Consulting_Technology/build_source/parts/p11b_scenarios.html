Scenario cards: three rungs for every duty line

Thirty worked scenarios, one easy, one medium and one hard for each of the ten duty lines in the JD. Each card shows the trap, a scaffold answer in your own voice, the rule that makes it right, and the follow-up that tests whether you hold.

Every card uses the four-step protocol from the first half of this Part. Steady: pause, acknowledge, check the facts. Sort: rank what is at stake. Act: say the concrete next step, usually with a named person. Close: confirm, record, follow up. The ranking in the Sort step never changes:

THE PRIORITY ORDER USED ON EVERY CARD

1. Law, independence and the firm's name > 2. The client's real interest and fair treatment of people > 3. Process and an honest record > 4. Speed. When two pull against each other, the higher one wins. Deloitte's own dilemma method says it in three words, ACT: Assess, Consider and Consult, Take action, and adds "Speaking up is taking action!" [S340] [Confirmed].

Scenarios are illustrative, written for a first-year Analyst in Technology Strategy & Transformation (TST). Clients are generic on purpose. Numbers inside setups are invented for the exercise; every rule cited under "Why it works" is sourced.

D1 · Senior client engagement and rapport

The duty: "We work with their most senior leaders and top-level executives", and you will learn "How to build rapport with clients and ensure they have confidence in your capability" [S2].

D1 · Rung 1: First call with the CIOEASY
Setup

Three weeks into an IT operating model review for a UK retailer, your manager messages from a delayed train: "Five minutes late, can you open the call with the CIO?" (Chief Information Officer, the top technology executive). You have never met her.

The trap

Filling time with improvised opinions, or apologising so much that you waste a senior person's time.

What to say

Steady: re-read the call objective. Sort: her time matters most; my job is to start well, not run the content. Act: "Good morning, I'm [name], an analyst on the Deloitte team. [Manager] joins in five minutes. So we use your time well, could I confirm today's goal, agreeing the phase two interview list, and play back last week's three themes?" Close: log questions I can't answer as actions; hand over with a one-line summary.

Why it works

The JD names client confidence in your capability as a skill to build [S2]. The Code: "We are truthful about ... the knowledge we possess, and the experience we have" [S340]. Opening with the objective is Minto's point-first rule [S374].

The twist, and how to hold

"So, should we outsource infrastructure?" Hold: "That's exactly what phase two will answer. What I can share now is what your team told us." Give facts; park the opinion for your manager.

D1 · Rung 2: Director doubts your experienceMEDIUM
Setup

In a requirements workshop at a central government department, a client director asks you: "How many of these have you actually done?" You are seven months into the job. The room goes quiet.

The trap

Inflating ("quite a few") breaches integrity. Shrinking ("I'm only an analyst") tells the room to discount you.

What to say

Steady: the question is fair. Sort: honesty first, then his real worry: is this team competent? Act: "Honestly, this is my second programme of this kind. What I bring today is the detail: I've read all your process maps and I own the requirements log. The wider team has run this work many times, and [manager] can take you through that." Close: "If there's a specific concern about the approach, I'll note it now so we answer it before we finish."

Why it works

The Code requires truth about "the experience we have" [S340]. "Serve with integrity" calls client trust "our single most important responsibility" [S342]. Firm credentials are shown by the people who own them [Inferred].

The twist, and how to hold

"So we're paying for you to learn?" Fees are the partner's conversation. "That's a fair challenge; I'll make sure [manager] picks it up with you today." Then tell your manager straight after.

D1 · Rung 3: Client wants the bad news buriedHARD
Setup

The sponsor at a UK insurer asks you, in a corridor, to leave the red data-migration risk off Thursday's steering committee pack: "the board will panic". You keep the RAID log (Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies).

The trap

Agreeing to please her, or refusing flatly and starting a confrontation you do not own.

What to say

Steady: "I understand the worry about how it lands." Sort: hiding a red risk misleads the client's own board; presentation is my manager's and partner's call. Act: "I can't remove it on my own, but I'll take your concern to [manager] today. Could we show it with the mitigation plan and a named owner, so the board sees a risk being managed?" Close: same-day note to my manager quoting the request; the log stays unchanged.

Why it works

The Code: be "straightforward and honest in our professional opinions", and consult, because "No one at Deloitte should have to navigate a difficult situation alone" [S340]. Misstating status to win the next phase is a fraud risk; the failure-to-prevent-fraud offence has applied since 1 September 2025 [S361, S362] [Confirmed].

The twist, and how to hold

"Your partner already agreed." Hold: "Then it'll be a quick conversation. I'll confirm with them before I change anything." Never act on a second-hand approval.

D2 · IT operating model

The duty: "helping organisations move away from IT, the department of computers, to technology as a business partner and enabler"; TST's first listed offering is "Technology Operating Model" [S2]. An operating model is how a function is organised, funded, sourced and governed.

D2 · Rung 1: Explain IT as business partnerEASY
Setup

In a current-state interview at a UK housing association, the finance lead asks what "moving IT from cost centre to business partner" means for her team. A cost centre is a unit judged mainly on what it spends.

The trap

A jargon answer, or promising outcomes the review has not yet found.

What to say

Steady: treat it as a real question; it is the heart of the project. Sort: clear and accurate beats impressive. Act: "Today IT is mostly judged on keeping systems running cheaply. As a business partner, IT would plan with you, agree what technology finance needs to hit its goals, and be measured on those goals too. For your team, that might mean a named IT contact in budget season instead of raising tickets." Close: "Does that match what you'd want? I'll note it as evidence for the review."

Why it works

It mirrors the JD [S2] and the TST page aim to "increase the value, agility, efficiency and experience of technology investments" [S41]. Answer first, then example, is the Minto pyramid [S374].

The twist, and how to hold

"So will my team get cut?" Hold: "I don't know, and the review hasn't reached recommendations. I can promise your view goes into the evidence." Never speculate about headcount.

D2 · Rung 2: IT head resists the new modelMEDIUM
Setup

At a UK logistics company, the head of infrastructure says the proposed model (moving his services to a managed provider) will cut his team and he will not support it. He asks what the draft report says.

The trap

False reassurance, leaking draft findings to keep him onside, or writing him off as a blocker.

What to say

Steady: acknowledge this is personal for him. Sort: drafts are confidential and belong to the sponsor; his concern is real evidence and deserves fair treatment. Act: "I can't share draft recommendations; they aren't final and they belong to [sponsor]. What I can do is capture your concerns properly. What would any new model have to get right for your team and the service?" Close: log it in the stakeholder map; tell my manager today and suggest a one-to-one with the engagement lead.

Why it works

On a power/interest grid he is high on both: "manage closely" [S380] [Reported]. The Code bars disclosing confidential information without permission [S340]. "Take care of each other" puts "respect, fairness" first [S342].

The twist, and how to hold

"Then I'll tell the CIO your team is out of its depth." Hold: do not trade information for peace. "That's your right. I'd still rather your points shape the work." Escalate to your manager at once.

D2 · Rung 3: Two sponsors, two operating modelsHARD
Setup

At a UK utility, the CIO privately asks you to make the options slides favour one central IT function. The COO (Chief Operating Officer) separately asks you to favour IT embedded in each business unit. You draft the options pack.

The trap

Tilting slides towards whoever spoke last or ranks higher, or settling the politics yourself.

What to say

Steady: thank each; note the request. Sort: objectivity and the organisation's interest beat either executive's preference; the decision sits with the client's governance. Act: to each: "I'll make sure both options are scored fairly against the agreed criteria." To my manager: "Both sponsors have asked me for different outcomes. Could the steering committee re-confirm the criteria and weights, and we score both openly?" Close: the scoring workbook records every criterion, weight and source.

Why it works

The Code: "We do not allow bias, conflict of interest, or undue influence, to alter our interactions" [S340]. The ICAEW Code names "undue influence of ... individuals" as an objectivity threat [S372]. A RACI gives each decision one Accountable owner [S379]: here, the steering committee. For a Deloitte audit client, "informed management" must decide [S364].

The twist, and how to hold

"Remember who signs off your invoices." That is commercial pressure: do not negotiate it, report it to the partner the same day. The Code also covers influence that is only "perceived" [S340].

D3 · The digital core and the technology landscape

The duty: "create a digital core" and "Discovering how technologies support everything an organisation does, and which technologies work best for each part of a business" [S2]. The digital core is the ERP (enterprise resource planning) and core platforms that finance, supply chain and HR run on.

D3 · Rung 1: Map the application landscapeEASY
Setup

A UK retailer sends a 400-row application inventory. You have two days for a first landscape view: a one-page map of which systems support which business capabilities. Rows have blanks, duplicates and missing owners.

The trap

A beautiful diagram on dirty data, or quietly deleting rows you think are duplicates.

What to say

Steady: confirm the purpose: steering pack or internal working view? Sort: accuracy and traceability before polish. Act: "I'd group applications by capability (finance, supply chain, stores, HR, customer), tag owner, platform and contract end date, and flag blanks and suspected duplicates in a column instead of deleting them. End of day one, I'd send a rough view plus the data-gap list." Close: every count traces to row IDs; gaps go to the client's application owner as questions.

Why it works

Buckets that do not overlap and leave nothing out are MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) [S375]. Improving the value of technology investments is TST's stated aim [S41]. An early check-in saves a day of rework [Inferred].

The twist, and how to hold

The client now wants it tomorrow. Hold: trade scope, not accuracy. "I can do the top three capabilities fully and mark the rest draft."

D3 · Rung 2: Vendor already chosen before analysisMEDIUM
Setup

Early in an ERP platform selection for a UK manufacturer, a client executive says the vendor is already decided and asks you to "make the analysis fit". Deloitte has alliances with several shortlisted vendors.

The trap

Reverse-engineering the scores. With alliance revenue in play, a rigged selection also looks like the firm steering for itself.

What to say

Steady: ask what the reasons are; there may be good ones. Sort: objectivity and the firm's name first; the client may choose, but our report must be honest. Act: "It's your decision. I'd suggest our report shows the criteria and scores as they come out, with your strategic reasons recorded beside them. That gives your board a decision it can defend." I tell my manager the same day. Close: the scoring workbook stays unaltered; the instruction is noted.

Why it works

Deloitte UK lists 14 alliance partners, including SAP, Oracle and Workday [S319] [Confirmed]. The Code bans "undue influence" over judgement [S340]. Decisions belong to the client's "informed management" [S364]. Business relationships go through conflict checks [S35].

The twist, and how to hold

"Your firm partners with that vendor anyway." Hold: "That's exactly why our scoring has to be clean. I'll flag the alliance point to [manager] so it's handled properly."

D3 · Rung 3: Legacy system nobody understandsHARD
Setup

On an ERP replacement for a UK water company, the cutover plan (the step-by-step switch from old system to new) assumes the legacy billing system is documented. It is not. It holds critical customer data, and its only expert retires in a month.

The trap

Assuming someone senior has spotted it, or announcing a crisis in a big meeting before you have facts.

What to say

Steady: check what documentation exists, the leaving date and which cutover tasks depend on it. Sort: customers' billing and data first; it belongs in the RAID log; the date makes it urgent. Act: "I think this is a red risk. Several cutover tasks assume documentation that doesn't exist, and the only expert leaves at month end. I'd suggest a knowledge-capture sprint with him from next week, and a client decision on extending his contract." Close: logged with owner and date; on the next status report.

Why it works

Tracking risks and deadlines is core junior work [S9]. Issues can become change requests [S377]. UK GDPR requires accurate personal data [S353]. Capita's £14m ICO fine followed a 58-hour delay in acting on an alert [S394] [Confirmed]: speed of escalation matters.

The twist, and how to hold

The expert refuses to be "interrogated" in his last month. Hold: "Take care of each other" [S342]. Frame it as recognising his knowledge; his line manager sets it up, in short sessions, recorded with consent.

D4 · Business case and value

The duty: "improving financial performance, accelerating speed to market" and "Help assess technology investments, identify opportunities to improve value, agility, efficiency, and user experience" [S2]. A business case sets out costs, benefits and risks so a client can decide whether to invest.

D4 · Rung 1: Sense-check a benefits numberEASY
Setup

Your manager asks you to sanity-check a £12m savings figure in a UK retailer's cloud migration business case before it goes to the client tomorrow.

The trap

Re-reading the slide and saying "looks fine", or rebuilding the whole model overnight.

What to say

Steady: ask how deep a check, and by when. Sort: accuracy over speed, inside the deadline. Act: "Three checks. Trace the £12m to its source cells. Test scale against the client's IT cost baseline. Check the basis: annual or five-year, gross or net of migration cost. If it's five-year and gross but the slide says 'per year', the label is wrong." Close: a short note with cell references; my manager decides the wording.

Why it works

Assessing technology investments is in the JD [S2]. The ICAEW Code asks professionals to "Act diligently" [S372]. Answer first, then evidence, is Minto [S374].

The twist, and how to hold

"Just tell me if it's roughly right." Hold: one line, plus the caveat that matters: "Roughly right, but it's five-year, not annual." Short is fine; dropping the caveat is not.

D4 · Rung 2: Benefits that do not add upMEDIUM
Setup

On a finance transformation business case for a central government department, you find 30 FTE (full-time equivalent posts) of savings already claimed in another programme's approved case.

The trap

Deleting the savings yourself, or ignoring it because the client supplied the number.

What to say

Steady: re-check both documents and versions. Sort: a board approving a double-counted case wastes public money; accuracy first, raised through my manager. Act: "I've found an overlap. The 30 FTE in our benefit line also appear in the other programme's approved case. Unless the two are coordinated, the department counts the same saving twice. Shall I draft a note for the client's finance business partner to confirm?" Close: an issue in the RAID log with an owner; the model's change log records the edit.

Why it works

Benefits must be mutually exclusive, the first half of MECE [S375]. Departments must "demonstrate the value-for-money case" for consultancy [S176] [Confirmed]. Honest professional opinions are a Code duty [S340].

The twist, and how to hold

"Everyone does it, and the other programme won't deliver anyway." Hold: "Then the case can say so openly, as a dependency. What we can't do is count it twice without saying."

D4 · Rung 3: Partner wants a bigger numberHARD
Setup

Late evening. A partner asks you to lift the productivity assumption in an ERP investment case so it clears the client's hurdle rate (the minimum return needed to approve an investment): "Make it 18%, not 10%."

The trap

Doing it because of seniority, or refusing as an accusation. There may be evidence you have not seen.

What to say

Steady: ask, do not accuse. Sort: misleading a client's investment decision touches the law and the firm's name. Act: "Happy to update it. Could you point me to the evidence for 18% so I can source it in the assumptions log? Our benchmark supports about 10%. If we can't source it, could we show 18% as an upside scenario beside the base case?" Close: the assumptions log records who changed what and why. If pushed with no basis, I consult my manager or Ethics.

Why it works

The Code: honest opinions, and "Speaking up is taking action!" [S340]. Since 1 September 2025 a large organisation can be guilty if an associate commits fraud meant to benefit it or its client [S361, S362] [Confirmed]. "Retaliation against those who raise ethical concerns is not tolerated" [S340].

The twist, and how to hold

"It's an assumption. Don't be naive." Hold: "Agreed, it's a judgement. I just want it written down with its basis, so it holds up if their board asks." Keep it about the record, not the person.

D5 · Net zero and inclusive ways of working

The duty: transforming how clients run "in ways that drive net zero and incorporate inclusive ways of working" [S2]. Net zero means cutting greenhouse gas emissions as far as possible and balancing the rest with removals.

D5 · Rung 1: Add carbon to the caseEASY
Setup

A UK retailer asks whether its cloud migration business case should include a carbon estimate. Your manager wants your view at tomorrow's stand-up.

The trap

Stating "cloud is greener" as fact, or brushing it off as out of scope.

What to say

Steady: check whether carbon is in the agreed scope. Sort: accuracy first, then the client's stated goal. Act: "Yes, as a separate line, kept apart from the financial benefits, because the client says net zero matters. But it needs sources: current data-centre energy use, the provider's published figures, and a range, not a point. If we can't source it, we say what data we'd need." Close: offer a one-slide method note; flag any scope change to my manager.

Why it works

Net zero is written into the JD [S2]. "Lead the way" includes "leading the way to a more sustainable world" [S342]. UK sustainability reporting standards were published for voluntary use in February 2026 [S202] [Reported]. APM: define scope early [S378].

The twist, and how to hold

The client wants a precise figure by Friday, with no data. Hold: give the method and a range labelled "Estimate", plus when a real figure becomes possible.

D5 · Rung 2: Workshop excludes remote colleaguesMEDIUM
Setup

Your all-day design workshop at a UK bank's head office is in person only. Three key client staff who work part-time or remotely cannot attend, including one area's process owner.

The trap

Running it anyway and "catching them up later", or rescheduling without asking anyone.

What to say

Steady: confirm who is missing and which sessions affect them. Sort: fair treatment and good requirements beat the convenience of the plan. Act: to my manager: "Three of the people closest to the process can't make Thursday. Could we add a video link for the two sessions on their area, inside their working hours? If not, I'll run a 45-minute follow-up with them before sign-off." Close: the notes record attendance and how absent colleagues' input was captured.

Why it works

"Inclusive ways of working" is in the JD [S2]. "Foster inclusion": Deloitte is "at our best when we foster an inclusive culture" [S342]. Requirements should be agreed by the people who own the process [Inferred].

The twist, and how to hold

"They can read the minutes." Hold: "They can, and they will. The risk is designing a process their team runs without them, so I'd like their input before sign-off." Frame it as a quality risk, because it is one.

D5 · Rung 3: Green claim without evidenceHARD
Setup

The client wants the final report to say the programme "delivers net zero IT". Your data supports only a modest cut in data-centre energy use. The report carries Deloitte's name.

The trap

Letting marketing language into a Deloitte deliverable, or lecturing the client.

What to say

Steady: re-check the data and method. Sort: honesty and the firm's name first; an unsupported claim also exposes the client. Final wording is my manager's and partner's call. Act: "I want the strongest claim the data supports. Today that's a reduction in energy use for in-scope IT, not net zero. Could we say it 'reduces emissions from in-scope IT, a step towards net zero', give the range, and list what going further would take?" Close: a footnote with method and sources; I raise the wording with my manager before the draft goes.

Why it works

The Code: "We are truthful about the services we provide" [S340]. "Serve with integrity" calls trust "our single most important responsibility" [S342]. As UK sustainability reporting matures [S202], climate claims face more scrutiny [Inferred].

The twist, and how to hold

"We'll put it in our press release anyway." Hold: "That's your call for your release. Our report has to say what our evidence shows." The relationship conversation is the partner's.

D6 · Large-scale transformation, design through implementation

The duty: "you will work on large-scale technology transformations, from the design through to implementation" [S2]. For a first-year Analyst this mostly means PMO (project management office) work: RAID logs, status reports, plans and meeting packs [Inferred].

D6 · Rung 1: Update the RAID logEASY
Setup

At 6pm you are handed the programme RAID log for tomorrow's 10am status meeting. Half the actions have no owner; several dates have passed.

The trap

Typing guessed owners in as fact, or perfecting the formatting until 2am.

What to say

Steady: ask which items will actually be discussed. Sort: an accurate record beats a complete-looking one. Act: sort by RAG status (red, amber, green) and due date; for the top ten, propose owners from past minutes, marked "proposed, to confirm". Then to my manager: "Twelve actions have no owner. I've proposed owners for the top ten, to confirm in the meeting. Two are overdue and red." Close: confirm owners in the meeting; update the log the same day.

Why it works

Junior postings openly list "tracking deadlines, and preparing materials" [S9]. A RACI gives each task one Accountable person [S379], so owners are agreed, not assigned by you. Answer-first notes are Minto [S374].

The twist, and how to hold

"Just put names in, nobody will notice." Hold: "I'll put them in as proposed, so nobody is surprised in the meeting." Just as quick, and honest.

D6 · Rung 2: Status report turning amberMEDIUM
Setup

Your testing workstream will miss a milestone by two weeks because the test environment arrived late. The weekly status report goes to the client tomorrow. Last week it was green.

The trap

Keeping it green and hoping to recover, or putting the bad news in the report before your manager has heard it.

What to say

Steady: confirm the cause, the size of the slip and my confidence. Sort: honest reporting first; my manager hears before the client. Act: "The environment arrived nine days late; at current pace we miss by two weeks. Options: two extra testers from the delivery centre for three weeks, or move two low-priority interfaces to the next release. I'd recommend the first." Close: the report shows amber with cause, impact, recovery plan and the decision needed, by when.

Why it works

APM: "Formal change control is required when tolerances are breached" [S377]. A Deloitte director describes client check-ins "so we can manage any potential risks" [S55]. Misreporting progress to secure more work is a fraud risk [S361].

The twist, and how to hold

The client's workstream lead asks you to stay green "until we've tried to recover". Hold: "I'll show amber with your recovery plan in it. That gives you credit for the plan."

D6 · Rung 3: Go-live with a known defectHARD
Setup

On an HR and payroll rollout for a large public sector employer, the client wants to go live on Monday. Testing has an open severity-one defect (the highest level): overtime is miscalculated for some part-time staff.

The trap

Marking the test as passed to protect the date, or acting as if go/no-go is your decision.

What to say

Steady: confirm who is affected and any workaround. Sort: paying people correctly and handling their data lawfully outrank the schedule; the client's accountable owner decides, with full information. Act: "It isn't my call, but the go/no-go meeting must see this clearly. The defect underpays some part-time staff. Options: go live with a checked manual adjustment for that group, or delay one pay cycle." Close: the decision and any accepted risk are minuted with the owner's name.

Why it works

User acceptance testing lets users "determine whether to accept the system" [S381] [Reported]. The Accountable person signs off [S379]. UK GDPR requires accurate personal data [S353]. A failed test reported as passed is a false record [S340].

The twist, and how to hold

"Deloitte gets blamed for any delay. Close it as a known issue." Hold: reclassifying is fine only if the client's accountable owner accepts it in writing, with a workaround. You never close it yourself.

D7 · Requirements

The duty: "Working with individuals from our clients' business and IT teams to understand what the problems and needs are, and transforming those into technical requirements" [S2]. A requirement is a testable statement of what a system must do.

D7 · Rung 1: Run a requirements interviewEASY
Setup

You have 45 minutes with an operations manager at a central government department to capture requirements for a new case-management system.

The trap

Asking "what features do you want?" and collecting a wish list, or steering her to a system you know.

What to say

Steady: prepare the objective, the current process map and about eight open questions. Sort: the problem before solutions. Act: "Could you walk me through the last case you handled? Where did it slow down? What happens when that step goes wrong?" Then play back: "So the need is to see all correspondence on a case in one place, and success is fewer missed deadlines. Is that right?" Close: same-day write-up; each requirement testable, linked to its source, and sent to her to confirm.

Why it works

A sister posting describes "Getting to the bottom of client's problems through interactive workshops and labs" [S8]. Asking "why" before "how" is the diagnostic issue tree [S376] [Reported]. Good requirements are testable, traced and agreed [Inferred].

The twist, and how to hold

She starts naming members of the public in real cases. Hold: "I don't need names; the pattern is enough." Data minimisation is a UK GDPR principle [S353], and some government material needs vetting before you see it [S370].

D7 · Rung 2: Business and IT disagreeMEDIUM
Setup

At a UK retailer, store operations want real-time stock visibility. IT says it is impossible within budget. Each side asks you, separately, to back them.

The trap

Picking a side to be liked, or setting the priority yourself.

What to say

Steady: listen to both; write down each claim. Sort: my job is to make the trade-off visible; the client's product owner decides. Act: "It isn't my call, but I can make the choice clearer. Can we separate the need from the solution? If the need is stock accurate to within an hour, does it have to be real time? IT, what would hourly cost?" Then two or three options with cost, benefit and risk go to the product owner. Close: the decision and reasons go in the decision log.

Why it works

The Scrum Guide makes the Product Owner "one person, not a committee" [S373]. A solution issue tree breaks "how" into options [S376]. "Collaborate for measurable impact" is a Shared Value [S342]. For an audit client, management must decide [S364].

The twist, and how to hold

"You're the consultant, just tell us who's right." Hold: give a recommendation with evidence, clearly labelled as one, and leave the decision with the owner.

D7 · Rung 3: Scope creep from a senior userHARD
Setup

A client finance director keeps emailing you new requirements outside change control (the agreed process for approving scope changes) and expects them in the release two weeks away.

The trap

Quietly building them in (untested, unpaid, a risk to the release), or a blunt "no" to a senior client.

What to say

Steady: thank him; the ideas may be good. Sort: a stable release and the agreed contract come first, handled so the relationship holds. Act: "Thank you, these are useful. To get them in safely, I'll log them as change requests today so the product owner can weigh them against the sprint goal. I'll come back by Thursday with the time each would take." I copy my manager. Close: change register, plus a weekly summary to the sponsor of requested, approved and deferred items.

Why it works

APM change control: log, assess impact on "benefits, scope, quality, time, resources, costs, risks", then approve, reject or defer [S377]. Scrum: "No changes are made that would endanger the Sprint Goal" [S373]. Clear scope prevents later misunderstanding [S378].

The twist, and how to hold

"I'm paying for this programme. Just do it." Hold: "Understood. The fastest safe route is through the product owner this week." If it repeats, your manager takes it to the engagement lead; the commercial point is theirs.

D8 · Documents and client materials

The duty: "Creating documents crucial to the delivery and execution of the programme, alongside materials presented back to clients" [S2]. In practice: steering committee packs, design documents and workshop materials, mostly in PowerPoint [Inferred].

D8 · Rung 1: Tighten a slide for tomorrowEASY
Setup

At 7pm your manager says your three-slide summary is "too busy" and needs rewriting for a 9am client meeting.

The trap

Shrinking the font, defending every point, or working all night.

What to say

Steady: ask one question: "What's the one decision the client needs to make tomorrow?" Sort: clarity over completeness. Act: "I'd rewrite each title as a full sentence stating the point, keep three supporting points with numbers under each, and move detail to an appendix. Version two by 9pm, with a note: 'Titles now tell the story in order; detail is in the appendix.'" Close: every number ties to its source; template and spelling checked.

Why it works

Minto: ideas are easiest to grasp "organized as a pyramid under a single point" [S374]. Supporting points that do not overlap are MECE [S375]. Several review rounds on client documents are normal [Inferred].

The twist, and how to hold

At 8am your manager rewrites it again, differently. Hold: no ego. Make the change, keep a version log, and later ask which principle to apply next time.

D8 · Rung 2: Typo found after sendingMEDIUM
Setup

An hour after a client pack went to twelve client stakeholders, you spot a cost shown as £12m that the source gives as £1.2m.

The trap

Hoping nobody notices, or sending your own panicked correction to all twelve without your manager.

What to say

Steady: verify the error and whether it changes any conclusion. Sort: honesty with the client, through the agreed route; fast, but speed comes last. Act: message my manager within minutes: "The cost slide shows £12m; the source says £1.2m. The conclusion is unchanged. Suggested fix: a corrected version with a one-line note. Draft attached." Close: once agreed, the correction goes out, the version log is updated, and I add a number tie-out step to my checklist.

Why it works

The Code requires honesty with clients [S340]; the ICAEW Code asks for due care [S372]. Owning every line of a deliverable is the lesson of Deloitte Australia's 2025 report refund [S388] [Confirmed].

The twist, and how to hold

The email also went to an outside supplier, and the appendix lists staff names and salaries. Hold: that may be a personal data breach, which covers accidental disclosure. Escalate at once to your manager and the firm's incident route; the client may have 72 hours to notify the ICO [S354].

D8 · Rung 3: Pack contradicts the client's dataHARD
Setup

The night before a board pack goes out, you see the client's latest quarterly data. Volumes have fallen sharply, and the savings behind the headline recommendation rest on the old, higher volumes. Seniors have signed off.

The trap

Staying quiet because it is late and approved, or changing the recommendation yourself.

What to say

Steady: check the version and date of both data sets. Sort: the board must not decide on a false basis; that and the firm's name outrank the deadline. Act: phone my manager tonight, then send the comparison: "Last week's data shows volumes well below the pack's baseline. If right, the headline savings fall by about two-thirds. I've checked twice. Options: hold the pack a day, or send it with a flagged caveat and an update to follow." Close: record what was found and decided.

Why it works

The Code requires honest opinions and consultation: "No one at Deloitte should have to navigate a difficult situation alone" [S340]. Trust is "our single most important responsibility" [S342].

The twist, and how to hold

"Too late, the partner signed off. Send it." Hold: "Could I have two minutes with the partner? I'd rather they decide knowing this." If still told to send, ask for the instruction in writing and consult further. Speak Up is open to you, and retaliation is not tolerated [S340].

D9 · Firm-approved AI, used responsibly

The duty: "Using firm-approved AI tools responsibly to support client delivery" [S3]. Two words carry the weight: firm-approved.

D9 · Rung 1: Which AI tool to useEASY
Setup

You want an AI assistant to summarise fifteen sets of stakeholder interview notes. You are not sure which tools the firm approves for client material.

The trap

A personal account on a public chatbot "just this once", or avoiding AI altogether out of fear.

What to say

Steady: ask before starting. Sort: client confidentiality first, then the record. Act: "I'd ask my manager which tools are approved for client data on this engagement, and whether the contract allows AI use. Then I'd use the approved tool, strip out names it doesn't need, and check each summary against the notes." Close: keep the source notes, mark that AI assisted, and treat each reviewed summary as my own work.

Why it works

Deloitte UK rates GenAI a "Very High", new priority risk, controlled by use-case approval and a "clearing house" process [S345] [Confirmed]. Its internal tool PairD works "within its secure digital ecosystem" [S345]. The Code bars disclosing confidential information [S340]. Deloitte: "accountability is squarely a human domain" [S347].

The twist, and how to hold

"Use whatever's quickest." Hold: "I'll use the approved tool; it's just as quick and keeps us inside policy."

D9 · Rung 2: Colleague pastes data into ChatGPTMEDIUM
Setup

You see a fellow analyst paste client interview transcripts, with names and job titles, into a public AI chatbot to save time.

The trap

Ignoring it as "not my business", or calling it out in front of the team.

What to say

Steady: speak privately, straight away. Sort: confidentiality and a possible breach first; care for the colleague next. Act: "I think that's a public tool, and client data isn't meant to go in there. Can we stop and tell [manager] now? It's better coming from you, and I'll come with you." If they refuse, I raise it myself the same day. Close: the manager and the firm's route decide if it is reportable; I note what I saw and when.

Why it works

The Code's duty to report covers "a colleague" [S340]. A personal data breach includes accidental "unauthorised disclosure"; notifiable ones go to the ICO within 72 hours [S354] [Confirmed]. "Take care of each other" is why you offer to go with them [S342].

The twist, and how to hold

"Everyone does it, and I've deleted the chat." Hold: deleting does not undo the disclosure, and destroying records can make things far worse, as McKinsey's obstruction plea showed [S390]. Escalate the same day.

D9 · Rung 3: AI draft cites a fake sourceHARD
Setup

A senior colleague drafted, with AI help, a section of a client report on operational resilience. It cites a regulation paragraph and a study you cannot find anywhere. The report is due tomorrow.

The trap

Quietly deleting the citations but keeping the claims, or assuming they exist because a senior wrote them.

What to say

Steady: search the primary sources: legislation.gov.uk, the regulator's site, the journal. Sort: the firm's name and client trust first. Act: "I can't verify two references in that section. I've checked the regulator's site and the journal; they may be AI errors. Can we find the real sources, or remove the claims they support?" Close: every citation verified and logged, the claims re-checked, and AI use disclosed if the contract requires it.

Why it works

In 2025 Deloitte Australia agreed to repay part of a roughly A$440,000 government report found to contain nonexistent references [S388] [Confirmed]. "Because an AI model cannot be meaningfully held accountable for its outputs, accountability is squarely a human domain" [S347]. The ICAEW Code warns against "undue reliance on ... technology" [S372].

The twist, and how to hold

"The point is right even if the reference is off. Cite something similar." Hold: a citation must support the exact claim. If no real source does, the claim goes, or is labelled as our judgement.

D10 · Teaming across the wider T&T practice

The duty: "you will be working with teams across our wider Technology and Transformation practice, understanding how we bring our range of technical expertise and combine them" [S2]. This is the matrix: offerings, industries, alliances and delivery centres.

D10 · Rung 1: Hand-off to the delivery centreEASY
Setup

On Friday you must hand a testing workstream to an offshore delivery-centre team six hours ahead of the UK. On Monday you move to another project.

The trap

A 6pm email saying "see attached", with context only you understand.

What to say

Steady: list what the new team needs to work without me. Sort: continuity for the client, then a written record. Act: "I'd write a handover pack: scope, status of each test, open defects with owners, environments and access, contacts, and what 'done' looks like. Then a call in the overlap window to walk through open defects, asking them to play back the top three priorities." Close: access through the proper request; a daily written update for the first week.

Why it works

Deloitte UK postings ask for clear communication "with clients and colleagues both on and offshore" [S51] [Confirmed]. Deloitte has opened UK technology centres in Belfast, Cardiff, Manchester and Newcastle [S323] [Reported]. "Collaborate for measurable impact" means "teaming across businesses, geographies, and skill sets" [S342].

The twist, and how to hold

The team lead has no client system access yet and asks to borrow your login. Hold: never. Using someone else's credentials is unauthorised access under the Computer Misuse Act [S363]. Chase the access request and tell your manager about the delay.

D10 · Rung 2: Alliance partner oversells the clientMEDIUM
Setup

In a joint client meeting, a software alliance partner's salesperson promises the client features that are not on the vendor's published product roadmap.

The trap

Contradicting the vendor in front of the client, or staying silent while the client plans around features that may never arrive.

What to say

Steady: no confrontation in the room. Sort: the client's interest and honest advice come before the alliance relationship. Act: in the room: "That's an important point. Could we capture an action to confirm the timing in writing?" Afterwards I tell my manager, with the roadmap reference, and suggest the plan and business case don't assume the feature until it is confirmed. Close: the action log shows the confirmation request, owner and date.

Why it works

Deloitte UK works with 14 alliance partners [S319] [Confirmed]. Typically the vendor sells the licence and Deloitte sells the implementation [Inferred], so clients must trust that the advice is independent. The Code requires objectivity and truthfulness [S340].

The twist, and how to hold

The rep messages you: "Don't rock the boat, we bring you a lot of work." Hold: pass it to your manager. The Code bans influence even "perceived to" affect judgement [S340].

D10 · Rung 3: Two practices, one client, conflictHARD
Setup

Your TST team is advising a central government department on reducing reliance on one outsourced IT service. You hear another Deloitte practice is bidding to run a managed service there, which could compete with, or gain from, your recommendation.

The trap

Passing your engagement's findings to the bid team "to help the firm", or ignoring what you heard.

What to say

Steady: separate what I know from what I heard. Sort: law and the firm's name first: procurement rules and client confidentiality. Act: "I wouldn't share anything from our engagement with the bid team. I'd tell my manager today what I heard, so the engagement partner and the firm's risk team can check whether there is a conflict to disclose or manage." Close: stay inside my engagement's boundaries; note when I raised it.

Why it works

Deloitte UK conflict-checks all proposed engagements and declines those it cannot manage [S35] [Confirmed]. The Procurement Act 2023 makes authorities manage conflicts, and a supplier with an unfair advantage can be excluded [S366, S367]. PwC Australia's tax leak shows the cost of reusing one client's information for another [S392]; McKinsey's FDA case shows non-disclosure is itself the failure [S391].

The twist, and how to hold

Over coffee, a bid-team colleague asks: "What's their real budget pressure?" Hold: "Sorry, I can't talk about my client's work. Best to go through the engagement partner." Then tell your manager.

SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

"When something goes wrong on a project, I steady myself and check the facts, then rank what's at stake: the law and the firm's name first, then the client and the people affected, then the process, and speed last. Then I take one concrete step with a named person and write it down. Your Code calls that ACT, and says speaking up is taking action." [S340]
