The scenario playbook: frame method, ladder, composure set, CV grill

One answer protocol and one priority order that work on any scenario Deloitte puts in front of you, from the ranking items in the online assessment to a director pushing back in the final interview. Then the eight ways interviewers test whether your answer holds, how to keep your body calm, the 30-scenario ladder, five hard scenarios worked in full, and a drill for surviving a three-levels-deep grilling on your CV.

Why scenarios decide this process

Deloitte UK tests judgement at every stage of its early-careers process. The Immersive Online Assessment (stage 2) has a behavioural part built on "realistic workplace scenarios" where you rank possible actions from most to least likely [S350] [Confirmed]. The Job Simulation (stage 3) adds ranking, selecting responses, written answers and recorded video answers. Deloitte says "some scenarios may refer to workplace tools like AI" and that it is interested in "how you think and make decisions ... and consider using AI responsibly in a professional setting, not your technical AI knowledge" [S350] [Confirmed]. The Final Stage skills and motivation interview, about 50 minutes, includes scenario-based questions according to TargetJobs [S260] [S263] [Reported].

So the same skill is scored three times, in three formats: a click-to-rank screen, a 2-minute recording, and a live conversation where someone can push back. This Part gives you one method that works in all three. It has two pieces. A protocol tells you what to say, in what order, in about a minute. A priority order tells you which concern wins when two good things collide. Learn both until they are automatic, because Deloitte's own advice on the ranking tasks is to "go with your gut instinct" [S260] [Confirmed]. The aim of practice is to train your gut, not to game the test.

KEY TERMS

Scenario question: "What would you do if...?" It asks about a situation you have not met, so it tests judgement rather than memory. Situational judgement test (SJT): the written or on-screen version, where you rank or pick from given options. Frame: the logic that holds your answer together (what matters most and why). An interviewer "breaks the frame" when they push until you drop that logic. Escalate: pass a decision to someone more senior or to a specialist team, openly and early.

Deloitte does not publish how its ranking items are scored or which "capabilities" the final interview scores against [S260]. The four-step answer protocol

Every scenario answer, spoken or written, follows four steps: steady, sort, act, close. A full spoken answer takes 45 to 75 seconds. That is long enough to show judgement and short enough to leave room for the follow-up, which is where most marks are won or lost [Inferred]. In a recorded video answer with 2 to 3 minutes available, use the same four steps and spend the extra time in step 3, on the twist you expect next.

Step 1: Steady (about 0 to 8 seconds)

Take one slow breath out. Then repeat the problem back in one line. This does two jobs. It buys you thinking time without dead air, and it shows the interviewer you heard the actual question rather than the one you hoped for. If a fact is missing that changes the answer, ask one question now, not five.

SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

"Let me make sure I've got this. The client wants the pack tonight, and the data in it isn't checked yet."

"Before I answer: is the client also a Deloitte audit client? That changes what I can do." (Use only when it really changes the answer.)

Step 2: Sort (about 8 to 25 seconds)

Say what is at stake, then rank it out loud using the priority order in the next section. Separate what you know from what you would need to find out. This is the step most candidates skip. They jump straight to action, which makes the answer sound like a reflex instead of a judgement. Deloitte's own Code tells its people to "consider all available information to assess the situation, use professional judgment, consult with relevant stakeholders as appropriate and, where necessary, take action" [S340] [Confirmed]. Step 2 is the "assess" part of that sentence.

SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

"Two things matter here. The first is client confidentiality, because that's a legal and Code issue. The second is the deadline. The first one decides how I do it. The second decides how fast."

Step 3: Act (about 25 to 60 seconds)

Give the first concrete step, the person you tell and the time by which you tell them. Then give the route that still meets the goal. An answer that only says "I would refuse" or "I would escalate" scores badly because it stops the work [Inferred]. Deloitte's Code calls its method ACT: Assess the situation, Consider and Consult, Take action. It adds: "Speaking up is taking action!" and "No one at Deloitte should have to navigate a difficult situation alone" [S340] [Confirmed]. So "I'd talk to my manager" is a proper action in Deloitte's own terms, as long as you also say what you would do meanwhile.

SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

"First I'd do the analysis in the firm's approved tool, not a public one. Then I'd tell my manager before 6pm that one section rests on an assumption, and offer to firm it up by 10am."

Step 4: Close (about 60 to 75 seconds)

State the outcome, the record you leave behind and the follow-up. Then stop. Silence after a clean close is fine. The record matters because it is what protects you, your manager and the client later. The McKinsey opioid case ended with a former senior partner agreeing to plead guilty to obstruction for deleting files [S390] [Confirmed]. A junior does not need that level of drama to learn the point: write it down.

SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

"So the client gets the pack on time, nothing confidential leaves the firm, and the email to my manager is the record. I'd check back at 5pm."

DON'T SAY THIS

"It depends." (Alone, it sounds like dodging. Say what it depends on, then answer both branches.) "I'd just do what the partner says." (Rank is not a reason.) "I'd report them straight to the regulator." (A junior escalates inside the firm first, unless the firm itself is the problem; Deloitte's Speak Up channel exists for that case [S340] [S343].) "I'd go above my manager's head immediately." (One level at a time, unless it is an ethics or legal matter [Inferred from S340].)

The priority order when good things collide

Most hard scenarios are not good against bad. They are two good things that cannot both win: the deadline and the check, the client's wish and the rule, your manager's request and the partner's. You need a fixed order, decided in advance, so you are not inventing your values under pressure. For this role the order is:

  1. Law, independence and the firm's name.
  2. The client's real interest and fair treatment of every stakeholder.
  3. The process and the record (change control, documentation, accurate timesheets, approved tools).
  4. Speed and convenience.

Each level beats every level below it. Within a level, use judgement. And when nothing above is at stake, speed wins: most of the job is simply getting good work done fast.

THE ONE SENTENCE THAT HOLDS EVERY FRAME

"I'll keep it legal and honest first, do right by the client second, follow the process and leave a record third, and go as fast as those three allow."

Say it to yourself before every scenario. Say it aloud only if an interviewer asks how you make these calls in general.

YOUR OPINION GOES HERE

Prompt: should the client's interest ever rank above the process? For: processes can be slow and a rigid junior can hurt a client by waiting for sign-off on something obvious. Against: the process exists because individuals under pressure misjudge the client's interest; change control protects the client from scope creep and surprise cost [S377]. Suggested stance: the client's interest ranks higher, which is why you raise a process that is hurting the client openly and fast. You do not quietly bypass it.

Ranking items: applying the order to multiple choice

In the Immersive Online Assessment you see a short workplace scenario and four or five possible actions. You rank them from the one you would most likely take to the one you would least likely take [S350] [Confirmed]. The Job Simulation includes the same kind of item, some about AI [S350]. Deloitte does not publish the scoring key. Typical SJT design, which this pack infers applies here, scores how close your order is to an expert order, so getting the top and bottom right matters most [Inferred].

The five-pass method

  1. Bottom first. Any option that breaches level 1 (shares confidential data, misstates facts, hides a problem, breaks a law) goes to the bottom. If two do, the one that does more harm, or is harder to undo, goes lowest.
  2. Then the do-nothing options. Ignoring the problem, or passing it on without a view, sits just above the breaches.
  3. Top next. The best option protects every level that is at stake and still moves the work forward. It usually involves acting and telling someone.
  4. Middle by level. Between two safe options, the one that serves a higher level ranks higher. If they serve the same level, prefer the one that consults at the right level and leaves a record.
  5. Gut check. Read your order top to bottom. Would you be comfortable explaining it to the partner and to the client? If not, swap.

Eight ways interviewers break a frame

A live interviewer listens to your first answer and then tests whether it holds. Deloitte's own recruiters say assessors "are not trying to trip you up or trick you" [S260] [Confirmed], so read the moves below as probes rather than hostility. Most are the natural follow-ups a manager would ask if you gave that answer at work. Your job is to stay in the frame: keep the same priority order, change your plan only if new facts justify it, and stay pleasant.

TacticWhat they are really testingHow to hold, and what not to do
1. InterruptionCan you stay organised when your structure is broken? Do you get flustered or defensive?Finish one sentence at most, then go where they lead. Do not restart your whole answer later. If the interruption was the more important question, say so: "Good point, that's the bigger issue."
2. Flat contradictionDo you cave the moment a senior person disagrees? Or do you dig in without listening?Name what evidence would change your view, then keep your view if that evidence is absent. Caving loses the frame; arguing loses the room. Integrity in the Code means "straightforward and honest in our professional opinions" [S340].
3. SilenceWill you fill the gap by retracting, over-explaining or adding a worse idea?Silence is not a signal you were wrong. Count three seconds, then offer to go deeper on one part. Never add "or I suppose I could just do what they asked".
4. EscalationDoes your answer survive higher stakes and more senior pressure?The higher the stakes, the higher the person you consult, not the faster you give in. Deloitte's Code says retaliation against those who raise concerns "is not tolerated" [S340].
5. Authority squeezeWill rank alone change your answer?Separate the goal from the method. Agree to the goal, decline the method, offer an alternative with a time. Seniority changes who decides, not what the law says.
6. False choiceCan you see a third option under pressure?There is almost always a third route: deliver part on time, flag the rest, raise it early. Name it. If there truly is no third route, choose by the priority order and say so.
7. Knowledge trapWill you bluff?Say you do not know the detail, state the principle you do know, and say who you would check with. Bluffing about law in an interview is exactly the behaviour they fear on a client site.
8. Invitation to badmouthWill you blame others, and will you talk about people behind their backs?Decline the bait warmly. Talk only about what you did and would do differently. The Code applies to behaviour "both inside and outside of the Deloitte workplace" [S340], and assessors read badmouthing as a preview of how you will talk about clients.
SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

When you genuinely change your mind after a push: "That's a fair point I hadn't weighed. With that fact, I'd change step two to X. The rest stays the same." Changing a step because of a new fact is judgement. Changing your values because of a frown is losing the frame.

Staying calm physically: what the evidence supports

A frame holds only if your body lets your working memory work. Three techniques have peer-reviewed evidence behind them. None is magic, and each study tested something slightly different from a Deloitte interview, so each comes with a caveat.

TechniqueWhat the study foundHow to use itCaveat
Cyclic sighing (exhale-focused breathing)Balban et al. 2023, Cell Reports Medicine: a one-month randomised study of 5-minute daily practices. Cyclic sighing gave more improvement in mood and a bigger drop in breathing rate than mindfulness meditation (p < 0.05) [S385] [Confirmed].Practise 5 minutes a day in the weeks before. On the day, do a few rounds in the waiting room: a full breath in through the nose, a short second top-up breath, then a long slow breath out through the mouth.The study measured daily practice over a month, not one breath before an interview. One author disclosed an advisory role with WHOOP [S385]. A few slow exhales are low cost and low risk [Inferred].

Saying "I am excited" out loud worked [S384] [Confirmed]. Jamieson et al. 2010: students told that arousal improves performance did better on GRE maths, including on the real test 1 to 3 months later [S386] [Confirmed].Do not tell yourself to calm down. Say "I'm excited" quietly before you go in. Treat a racing heart as fuel. Both states are high arousal, so relabelling is easier than switching off [S384].Lab and test settings, mostly with students. The composure routine on the day [Inferred, built on S384, S385, S387]

  1. That morning: 10 minutes of expressive writing about your worries. Bin the page.
  2. In the waiting room: three to five cyclic sighs. Then say "I'm excited" quietly to yourself.
  3. In the room: use the four-step protocol. A fixed structure means your working memory goes on the content, not on organising it.
  4. When pushed: breathe out before you answer. One exhale is your "steady" step.

The scenario ladder: every JD duty line, three rungs

The ladder turns each of the ten duty lines in the Technology Strategy & Transformation graduate job description (D1 to D10, decoded in Part 1) into three practice scenarios of rising difficulty [S2] [S3]. The easy rung is a craft task: can you do the work well? The medium rung adds a stakeholder pulling against you. The hard rung puts an ethical or legal line in play, so the priority order decides the answer. The full 30 cards follow this section, in the same order as the grid.

HOW TO PRACTISE THE LADDER

Practise one row per day for ten days. Say each answer aloud, timed, using the four steps. Then have a friend play the interviewer and use two of the eight tactics on your hard-rung answer. Record yourself once a week; the Job Simulation is recorded, so you need to be comfortable talking to a camera [S260].

The composure set: five hard role-specific scenarios

These five are the scenarios where candidates most often lose the frame, because each pits a senior person, a deadline or their own pride against the priority order. Each card gives the setup, the trap, the answer, why it works, the twist an interviewer will add, and a decision tree. Figure 30 draws the trees for the two highest-stakes cards. The other three trees are written out in the cards.

Composure set 1 · Two seniors, one deadlineHARD
Setup

It is 1pm. The engagement partner asks you for a cut of the cost baseline for a 5pm call with the client CFO. Ten minutes later the client's IT director, whom you sit next to on site, asks you to rebuild the application inventory for her 5pm meeting with her own boss. Each task is about four hours. Your manager is on leave today.

The trap

Saying yes to both and failing one at 4.55pm. Or picking by rank ("the partner wins") without telling the other person. Or picking by proximity (the client is sitting next to you).

What to say

"I can't do both properly by 5. Both matter, so I wouldn't choose silently. I'd tell both of them now, within ten minutes, and ask whoever owns my priorities on this engagement to decide. With my manager away, that's the engagement lead. While I wait, I'd start the piece that is client-facing at board level, the CFO call, and I'd tell the IT director what I can give her by 5: a cleaned version of the existing inventory with the gaps marked. Then I'd confirm the decision in a two-line email."

Why it works

It applies the order: nothing in level 1 is at stake, so the call is level 2 (the client's interest), then level 3 (the record), then speed. It follows the Code's encouragement to consult "others in their chain of command" rather than deciding alone [S340]. It respects the idea that one person is accountable for a decision, as in a RACI matrix [S379]. It offers a partial delivery instead of a silent miss, which is "Collaborate for measurable impact" in practice [S342].

The twist, and how to hold

"Both of them say: don't bother anyone, just do mine." Hold: "I understand. I'll give you an honest time instead of a hopeful one. I'd rather tell you now that 5pm isn't possible for both than tell you at 4.55." Then you still tell the engagement lead, because a missed client deadline is their risk too.

Decision tree

Can both be done well by the deadline? Yes: do both, confirm times. No: tell both people now. Then: is there an owner of your priorities you can reach? Yes: they decide; start the more client-critical task meanwhile. No: choose by the priority order (legal or board-facing first), offer the other person a partial deliverable with a time, and write it down.

Composure set 2 · Asked to skip a controlHARD
Setup

It is 7pm before a steering committee. A director on your team says: "The firm's AI tool is too slow. Paste the client's survey export into ChatGPT, get me the themes, and we're done by eight." The export contains client employees' names and comments. Variants an interviewer may use instead: "Backdate your timesheet so last week's hours go on the new code", or "Skip the conflict check, we start on Monday regardless".

The trap

Complying because a director asked. Or refusing with a lecture and no alternative, which leaves the director stranded and makes you look rigid.

What to say

"I want to get you those themes tonight. I can't put client data into a public tool, because that's disclosing it outside the firm. What I can do is run it in our approved tool or theme it by hand from the top 200 comments, and you'll have it by 8.30. If 8.30 is too late, I'll tell the engagement partner now so the timing risk isn't on you alone."

Why it works

Deloitte's Code prohibits "disclosure of confidential and personal information entrusted to us unless granted permission" [S340]. The JD itself specifies "firm-approved AI tools" [S3]. Deloitte UK runs GenAI through use-case approval and gives staff secure tools that analyse confidential data "within its secure digital ecosystem" [S345]. Personal data brings UK GDPR, whose principles include integrity and confidentiality [S353]. For the timesheet variant, the Code says "We record hours worked timely and accurately" [S340], and since September 2025 a firm can be criminally liable if an associate commits fraud intended to benefit it or its client [S361] [S362]. For the conflict-check variant, Deloitte UK says "all proposed engagements ... go through a conflict check before they are accepted" [S345].

The twist, and how to hold

"Everyone does it. I'm the director and I'm telling you to." Hold: "I believe you that it's common. I still can't do it, and I'd rather be the person who got you a slightly later answer than the person who caused a data incident on your engagement. Do you want me to raise it with the partner, or shall I just crack on with the approved route?" If the pressure continues, the Code's route is to consult the chain of command or Ethics, and retaliation for raising concerns "is not tolerated" [S340].

Composure set 3 · Overheard deal talkHARD
Setup

You are queuing for coffee near the office. Two people you recognise from another Deloitte team are discussing, fairly loudly, a "Project Falcon" and that a FTSE 250 company you know is "being bought at a big premium next month". You are not on that work. A friend who works in finance is texting you about shares that evening.

The trap

Treating it as gossip. Telling your friend "I can't say, but I wouldn't sell". Trading yourself. Or pretending you heard nothing and doing nothing.

What to say

"I'd assume it could be inside information, so I wouldn't trade, tip or repeat it to anyone, including friends. I'd tell my manager or the Risk or Ethics team, briefly, what I heard, where and when, because a leak in a public place is something the firm needs to know about, and they may need to manage it. I wouldn't confront the colleagues myself or try to judge whether it was price-sensitive. That's Risk's call."

Why it works

UK MAR defines inside information as "information of a precise nature, which has not been made public ... which, if it were made public, would be likely to have a significant effect on the prices" [S358]. It bans insider dealing, recommending it, and unlawful disclosure [S360]. Disclosure is lawful only "in the normal exercise of an employment, a profession or duties" [S359], so need-to-know sharing inside the engagement team is fine; telling a friend is not. Deloitte's Code prohibits "the use of confidential information about our clients for personal advantage or for the benefit of third parties" [S340] and asks everyone to report suspected noncompliance whoever is involved [S340].

The twist, and how to hold

"Your friend says: just tell me if I should hold or sell." Hold: "I can't talk about clients, and please don't ask me about shares." Even a hint is a recommendation. "The colleagues are senior. Won't reporting them damage your career?" Hold: "The Code says retaliation isn't tolerated [S340]. And I'm not accusing anyone. I'm reporting a risk so the firm can deal with it."

Composure set 4 · Your AI-assisted figure was wrongHARD
Setup

Yesterday your team sent a client a benefits analysis. You built one section with help from the firm's approved AI tool. This morning you notice the tool had combined two years of savings into one, so a headline figure is overstated by about a third. The client has not mentioned it. Your manager is in back-to-back meetings.

The trap

Quietly fixing the file and hoping. Blaming the tool. Or panicking and emailing the client directly before telling your team.

What to say

"I'd message my manager now, short and factual: the figure, the correct figure, how it happened, and that it's my section. I'd suggest a correction note to the client today, with the corrected page. While they decide, I'd re-check every other number I produced with the tool, so we only correct once. Afterwards I'd add a step to my own checklist: any AI-assisted number gets traced back to its source before it leaves my hands."

Why it works

Deloitte's own guidance says "an AI model cannot be meaningfully held accountable for its outputs, accountability is squarely a human domain" [S347]. The Code's integrity rule and duty to report apply [S340]. The ICAEW Code names "undue reliance on ... technology" as a threat to objectivity [S372]. And the firm has lived this: Deloitte Australia agreed to repay part of a A$440,000 fee after an AI-assisted government report was found to contain nonexistent references and an erroneous summary of a court case [S388] [Confirmed].

The twist, and how to hold

"Your manager says: nobody will notice, just fix it in the next version." Hold: "I'd rather the client hears it from us today than finds it at their board. If the decision is to correct it in the next version, I'd want that decision written down and the client told the figure is being revised." That keeps level 2 (the client's real interest) above level 4 (convenience).

Decision tree

Has the wrong figure left the firm? No: fix it, tell your manager, check related numbers. Yes: tell your manager now. Then: could the client act on it before a correction? Yes: push for a same-day correction note. No: agree a correction route and date, in writing. In every branch: never fix silently, never blame the tool, re-check everything else you touched.

Composure set 5 · Deadline with half the dataHARD
Setup

A steering committee pack is due to the client at 9am. One input, the licence costs from the client's procurement team, has not arrived. Without it, the total cost of the preferred option could be out by 10 to 20 per cent. It is 10pm, and procurement are not answering.

The trap

Guessing the number and presenting it as fact. Or holding the whole pack back, so the committee meets with nothing.

What to say

"I'd deliver on time with what's solid and make the gap impossible to miss. I'd use last year's licence figure as a labelled assumption, show the total as a range, and put a line on the summary slide saying which number is provisional and when it'll be firm. I'd tell my manager tonight, so they aren't surprised in the meeting, and I'd log it as an assumption and a dependency in the RAID log. First thing tomorrow I'd chase procurement through the client's programme lead."

Why it works

The Code asks for being "straightforward and honest in our professional opinions" [S340]: a clearly labelled assumption is honest, a silent guess is not. Representing an estimate as fact to win the next phase could even become a fraud risk [S361]. A RAID log (risks, assumptions, issues, dependencies) exists for exactly this, and APM notes that issues can turn into change requests [S377]. The pyramid principle, leading with the answer and then the support, still works with a range [S374].

The twist, and how to hold

"The client sponsor says: the board hates ranges, give me one number." Hold: "I can give you one central number on the slide, with the range and the assumption in a footnote. If the board asks, you'll have the answer ready." You give the sponsor what they need without hiding the uncertainty from the board.

Decision tree

Can you get the missing fact before the deadline? Yes: get it. No: would the gap change the recommendation? Yes: tell your manager tonight and agree whether to delay that one decision, not the whole pack. No: deliver on time with a labelled assumption and a range, log it, and chase the fact in the morning.

The CV grill: holding your ground three levels deep

The skills and motivation interview will include "tell me about a time" questions and probably "run me through your CV" [S260] [S289]. A good interviewer does not stop at your first answer. They dig three levels deep on one line of your CV, and the frame you have to hold is simple: every level must match the one above it. If your first sentence inflates, the third level will find it.

Level 1: the claim

"Tell me about your role as society treasurer." They hear your headline and pick the word that sounds biggest ("led", "transformed", "managed").

Level 2: the mechanism

"How exactly did you do that? Why that approach?" They test whether you did it or watched it, and whether you understood why it worked.

Level 3: the proof and the counterfactual

"How do you know it was you? What would have happened without you? What did it cost?" They test honesty and self-awareness.

Prospects, the UK careers service, recommends the STARR structure (situation, task, action, result, reflection), "five to eight STARR examples", and a focus on "what you specifically did and decided", with tangible outcomes [S382] [Confirmed]. Deloitte's Code adds the integrity rule that governs the whole grill: "We are truthful about ... the knowledge we possess, and the experience we have" [S340]. In Deloitte's terms, claiming more than you did is an integrity question.

The four-line drill for every CV bullet

Before any interview, take every bullet on your CV and write four lines under it. If you cannot fill all four, either cut the bullet or go and find the evidence. This is how you survive level 3.

  1. Claim: the one sentence you would say first, with an honest verb (built, ran, wrote, fixed, persuaded).
  2. Evidence: what proves it, and who could confirm it (a file, minutes, a reference, a link).
  3. Number: the one figure that shows scale or result, and how you know it. If none, the honest proxy.
  4. Lesson: what you would do differently, and where you have since applied it.

One-page summary of this Part

  • Protocol: steady, sort, act, close. 45 to 75 seconds. Deloitte's ACT method (Assess; Consider and Consult; Take action) sits inside it [S340].
  • Priority order: law, independence and the firm's name; the client's real interest and fairness; the process and the record; speed. "Legal and honest first, the client second, process and record third, as fast as those allow."
  • Ranking items: bottom first (breaches), then do-nothing options, then the top (protects everything at stake and moves the work), then the middle by level.
  • Frame-breakers: change a step for a new fact, never your values for a frown.
  • Composure: write out worries, sigh out slowly, call it excitement [S384] [S385] [S387].
  • CV grill: Claim, Evidence, Number, Lesson for every bullet.
