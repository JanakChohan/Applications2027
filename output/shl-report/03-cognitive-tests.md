## What you are actually being asked

The Verify range is not a maths test or a vocabulary test. It is a **reasoning** test that uses numbers, patterns and rules as the raw material. Every item is solvable from what is on the screen; nothing requires outside knowledge, and nothing requires memorised formulas beyond arithmetic you already have. Understanding that framing is worth several marks on its own, because the commonest failure mode is candidates trying to *recall* rather than *derive*.

There are two generations in circulation, and you should know which you are sitting because the strategy differs slightly. The **legacy Verify G+** is a fixed-ish form of roughly **30 questions in 36 minutes**, split about evenly across numerical, inductive and deductive reasoning. The **current Verify Interactive G+** is shorter and adaptive — about **24 questions in 36 minutes** — and uses drag-and-drop and other interactive item formats rather than plain multiple choice. `[PREP-VENDOR — multiple agreeing sources]` Both report sub-scores for the three reasoning types plus an overall "g" estimate.

## The three question types, in detail

### Numerical reasoning — data interpretation under time pressure

**What an item looks like.** A table, chart or small dashboard of figures — often financial or commercial in flavour — followed by a question requiring you to extract the right values and perform one or two operations. Typical operations: percentage change, share of total, ratio, rate conversion, simple projection.

**The three patterns that cover most items.** Internalise these until they are reflexes, because at roughly a minute an item you cannot afford to re-derive them:

1. **Percentage change** = (new − old) / old. Its relatives are where candidates lose marks: *percentage of* (part/whole), *percentage-point difference* (a change in two percentages, not a percentage change), and *reverse percentage* (a figure is £120 after a 20% rise → divide by 1.2, not multiply by 0.8).
2. **Two-step extraction.** The answer is a ratio or difference between two cells you must first *locate* — often across different rows, columns or years. **Locate before you calculate**; the arithmetic is trivial, the finding is where the time goes.
3. **Unit and scale traps.** The table is in thousands or millions; a rate is annual but the question is quarterly; two columns use different currencies. Read the axis labels, column headers and footnotes *first*.

**The distractor logic.** Wrong answer options are not random — they are constructed from plausible wrong routes: the right operation on the wrong cells, the wrong operation on the right cells, or the correct answer at the wrong scale. This means **an answer "appearing in the options" is no confirmation you're right**, and it means that if your figure matches an option exactly you should still sanity-check the units.

**Calculator and paper.** SHL's numerical items generally permit a calculator and rough paper because they test reasoning, not arithmetic — but the *employer* can override this, and the interactive formats are increasingly built so a calculator helps less than you'd expect. ### Inductive reasoning — finding the hidden rule

**What an item looks like.** A sequence or set of abstract figures governed by a rule you must infer, then apply — either to continue the sequence, complete a matrix, or identify the odd one out.

**The method that works.** Do not stare at the whole item hoping for a gestalt. Run a **dimension checklist**, one at a time: **shape, number, size, shading/colour, position, orientation/rotation**. Most items encode **two simultaneous rules** — a shape rotates *and* a count increments — and the classic failure is spotting one rule, finding an option that satisfies it, and moving on. If exactly one option satisfies your rule, check it satisfies the *second* dimension too.

**Odd-one-out is a different mental operation.** You are not finding a pattern in the target; you are finding the rule the *other* items share and identifying the breaker. Candidates routinely conflate the two and lose easy marks.

### Deductive reasoning — reasoning validly from given rules

**What an item looks like.** A set of premises, constraints or rules — sometimes verbal, sometimes a seating/ordering puzzle, sometimes conditional logic — from which exactly one conclusion follows.

**The method.** **Externalise the logic**: sketch the ordering, draw the if-then arrows, tabulate the constraints. Holding four constraints in working memory while evaluating five options is how errors happen. Then test each option against *every* constraint, not just the one that first seems relevant.

**The conditional trap.** "If A then B" implies "if not B then not A" (the contrapositive — valid, and the most commonly missed inference). It does **not** imply "if B then A" (the converse — invalid, and the most commonly *committed* error). If you learn one piece of formal logic before an SHL test, learn this pair.

**Verbal items and "cannot say."** Where SHL uses verbal-critical items, the discipline is strict entailment: **"cannot say" is correct whenever the passage does not entail the statement**, even when the statement is obviously true in the real world. Answer only from what is on the screen.

## The adaptive layer — and why harder means better

The Interactive range delivers items adaptively using item-response theory: answering correctly pushes the next item harder and worth more; answering wrongly eases it. Three consequences follow.

**First, your raw count is not comparable to anyone else's.** Two candidates with the same number correct can receive very different scores if they climbed different difficulty ladders. The model estimates an underlying ability level (a "theta") from both *how many* and *how hard*.

**Second, difficulty rising is a success signal, not a failure signal.** Candidates who don't know this read the ramp as evidence they're failing and panic in the last third — a wholly self-inflicted wound.

**Third, early items matter more.** Because early answers steer your difficulty path, a careless slip at item three costs more than the same slip at item twenty. **Start deliberately**, then find your rhythm.

## Pacing: the whole-test clock

SHL's clock covers the whole test, which makes pacing a resource-allocation problem rather than a series of independent sprints. On a 24-item/36-minute test that is **~90 seconds per item**; on a 30-item/36-minute test, **~72 seconds**. Practical rules:

- **Set a per-item budget and hold it.** Rescuing one hard item for three minutes costs you two later items — a guaranteed net loss.
- **Never leave a blank.** There is no evidence of negative marking on Verify, so an unanswered item and a wrong item score identically: guess a reasoned option and move. (This is the exact opposite of the Aon rule — see the Aon report — and importing the wrong habit between vendors is a real and avoidable way to lose marks.)
- **Bank the easy marks first** where navigation permits; on the adaptive version you generally cannot go back, so commit and move.
- **Leave a small buffer.** Finishing with two minutes spare and every item attempted beats finishing perfectly on twenty items and blank on four.

## The practice items — always do them

Every SHL delivery offers untimed practice items before the clock starts. **Do them every single time, even if you've sat the test before.** They are the only rehearsal of the exact interface you will face, and on the Interactive range the drag-and-drop mechanics are genuinely fiddly the first time. Losing thirty seconds on test day to work out a control is a pure, avoidable loss. SHL also hosts free practice materials on its candidate site (shldirect.com) — the closest available match to the real thing. `[VENDOR]`

## What good preparation actually looks like

Not "do a hundred practice tests." The productive sequence is: sit one full timed test cold to find your weakest construct and your pacing gap; drill that construct in short daily sets with an **error log** recording *why* each miss happened (misread the question / wrong quantity / units / arithmetic slip / ran out of time); re-test under full conditions weekly. Errors cluster, and the cluster is your syllabus. The six-week programme in the companion guide (Chapter 6.5) operationalises this in detail.
