# Chapter 4 — The OPQ: SHL's Personality Questionnaire, in Full

> ### ⚡ At a glance — OPQ32r (the current standard)
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **Format** | **104 blocks of three statements.** In each block you pick the one **most like you** and the one **least like you** — equivalently, "most of the three, then most of the remaining two" |
> | **Length** | **Untimed**, but **pacing is monitored**; most candidates finish in **25–35 minutes** |
> | **Measures** | **32 workplace-relevant traits** across Relationships with People, Thinking Style, and Feelings & Emotions |
> | **Scoring** | **Thurstonian item-response theory** — your rankings become ~312 pairwise comparisons, producing **normative** (candidate-comparable) trait estimates |
> | **Integrity checks** | A **consistency indicator**; the forced-choice design itself defeats "fake good"; the normative OPQ32n variant adds an explicit **Social Desirability scale** |
> | **Right answers?** | **None.** There is no pass mark — your profile is compared against a role profile the employer defined |

---

## The three generations — know which you are sitting

- **OPQ32i** *(legacy)* — blocks of **four** statements, most/least. This is what older prep material describes, and why you'll see "blocks of four" in guides that haven't been updated.
- **OPQ32r** *(current standard)* — blocks of **three**, 104 of them. SHL derived it from OPQ32i by removing one statement per block: faster, less cognitively taxing, and with **equal or better validity** thanks to the IRT scoring model. `[INDEPENDENT — OPQ32r development literature]`
- **OPQ32n** *(normative variant)* — you rate each statement independently on a scale rather than ranking them. Longer (45–60 minutes) and includes an explicit **Social Desirability scale**. Less common in graduate hiring.

If your test presents **three statements and asks for most/least like you**, you are sitting the OPQ32r. That is the version this chapter is built around.

## What the questions look like

Each block presents three short self-descriptive statements, and — critically — **they are matched for desirability**. You will not be asked to choose between "I work hard" and "I am lazy." You will be asked to choose between things like:

> *I enjoy persuading others to my point of view* · *I pay close attention to detail* · *I stay calm when plans change*

All three are attractive. All three describe traits an employer might want. That is the entire design: **you cannot inflate every trait at once, because every choice to elevate one is a choice to suppress another.** Across 104 blocks, the pattern of your trade-offs — not any individual answer — is the measurement.

The statements load onto SHL's **32 traits**, grouped into three domains: **Relationships with People** (persuasive, controlling, outspoken, socially confident, democratic, caring…), **Thinking Style** (data rational, evaluative, conceptual, innovative, forward thinking, detail conscious, conscientious…), and **Feelings and Emotions** (relaxed, worrying, tough minded, optimistic, competitive, achieving, decisive…). You are never told which trait a statement measures — and, importantly, **you often cannot reliably tell**, which matters for strategy below.

## How the ranking becomes a score — Thurstonian IRT

This is the part almost no prep site explains, and it is the key to answering well.

When you rank three statements, you have implicitly made **three pairwise judgements**: most > middle, most > least, and middle > least. Across 104 blocks, that is roughly **312 pairwise comparisons**. A **Thurstonian item-response model** takes all of them and triangulates where you sit on each of the 32 traits. `[INDEPENDENT — the Brown & Maydeu-Olivares Thurstonian-IRT work underpinning OPQ32r]`

The crucial consequence: because each trait appears in **many blocks, paired against different competing traits**, the model can recover **normative scores** — genuinely comparable between candidates — from a purely comparative task. Older forced-choice tests were "ipsative": they could only tell you which of *your own* traits was strongest, not how you compared to anyone else. The OPQ32r is not, and this is why it can legitimately be used for selection.

For you, one structural fact matters most: **every trait is tested repeatedly, in shifting company.** A drive-loaded statement meets sociability in one block, composure in another, detail-focus in a third. Your answers are cross-checked against each other automatically, by design.

## Is it timed? The pacing question

The questionnaire is **untimed** — but **response pacing is recorded**, and SHL's guidance is to answer thoughtfully but briskly. Candidates report in-test prompts when answering unusually slowly or unusually quickly. `[PREP-VENDOR + CANDIDATE, n=1]`

The two tails signal different problems:

- **Too fast** pattern-matches to careless or random responding — which also degrades your consistency indicator, since coherent trade-offs require actually reading the statements.
- **Too slow** signals over-deliberation. SHL's own guidance notes that over-thought responding produces a **flatter, less differentiated profile** — which is worse for you, because a flat profile gives the employer nothing to match against their role model.

The target is a **steady conversational rhythm of roughly 10–20 seconds per block**: read all three statements, first-instinct the "most," take the easier binary on the remaining two, move on.

## The strategy: an authentic, role-calibrated hierarchy

The most common candidate question is whether to decide in advance which traits to prioritise and then hold that ordering consistently. **Mechanically, yes — that is exactly right.** But with one amendment that decides whether it works or backfires.

**Why the hierarchy approach is mechanically correct.** Because each trait is re-tested across many blocks in different combinations, the profile that scores cleanly is one expressing a **stable underlying ordering**. If drive genuinely sits above sociability for you, it should beat sociability in *every* block where they meet. A pre-decided hierarchy gives you an instant tiebreaker for the hard desirable-versus-desirable blocks, which does two things at once: it makes your ~312 comparisons **cohere** (precisely what the consistency indicator measures), and it keeps you **decisive** (protecting your pacing).

**The amendment: the hierarchy must be your real one, calibrated to the role — not an invented ideal.** Build it by taking the employer's published competency language (drive/achievement, analytical rigour, conscientiousness/detail, resilience/composure, teamwork, influence) and honestly ranking **which of those you actually have most of**. That is a different exercise from ranking what you think they want most.

An invented hierarchy fails for three compounding reasons:

1. **You cannot reliably classify the statements.** Is "I get things finished" conscientiousness, drive, or achievement orientation? Across 104 engineered trade-offs you will misclassify a meaningful number — and each misclassification pushes your fabricated ordering off course, producing exactly the incoherence the model flags. Your *authentic* preferences, by contrast, express themselves consistently for free, without you having to identify what anything measures.
2. **You are optimising against an invisible target.** The employer's role profile weights traits in ways you cannot see — and "more of every good thing" is not what these profiles look like. A profile that is maximal on everything is not an ideal candidate; it is an implausible one.
3. **The profile follows you into the room.** At many firms your OPQ report seeds the interviewers' probe areas. A profile claiming high detail-consciousness invites detail questions; if it was fabricated, you meet them cold.

**The elegant design point:** the prepared-authentic candidate is *naturally* fast and consistent, while the fabricating candidate is *structurally* slow and incoherent. Honesty is not just the ethical strategy here — it is the fast one.

## The playbook

1. **Before the test (20 minutes).** Read the employer's competency framework. Write down your genuine **top-5 trait ordering in their vocabulary**, and recall one real example of each. This anchors your confidence in the ranking and doubles as interview preparation, since the same profile follows you.
2. **In-test.** First instinct on "most"; quick binary on the remaining two. When a block is hard because **both remaining statements are true of you**, apply your hierarchy as the tiebreaker. When a block is hard because **neither is like you**, pick the least-unlike and move — the model handles it across 104 blocks.
3. **Never.** Don't mid-block strategise about what a statement measures. Don't reverse an earlier trade-off because a block "feels" like it wants something different. Don't race a clock that isn't running.
4. **Avoid the mid-point trap.** On any rating-style variant (OPQ32n), clustering everything in the middle reads as evasive and produces the flat profile that helps nobody — least of all you.
5. **Afterwards.** Note what you presented. If you reach interview, your profile is in the room with you.

## What the employer receives

A **profile across the 32 traits**, typically expressed in **stens** (a 1–10 standardised scale) against a chosen norm group, often accompanied by competency-mapped narrative reports and — where the employer has configured one — a **fit indication against a role profile**. There is no pass mark in the instrument itself; the interpretation, and any threshold, belong to the employer (Chapters 6 and 7). Some SHL reports also generate **suggested interview probes** from your profile — which is precisely why authenticity pays downstream.

## Adjustments

The OPQ is untimed, so extra-time adjustments are less relevant than on the cognitive tests — but if a condition affects reading, processing or sustained concentration across 104 blocks, that is a legitimate basis for requesting an adjustment. Request it **in writing, in advance, through the employer**, under the Equality Act 2010; the companion guide's Chapter 6.7 carries the template.
