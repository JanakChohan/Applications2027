# Chapter 6 — Personality & Motivation: shapes, views, motive.q and ADEPT-15

Aon runs two personality lineages side by side: the **cut-e questionnaire family** (shapes, views, motive.q) built on its "adalloc" allocation format, and the US-lineage **ADEPT-15** inherited from Aon Hewitt. They share one strategic truth — **none of them can be beaten by picking "good" answers, because none of them scores answers as good or bad** — but their mechanics differ enough to matter.

---

## shapes — the adaptive personality questionnaire

**What it is.** cut-e's flagship personality instrument, and mechanically the most unusual in this report. Each page presents a **block of three statements**, and you **allocate six points across the three** to weight how well each describes you — cut-e's patented **adalloc™** ("Adaptive Allocation of Consent") format. It is a *self-description* questionnaire covering work behaviours, potentials and competencies. `[VENDOR — the cut-e vendor deck, verbatim descriptions]`

Two design features most candidates never learn:

- **It is adaptive — in a personality test.** Which statements get grouped together on later pages **depends on your earlier answers**: the engine re-pairs statements to sharpen discrimination between traits you rated similarly, which is how "a highly sophisticated profile" comes from "a very short questionnaire" (~15–20 minutes, no per-item clock). `[VENDOR]`
- **Even the personality items are randomly drawn** from an item pool — per-candidate variation extends beyond the ability tests. `[VENDOR deck — "random item selection and adalloc™ measuring technology"; note this corrects prep-vendor claims that only aptitude tests randomise]`

**The six variants — verified from the vendor's own deck** `[VENDOR]`:

| Variant | Scales | Items per scale | Built for |
|---|---|---|---|
| shapes **basic** | 15 | 6 | Administrative staff & apprentices |
| shapes **graduate** | **18** | 6 | **Graduates (the one you'll meet)** |
| shapes **sales** | 24 | 6 | Sales / direct customer contact |
| shapes **expert** | 18 | 8 | Specialists without management duties |
| shapes **management** | 18 | 8 | Middle/senior management |
| shapes **executive** | 24 | 8 | Senior management |

The management variant's dimensions give the flavour of what's measured: Influence, Networking, Execution, Systematic approach, Constructive teamwork, Vision and strategy, Initiative and responsibility, Steadiness, Bottom-line focus, Analysis and judgement, Innovation, Self-development, and so on. `[VENDOR deck]`

**How it is marked.** Your allocations build a **trait profile**, which is matched against a **role or "ideal candidate" competency profile** chosen by the employer. No absolute pass/fail; fit is the output. `[VENDOR structure + PREP-VENDOR]` The anti-faking control is **built into the format**: six points across three statements is zero-sum — you *cannot* rate everything "very me" — and the adaptive re-pairing keeps re-testing the same traits in fresh combinations, so a fabricated persona has to be maintained consistently across dozens of shifting trade-offs. A named lie/consistency index is `[UNKNOWN — not documented]`; the format is the control. One prep source adds the practical deterrent: distorted answers "might get further questions at a later stage for verification" — your interview panel reads this profile. `[PREP-VENDOR]`

**How to win.** Decide before you start which authentic version of yourself fits the role (read the firm's competency language), then **allocate decisively and consistently from that persona**. Wild point-splitting (2-2-2 on every page) produces a flat, incoherent profile; so does flipping persona mid-test. The trade-off format actually favours the honest candidate: real preferences are easy to reproduce consistently for twenty minutes, fabricated ones are not.

## views — values, motives and interests

**What it is.** The person–culture fit questionnaire: **18 value characteristics in three areas** — **Objectives** (professional challenge, recognition, financial reward, development, fun at work, identification), **Relationships** (harmony, honesty, cooperativeness, security, fairness, hierarchy), and **Environment** (structure, rate of change, integrity, absence of stress, influence, working environment). **108 items in blocks of three = 36 pages**, same six-point adalloc allocation, 15–20 minutes, adaptive like shapes. `[VENDOR deck — verbatim value model]`

**How it is marked.** A **preferences profile** matched against the employer's culture/values profile. Genuinely no right answers — this is two-way matching, and it is the one instrument where "gaming" is most obviously self-defeating: talking your way into a culture you'd hate is a cost you pay later, in the job. **How to approach it:** answer honestly and let it work as the fit-check it is.

## ADEPT-15 — Aon's US-lineage adaptive personality test

**What it is.** Aon's other personality flagship: **15 personality "aspects"** (ten mapping to the Big Five plus five leadership-relevant additions — names include Awareness, Positivity, Liveliness, Sensitivity, Drive), grouped into six higher-order styles (Task, Adaptation, Achievement, Interaction, Emotional, Teamwork). Delivered as a **computerised-adaptive test**: pairs of statements, and you indicate **which you agree with more and how strongly** (a slider between the pair), across roughly 25–30 minutes, usually untimed. The version in Aon's own technical documentation was **7.1 (November 2022)**. `[VENDOR + INDEPENDENT — the ACLU model cards, which cite Aon's technical documentation]`

**How it is marked — the deepest psychometrics in the catalogue.** The forced-choice pairs are scored with **IRT models — MUPP (Multi-Unidimensional Pairwise Preference) and GGUM (Generalized Graded Unfolding Model)** — and the adaptive algorithm picks each next pair based on your prior responses. `[VENDOR technical documentation, corroborated across academic and Aon sources]` Output: scores on all 15 aspects (or an employer-configured subset), which can be composited with other instruments — including gridChallenge — into ranked candidate lists. `[INDEPENDENT — ACLU citing Aon docs]` The faking-resistance argument is the standard forced-choice one, with independent meta-analytic support at the *format* level: desirability-matched pairs remove the "agree with everything nice" strategy, and adaptivity means your own answers steer which trade-offs you face next. A named social-desirability index: `[UNKNOWN]`.

**The fairness dispute — recorded both ways, because it matters to this report's audience.** In 2024 the **ACLU filed an FTC complaint** alleging that ADEPT-15's constructs are "closely tied to characteristics commonly associated with autism and mental-health diagnoses," that Aon's "bias-free" marketing overstates the evidence, and that the technical documentation shows no autism- or mental-health-specific mitigation. Aon's marketing asserts the opposite — designed from the ground up for minimal demographic differences, including disability. The technical documentation itself was withdrawn from public availability. Neither position has been adjudicated; both are recorded, and the practical takeaway for a neurodivergent candidate is the companion guide's standing advice — **request adjustments in writing, in advance, through the employer**, where the Equality Act 2010 duty attaches. `[INDEPENDENT (ACLU) vs VENDOR — unresolved]`

**How to win.** As with every forced-choice instrument in this guide family: know the role's competency model, answer as your genuine relevant self, **decisively and consistently**, and don't attempt trait-inflation — the pairing format spends your every attempt to look perfect on a trade-off against something else desirable, and inconsistency is itself a signal. The adaptive delivery means there is no answer key even in principle: your test is assembled live from your own responses.
