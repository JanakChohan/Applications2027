## scales cls — the rule-discovery test

**What it is.** Aon's deepest inductive instrument, and the one with official documentation. You see **six tables of numbers and letters**, colour-marked into **two categories** (for example marked above versus below, or blue versus gold). Some shared rule separates the categories. Your task: **discover the rule, then assign four unassigned tables** to the correct category. **12 items in 12 minutes**, and Aon's own instructions say plainly that "most people are unable to complete all 12." You may skip and reorder via the control panel (Aon recommends sequential order), each item has exactly one solution, and the test cannot be paused. `[VENDOR-VIA-CLIENT — Aon-authored SMU candidate guide]` The vendor targets it at roles built on data analysis — controllers, processing staff, R&D, trainees, junior-to-middle managers. `[VENDOR catalogue snippet]`

**How it is marked.** Aon's instructions state: "**Negative marking is applicable in this test** for incorrect responses only. No points will be deducted for empty responses." The *magnitude* of the deduction is not stated for cls (unlike the −0.5 documented for numerical/verbal — Chapter 1), and Aon's in-test advice is explicit: "**Avoid guessing.**" `[VENDOR-VIA-CLIENT]` Raw score converts to a percentile against a norm group as with the rest of the family.

**How to win.** A minute per item is generous by cut-e standards — this test rewards **methodical rule-hunting, not speed**. The recurring rules are countable properties: how many times a letter or number appears, repeated characters, positions ("contains the same letter three times"). The discipline that separates scorers: **test your candidate rule against all six example tables before assigning anything** — a rule that fits five tables and fails the sixth is the designed trap. If no rule yields, skip via the panel and return; with negative marking confirmed and the magnitude unknown, an unverified classification is a bad bet, and a blank costs nothing. `[PREP-VENDOR consensus + INFERRED]`

## scales clx and scales ix — the fast inductive pair

**clx**: six minutes; each task shows an **example pair of grids** sharing a rule, and you must pick **which two of four candidate grids** follow the same rule. Unlike cls, there is **no back-navigation** — a skipped item is gone, so the skip decision is permanent. **ix**: five to six minutes, up to ~20 tasks; **nine figures share a rule and one breaks it** — find the odd one out. `[PREP-VENDOR — GraduatesFirst, Fintest; consistent with the companion guide]`

**Marking:** no per-test documentation; the family-wide negative-marking rule is prep-vendor-supported for the logic tests but magnitudes are `[UNKNOWN]`. Play the same policy: eliminate-then-commit, blanks over blind guesses.

**How to win.** Both tests are decomposition drills. Run the dimensions checklist — **shape, count, size, shading, position, orientation** — one at a time rather than staring for a gestalt; most items encode **two simultaneous rules** (a shape rotates *and* a count increments). On ix, remember the mental operation is different from a sequence: you're finding the rule the *other eight share*, then the breaker. On clx, note both grids of the example pair before testing candidates — the rule lives in what they have *in common*. The no-back-navigation rule on clx means your per-item budget must be a hard one: decide, answer or skip, move.

## scales lst — the adaptive deductive grid

**What it is.** A six-minute deductive test (with roughly eight minutes of instructions and practice beforehand): **sudoku-like grids** where every symbol appears exactly once per row and column, and you identify which symbol belongs in the **marked cell**. Grids start 4×4 and grow — 5×5 and, per some sources, 6×6 — as you succeed: this is one of the family's genuinely **adaptive** tests. You can select blank tiles to test hypotheses. `[PREP-VENDOR — Fintest, AssessmentDay, and the companion guide]`

**How it is marked.** Undocumented in detail — `[UNKNOWN]`. As an adaptive test its output is an ability estimate reported as a stable percentile (the companion guide's Chapter 6.3 explains why raw counts aren't comparable on adaptive tests); whether an explicit point deduction also applies is not documented. What is clear from the design: **wrong answers demote you to smaller grids**, which caps your ceiling — the adaptive ladder is itself the penalty.

**How to win.** The key insight comes straight from the format: **you do not need to complete the grid — only enough cells to pin the marked one.** Scan the marked cell's **row and column first**: the symbols already present exclude candidates immediately, and often two intersecting constraints solve it outright. Work outward only as needed. Because it's adaptive, early accuracy matters most (it sets your difficulty path), and growing grids mean you're winning — climb calmly. `[PREP-VENDOR technique + INFERRED]`

## Anti-cheat notes (family)

All four draw from generated item banks — no two candidates sit the same items, so shared answers are worthless, and lst's adaptivity adds a second layer (your path depends on your answers). Session logging and the optional proctoring layer apply as everywhere (Chapter 9). Honest-candidate risks: the **no-pause rule** (a disconnection mid-test cannot be resumed — stable connection, protected window), and on clx the **no-return rule** — neither is an integrity flag, but both can wreck a sitting.

## Quirks and adjustments

The instructions-plus-practice phases before each test are untimed and repeatable **before** you start the real clock — use them fully, every time; they are the only official rehearsal of the exact mechanic you'll face.
