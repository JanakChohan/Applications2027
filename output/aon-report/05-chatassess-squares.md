# Chapter 5 — chatAssess (the messaging-simulation SJT) and squares (the integrity questionnaire)

These two instruments measure judgement and character rather than speed — and their scoring logic is entirely different from the ability tests: **there is no negative marking here, because there are no "wrong answers" in the arithmetic sense.** Instead, your responses are matched against a key or profile the employer helped build. That changes both how you prepare and what "cheating" even means.

---

## chatAssess — the situational judgement test in a messaging app

**What it is.** Aon's flagship simulation: "a **customised situational judgement questionnaire embedded into an instant-messaging simulation** for smart devices." `[VENDOR — verbatim from Aon's Rolls-Royce case study PDF]` The interface is a messenger app: named virtual colleagues ("Sarah Walford", "Daniel Popper" in the vendor's screenshots) send you messages — sometimes with images or video attached — and you reply by choosing from **multiple-choice message bubbles**. New messages keep arriving during the assessment, **including replies to what you sent**, so it plays as a flowing conversation; whether the storyline genuinely *branches* on your choices is `[UNKNOWN]` — the documented behaviour is responsive messaging, not confirmed adaptive branching. It runs about **20 minutes** under an overall time limit rather than per-message clocks — though note that **response latency may be recorded even where nothing is visibly timed**. `[VENDOR + PREP-VENDOR]` (JobTestPrep sells a "90-minute chatAssess simulation" — that is the *practice product's* length, not the real test's; a conflict worth resolving explicitly since it misleads candidates about the commitment. `[PREP-VENDOR]`)

**How it is marked.** Your chosen replies are compared against **employer-keyed "best-fit" responses** — the key is built with the hiring organisation against its values and competency framework — and benchmarked against a norm group, producing a **percentile plus per-competency sub-scores** (communication, drive, analysis, decision-making, adaptability, social interaction, workplace judgement — the exact set varies by build). There is **no universal pass mark**: the "right" answer at one firm can be mediocre at another, because the key encodes that firm's culture. `[PREP-VENDOR + VENDOR structure]`

**Bespoke per employer — confirmed at the source.** The Rolls-Royce case study describes a chatAssess **custom-built** for RR's Business and Engineering graduate streams. `[VENDOR]` In finance, JobTestPrep names **Morgan Stanley, Deloitte and Credit Suisse** among users `[PREP-VENDOR]`; the companion guide's Morgan Stanley chapter documents an email/inbox-style SJT keyed to the Business Unit applied to — the same product family. The practical meaning: **research the firm before the SJT, because the firm wrote the answer key.**

**The vendor's own performance claims** — worth knowing, tagged as the case-study marketing they are `[VENDOR — Rolls-Royce case study]`: high chatAssess scorers were "50% more likely to get a job" and "65% more likely to perform highly at the assessment centre"; completion rates rose sharply when it replaced conventional tests (Business stream 74%→96%); over 60% of graduates preferred it to previous online assessments. Whatever the spin, the underlying signal is real: employers weight this instrument, and candidates under-prepare for it relative to the numerical tests.

**How to win.**
1. **Learn the firm's published values and competency language first** — this is legitimate and decisive, because the key is literally derived from them.
2. Apply the standard SJT keying logic (companion guide, Chapter 6.4): responses showing **ownership, constructive direct communication, and proportionate escalation** key high; avoidance, blame, and reflexive escalation key low.
3. **Stay in character consistently.** The virtual colleagues' threads interleave; answering one storyline as a diplomat and another as a steamroller reads as incoherent judgement.
4. Don't rush — but don't stall either, since latency may be logged. Read each incoming message fully; some carry information that re-frames an earlier thread.

**Anti-cheat notes.** Unlike the ability tests, **chatAssess content is not randomised per candidate** — scenarios are fixed per employer build (the vendor's parallel-forms technology covers the *aptitude* instruments). Within one employer's cycle, answer-sharing is therefore theoretically possible, and the counter-measures are content refresh, the employer-specific key (a shared "best answer" from last year's build may simply be wrong this year), and latency telemetry. `[INFERRED + PREP-VENDOR; specifics UNKNOWN]` For you, the flip side matters more: **coaching folklore about "the right chatAssess answers" is unreliable by construction**, because keys differ by firm.

## squares — the integrity / counterproductive-work-behaviour questionnaire

**What it is.** cut-e's dedicated **integrity test** — designed to "predict unproductive behaviours at work": proneness to **counterproductive work behaviour (CWB)** — the vendor's list runs "distraction, boredom, superficiality, indifference, ambiguity, opportunism." It is built on a **situational model of CWB** which the vendor argues "does not stigmatise" and "offers the opportunity for change through training," improving candidate acceptance. Deployed for security- and risk-heavy roles especially, but positioned as valid for all jobs — and also used for tenure and safety prediction. `[VENDOR — catalogue snippet + the cut-e vendor deck]`

**Format.** Around **10 minutes** of self-descriptive statements answered on a **sliding scale with three anchors — "holds less true for me than for others" / "equally true" / "more true for me than for others"** — a *self-versus-others comparative* idiom shared with the shapes/views family (any position on the slider is selectable). `[PREP-VENDOR — psychometrictests.org]` One source family claims "~150 questions," which is implausible in 10 minutes and likely conflates squares with the full shapes battery — both figures are recorded, exact item count `[UNKNOWN]`. `[conflict noted]`

**How it is marked.** Six scales — how **empathetic, honest, reflective, disciplined, conscientious and cautious** you are, grouped into ethical awareness and impulse control — scored as a **profile matched against an "ideal candidate" profile** (often alongside shapes results), with a risk-flag output for CWB-proneness. Not a percentile ladder; a fit-and-risk read. `[VENDOR snippet + PREP-VENDOR]`

**Faking detection:** `[UNKNOWN]` — no source documents a lie/social-desirability scale for squares specifically. The comparative-slider format itself is argued (for the cut-e questionnaire family generally) to resist extreme-response faking, since "more true for me than for others" invites calibration rather than maximal self-praise. `[INFERRED from format; not vendor-confirmed]`

**How to win — and why "winning" is the wrong frame here.** An integrity test is the one instrument where strategic answering is both most tempting and most dangerous. The desirable direction of most items is transparent (be honest, be disciplined) — but the profile is matched, not maximised, and a saint-shaped profile on a *self-versus-others* scale ("I am more honest than everyone, more cautious than everyone, more disciplined than everyone…") is exactly the pattern a matched-profile system reads as non-credible. The defensible approach: answer **truthfully, calibrated against real other people**, lean genuinely toward the conscientious end where it is genuinely you, and keep your answers consistent with the shapes profile you gave (where both run, they are read together). And remember the sharpest practical point: anything you claim here can be probed at interview — prep sources note candidates who distort "might get further questions at a later stage for verification." `[PREP-VENDOR + INFERRED]`

**Anti-cheat notes.** As with chatAssess, content is fixed rather than candidate-randomised; the integrity protections are the format's own resistance, cross-instrument consistency reading, and downstream verification at interview. Session telemetry (Chapter 9) applies as everywhere on mapTQ.
