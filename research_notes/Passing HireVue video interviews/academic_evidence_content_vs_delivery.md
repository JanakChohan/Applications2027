# Academic evidence: content vs delivery in asynchronous video interviews (AVIs) and structured interviews

Reading notes on evidence quality used throughout:
- **[META]** = meta-analysis. **[FIELD]** = real applicants/incumbents/real interviewers. **[LAB]** = mock interview, students or Prolific/MTurk participants. **[ABSTRACT]** = I could only verify the abstract or a catalogue record, not the full text. **[SECONDARY]** = number taken from another paper's description of the study. **[THESIS]** = PhD thesis, not a journal article. **[VENDOR]** = authored by the AVI vendor's own staff.
- Most AVI-specific evidence is [LAB] with mock interviews. Field evidence with real applicants is rare (Liff et al. 2024; Dunlop et al. 2022; Maurer et al. 1998/2001/2008; Frieder et al. 2016; Bangerter et al. 2014).

## 1. Content vs delivery: how much of the rating is what you say versus how you say it?

### Takeaway
Both matter, but the weight of delivery shrinks sharply as the interview becomes more structured and anchored, and in the one published study of HireVue-built competency scoring the algorithm uses only the transcript (no voice, no face). In human-rated settings, delivery cues correlate roughly r = .2 with judgments as isolated cues; "what you say" (content, length, relevance) is the dominant predictor in every machine-learning decomposition of interviewer ratings except for ratings of extraversion.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Barrick, Shaffer & DeGrassi (2009, *J. Applied Psychology* 94, 1394-1411) meta-analysed three self-presentation tactics (appearance, impression management, verbal and nonverbal behaviour) against interviewer ratings and later job performance. Abstract: "what you see in the interview may not be what you get on the job and ... the unstructured interview is particularly impacted by these self-presentation tactics"; lab vs field design did not moderate. — [Barrick et al. 2009, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016532)
- [META][SECONDARY] Numbers from Barrick et al. (2009) as reported by Peck & Levashina (2017): impression management correlates r = .47 with interview ratings but only r = .15 with job performance ratings; verbal and nonverbal behaviour r = .41 in lab studies vs r = .32 in field studies; impression management r = .30 lab vs r = .36 field. — [Peck & Levashina 2017, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5309241)
- [META] Peck & Levashina (2017) own meta-analysis: impression management overall rc = .22 with interview ratings (k = 32, n = 3,792); self-focused tactics (self-promotion) rc = .24 (k = 20, n = 2,515); other-focused tactics (ingratiation) rc = .17 (k = 12, n = 1,277). Effect is much smaller when an observer rather than the interviewee codes the tactic (rc = .11 vs .27). — [Peck & Levashina 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5309241)
- [META][SECONDARY] In structured interviews specifically (Levashina, Hartwell, Morgeson & Campion 2014, *Personnel Psychology* 67, 241-293), self-promotion relates r = .26 and other-focused tactics r = .13 to ratings. — [reported in Peck & Levashina 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5309241); review itself: [Levashina et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12052)
- [FIELD] DeGroot & Motowidlo (1999, *J. Applied Psychology* 84, 986-993): in 110 news-publishing managers, a composite of vocal cues (pitch, pitch variability, speech rate, pauses, amplitude variability) correlated r = .20 with interviewers' judgments and r = .20 with supervisor performance ratings; a visual composite (physical attractiveness, smiling, gaze, hand movement, body orientation) correlated r = .21 with interviewers' judgments and r = .14 (p < .07) with performance. In a first sample of 60 utility managers the vocal composite correlated r = .18 with job performance. Effects were mediated by liking, trust and attributed credibility. — [DeGroot & Motowidlo 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.6.986)
- [LAB] Hickman, Bosch, Ng, Saef, Tay & Woo (2022, *J. Applied Psychology* 107, 1323-1351): machine-learning models trained to reproduce interviewer ratings of personality in mock video interviews (three samples, total N = 1,073; MTurk workers and undergraduates). Share of standardised regression weight by behaviour type: conscientiousness models 54.3% verbal, 36.4% paraverbal, 9.3% nonverbal; agreeableness, emotional stability and openness models 80-87% verbal; extraversion models 34% verbal, 38% paraverbal, 28% nonverbal. "Models tended to be dominated by verbal behavior, except for extraversion models." — [Hickman et al. 2022, accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10288660)
- [LAB] Same study: word count was a positive predictor in all three samples for both interviewer-rated extraversion and conscientiousness. For extraversion, the four cues that were positive predictors in every sample were word count, average loudness, loudness peaks per second (a speech-rate proxy), and variability in smile (AU12) intensity. — [Hickman et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10288660)
- [LAB] Hickman, Tay & Woo (2025, *J. Applied Psychology*): for machine-learning assessment of cognitive ability from mock video interviews, "verbal behaviors best predicted interviewee cognitive abilities, while neither paraverbal nor nonverbal behaviors provided incremental validity". — [Hickman, Tay & Woo 2025](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001236)
- [LAB] Koutsoumpis et al. (2024, *Computers in Human Behavior* 154): 710 participants, mock AVI; an algorithm using words, facial expressions and voice explained R² = .44 of human-rated interview performance; the paper's highlight states verbal, not facial/voice, features explain most variance. — [Koutsoumpis et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2023.108128); [Tilburg record with highlights](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/beyond-traditional-interviews-psychometric-analysis-of-asynchrono/)
- [LAB] Booth et al. (2021, ACM ICMI): 733 participants, hireability ratings from trained annotators; combining verbal, paraverbal and visual features "only marginally improves prediction accuracy" over the verbal modality alone, while increasing bias. — [Booth et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3462244.3479897)
- [LAB] Schroeder & Epley (2015, *Psychological Science*): evaluators (hypothetical employers in Experiments 1-3b; professional recruiters in Experiment 4) rated a candidate as more competent, thoughtful and intelligent, and were more interested in hiring, when they heard a pitch than when they read the same words; "adding visual cues to audio pitches did not alter evaluations". — [Schroeder & Epley 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615572906)
- [META] Huffcutt, Conway, Roth & Stone (2001, *J. Applied Psychology* 86, 897-913): across 338 ratings from 47 actual interview studies, high- and low-structure interviews focus on different constructs; structured interviews focus more on constructs that relate more strongly to job performance. — [Huffcutt et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.86.5.897)
- [LAB] Lübbe & Nitsche (2019, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment*): behaviourally anchored rating scales (BARS) reduced assimilation effects on ratings of standardised videotaped interviews by 40%; contrast effects were descriptively but not significantly reduced. — [Lübbe & Nitsche 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12230)

### Inferences
- The best-supported ordering for a structured, anchored, recorded interview is: content (relevance to the scored dimension, amount of codable material) first; vocal delivery second; visual behaviour a distant third. The evidence for "visual third" is convergent (Hickman 2022 nonverbal share 9% for conscientiousness; Booth 2021; Schroeder & Epley 2015; DeGroot & Motowidlo 1999 r ≈ .2).
- Delivery cannot be ignored when humans rate: voice adds to perceived intellect over identical words (Schroeder & Epley), and vocal cues carry a small but reliable signal (DeGroot & Motowidlo). But the size is modest next to content effects such as the ChatGPT-content manipulation in section 2 or the coaching and transparency effects in section 3.
- Delivery weight is construct-dependent: where the rated dimension is energy/sociability-like (extraversion, "communication", "presence"), paraverbal and smiling cues carry about two-thirds of the model weight (Hickman 2022). A candidate being scored on client-facing or communication competencies should expect delivery to matter more than on planning/analysis competencies.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Barrick et al. (2009) Tables 1-3, so I cannot give the corrected correlations for appearance, impression management and nonverbal behaviour separately by low/medium/high structure. Only the abstract's qualitative statement (unstructured interviews "particularly impacted") and the secondary figures above are verified.
- No study partitions variance in human AVI ratings into "content" and "delivery" with real applicants. All decompositions are machine-learning models of mock-interview ratings.
- I found no peer-reviewed evidence for an optimal speaking rate in words per minute for interviews, and no interview study that manipulates pitch variability alone. DeGroot & Motowidlo report only a composite.

## 2. AVI-specific findings: design features, preparation time, re-recording, response length, eye contact

### Takeaway
In AVIs, the behaviours that reliably raise ratings are using the preparation time actively (notes, structuring), using re-records when offered, and speaking long enough to deliver a complete answer (roughly two minutes when three are allowed) without padding after the answer is finished. Looking slightly above or below the camera does not lower ratings; looking sideways does. Merely being offered more time or retakes does nothing unless used.

### Cited Findings
- [FIELD] Dunlop, Holtrop & Wee (2022, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment* 30, 448-455): archival data from one Australian vendor, 2,550,105 responses from 627,999 candidates across 12,105 interview templates. Typical AVI: four to five questions, 30 seconds to prepare, 2 minutes to record; previewing questions or re-recording was "only rarely" permitted; median 10 candidates per AVI; settings mostly followed platform defaults. — [Dunlop et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12372)
- [FIELD][VENDOR] In the HireVue data used by Liff et al. (2024), interviews typically had five or six questions, three minutes were allotted per question and "respondents averaged around one minute and forty-five seconds per response". — [Liff et al. 2024, author manuscript](https://www.fisherphillips.com/a/web/5pa7UFPYmcygooLJEwwDgW/88Pn9s/psychometric-properties-of-automated-video-interview-competency-assessments.pdf)
- [LAB] Basch, Brenner, Melchers et al. (2021, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment* 29, 378-392): pilot plus main study; preparation time improved interview performance ratings, and the effect "was mediated by active response preparation" (making notes, structuring answers), not by reduced strain or by impression management. — [Basch et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12341)
- [LAB] Roulin, Wong, Langer & Bourdage (2023, *European J. Work and Organizational Psychology* 32, 333-345; online 2022): 2 x 2 experiment, N = 175, mock AVI. Offering unlimited preparation time and/or re-recording had no direct effect on outcomes, but usage did: "those who used more re-recording attempts performed better in the interview and engaged in less deceptive impression management", and "those who used more preparation time performed better". — [Roulin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/1359432X.2022.2156862)
- [LAB] Lukacik & Bourdage (2025, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment* 33, e12511): three mock-AVI experiments (N = 206 and N = 230 Prolific; N = 297 students). "Opportunities for longer preparation time and re-recording increased interview performance ratings." Faking warnings and being told the evaluator was human vs automated "did not affect behavior, reactions, or performance". — [Lukacik & Bourdage 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12511)
- [THESIS] Conflicting: Orji (2025, University of Neuchâtel) found preparation time did not affect performance in one experiment, whereas question wording that primed recall of a peak-performance example did raise performance and the likelihood of telling a story. — [Orji 2025](https://doi.org/10.35662/unine-thesis-3214)
- [THESIS][LAB] Response time limits (Orji 2025, Study 3): 185 participants randomly assigned to 1-, 3- or 5-minute limits. Mean speaking time 55 s, 127 s and 136 s respectively (speaking time plateaued after three minutes). Mean rated performance 3.00 (SD .39), 3.40 (SD .67), 3.15 (SD .60), a significant quadratic effect (β = -.33, p < .01). Speaking time predicted higher performance (β = .51, p < .01; model R² = .21). Holding speaking time constant, longer limits were associated with lower performance. Clarity and relevance ratings were affected; competence ratings were not. — [Orji 2025](https://doi.org/10.35662/unine-thesis-3214)
- [THESIS][LAB] Padding hurts (Orji 2025, Study 2): about 80% of interviewees kept talking at least once after signalling they had finished; these extensions contained more pauses and more opinion/result statements; "performance and persuasiveness were higher for initial than full responses". — [Orji 2025](https://doi.org/10.35662/unine-thesis-3214)
- [LAB] Patel, Powell, Roulin & Spence (2024, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment* 33, e12514): Study 1 (N = 404, Prolific) adding follow-up probes raised performance ratings, but "the effect of probing on interview performance ratings was driven by response length". Study 2 (n = 271) with a time-matched control: probing still raised ratings "above and beyond their increased response time". — [Patel et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12514)
- [LAB] Langer, König & Hemsing (2020, *J. Managerial Psychology* 35, 271-284): N = 124 (mainly German students). Participants told their answers would be scored automatically "engaged in less deceptive IM, felt they had fewer opportunities to perform ... and provided shorter interview answers". — [Langer et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1108/JMP-03-2019-0156)
- [LAB] Eye contact: Basch & Melchers (2024, *J. Business and Psychology*), two experiments with manipulated AVI recordings: a vertical gaze deviation "led neither to lower interview performance ratings nor to more negative perceptions of applicants' warmth and competence whereas a horizontal deviation of eye contact negatively affected perceptions of the hirability of applicants". Deviations lowered perceived social presence, which mediated lower ratings. Authors' conclusion: restrictions in eye contact "are no immediate driving factors for lower interview performance ratings". — [Basch & Melchers 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-024-09981-4)
- [LAB] Video vs face-to-face: Basch, Melchers, Kurz, Krieger & Miller (2021, *J. Business and Psychology* 36, 921-940): N = 114; ratings were lower in videoconference than face-to-face interviews; social presence, perceived eye contact and impression management contributed; "live ratings of interviewees' performance were higher than ratings based on recordings". — [Basch et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-020-09714-3)
- [LAB] Melchers, Petrig, Basch & Sauer (2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*): N = 88 final-year students and recent graduates; ratings on standardised questions were lower in videoconference (d = 0.49) and telephone (d = 0.89) than face-to-face; no differences in self-reported strain, heart-rate variability or state interview anxiety across media. — [Melchers et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.603632)
- [META] Blacksmith, Willford & Behrend (2016, *Personnel Assessment and Decisions*): 12 studies, k = 13, N = 1,557; technology-mediated interviews gave lower interviewer ratings (d = -.41) and worse applicant reactions (d = -.36) than face-to-face. Small, dated evidence base. — [Blacksmith et al. 2016](https://scholarworks.bgsu.edu/pad/vol2/iss1/2)
- [LAB] Conflicting on AVI vs live video: Langer, König & Krause (2017, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment*), N = 113: participants in digital (asynchronous) interviews "received better interview ratings" than those in videoconference interviews, though they found AVIs creepier and less personal. Basch et al. (2021, IJSA) attribute higher AVI ratings to preparation time. — [Langer et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12191); [Basch et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12341)
- [LAB] Media richness: Rizi & Roulin (2024, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment* 32, 54-68), N = 151: video-based (rather than text) question delivery increased perceived social presence and improved interview performance; higher social presence was associated with lower anxiety. This is a platform design factor the candidate does not control. — [Rizi & Roulin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12448)
- [LAB] Applicant reactions: Brenner, Ortner & Fay (2016, *Frontiers in Psychology*), 106 volunteers: perceived usefulness and ease of use predicted attitudes toward AVIs; interview self-efficacy, extraversion, neuroticism and conscientiousness did not. No performance outcome. — [Brenner et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00863)
- [LAB] Validity: Gorman, Robinson & Gamble (2018, *Consulting Psychology Journal*), 75 employed professionals: AVI ratings of mental capability, knowledge and skills, applied social skills and conscientiousness related to self-rated job performance. — [Gorman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/cpb0000102)
- [LAB] Honest vs deceptive impression management: Bourdage, Roulin & Tarraf (2018, *Personnel Psychology*; five studies, total N = 1,470) show honest and deceptive IM are distinct and "differentially impact interview outcomes and ratings". — [Bourdage et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12285)
- [META][ABSTRACT] Ho, Powell & Stanley (2021, *Canadian J. Behavioural Science* 53, 164-174): deceptive impression management was on average effectively unrelated to interview ratings; not much moderated by tactic type, interview fidelity, student status or gender. — [catalogue record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2594716237); DOI [10.1037/cbs0000223](https://doi.org/10.1037/cbs0000223)
- [LAB] Canagasuriam & Roulin (2024, *J. Personnel Psychology*), N = 150, AVI: situational and job-knowledge questions were associated with less faking than behavioural and background questions (small effect). — [Canagasuriam & Roulin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1027/1866-5888/a000361)
- [LAB] Roulin, Bangerter & Levashina (2014, *Personnel Psychology*), 246 professional and 270 novice interviewers: interviewers' attempts to detect impression management "were often unsuccessful"; they were better at detecting honest than deceptive IM; experience did not help. — [Roulin et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12079)

### Inferences
- The consistent mechanism across Basch 2021, Roulin 2023, Patel 2024 and Orji 2025 is that anything which leads the candidate to deliver more organised, relevant content raises ratings: active use of prep time, re-recording, and enough speaking time. Length helps until the answer is complete, after which continuing lowers ratings.
- The Orji data imply a practical target of roughly two minutes of speech for a three-minute limit; one-minute answers scored lowest. This matches the HireVue field average of about 1:45 reported by Liff et al. The claim rests on one thesis study, so treat as indicative.
- Camera gaze: the evidence does not support obsessing over staring into the lens. Vertical offset (looking at one's own image or at notes placed just below/above the camera) was not penalised; horizontal offset (notes or a second screen to the side) was.
- Deceptive embellishment does not buy higher ratings on average (Ho et al. 2021), while honest self-promotion does (section 1). The expected-value case is for honest, specific self-promotion.

### Gaps
- Roulin, Pham & Bourdage (2023) is covered in section 3; I could not retrieve its Study 2.
- No field study links re-record usage, prep-time usage or answer length to actual pass/fail decisions in real hiring.
- Response-length findings come from human-rated mock interviews; I found no peer-reviewed length/score curve for a commercial scoring algorithm.
- Lukacik, Bourdage & Roulin (2022) "Into the void" is a conceptual framework and research agenda; I did not retrieve its text and cite it only via the studies that test it.

## 3. Does interviewee coaching, training and practice work? Ability to identify criteria (ATIC)

### Takeaway
Yes, and the mechanism is knowing what is being scored and organising answers around it, not polish. Telling candidates the target dimensions raises structured-interview scores by roughly 7-12% of variance per dimension; coaching in real promotion interviews correlates about r = .09 to .25 with scores; candidates who correctly infer the dimension behind a question score higher (r about .2 to .5). In AVIs specifically, a single experiment found training helped performance while unguided practice alone did little.

### Cited Findings
- [LAB] Tross & Maurer (2008, *J. Occupational and Organizational Psychology*): post-test-only experiment, N = 144, structured experience-based (past-behaviour) interview, three coaching levels (introduction only; plus awareness training; plus skill training). "Individuals who participated in more comprehensive coaching ultimately received higher interview ratings"; interviewee knowledge partially mediated the effect. — [Tross & Maurer 2008](https://doi.org/10.1348/096317907X248653)
- [FIELD] Maurer, Solamon & Troxtel (1998, *J. Applied Psychology* 83, 128-136): police and fire promotion candidates in four jobs; attendance at a coaching session was significantly related to situational interview performance in 3 of 4 jobs, controlling for job knowledge and motivation. — [Maurer et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.83.1.128)
- [FIELD] Maurer, Solamon, Andrews & Troxtel (2001, *J. Applied Psychology* 86, 709-717): 213 promotion candidates; coaching attendance positively related to situational interview performance controlling for job knowledge, motivation, race and sex. "Coaching attendance and preparation by interviewees were positively associated with a tendency to use strategies in the interview that enhanced the organization of interviewees' answers, and this organization was positively associated with performance." Role-play and study-group preparation added beyond coaching. — [Maurer et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.86.4.709)
- [FIELD][SECONDARY] Effect sizes for the Maurer field studies as reported in a secondary review surfaced by search: coaching attendance correlated .25 with situational interview scores in Maurer et al. (2001), and Maurer, Solamon & Lippstreu (2008) found an effect size of .18 (equivalent to r = .09). I could not verify these against the original tables, and the search tool did not make clear exactly which review document carried the figures, so treat both numbers as indicative only. — [probable source: Levashina et al. 2014 review, copy](https://www.academia.edu/10691490/The_Structured_Employment_Interview_Narrative_and_Quantitative_Review_of_the_Research_Literature)
- [FIELD] Maurer, Solamon & Lippstreu (2008, *J. Organizational Behavior*): 146 public-safety incumbents; coaching focused on interview-relevant content (not peripheral manipulation); predictive validity and reliability of the situational panel interview were higher among coached than uncoached interviewees. — [Maurer et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.512)
- [LAB] Transparency: Klehe, König, Richter, Kleinmann & Melchers (2008, *Human Performance* 21, 107-137), application-training participants. Study 1 (N = 123): telling interviewees the dimensions improved performance, multivariate partial η² = .12; per dimension partial η² = .10 (planning), .09 (leadership), .08 (information management), .04 (cooperation). Study 2 (N = 269): multivariate partial η² = .11; per dimension .07, .07, .10. Criterion validity did not differ significantly between transparent and non-transparent conditions. — [Klehe et al. 2008, full text](https://jlupub.ub.uni-giessen.de/bitstream/jlupub/17122/1/KleheTransparency_2008.pdf)
- [LAB] ATIC: Ingold, Kleinmann, König, Melchers & Van Iddekinge (2015, *J. Business and Psychology*): 97 working adults in a mock situational interview with supervisor ratings collected. ATIC correlated r = .23 (p < .05) with situational interview performance and r = .29 (p < .01) with supervisor-rated job performance; the interview itself predicted job performance r = .24, dropping to r = .19 (ns) after partialling ATIC. — [Ingold et al. 2015, author manuscript](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/IngoldKleinmannKoenigMelchersvanIddekinge_inpress.pdf)
- [LAB] König, Melchers, Kleinmann, Richter & Klehe (2007, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment*), N = 95: ATIC measured in a structured interview and in an assessment centre correlated r = .40 with each other; ATIC from one procedure predicted performance in the other (r = .34 and r = .29). — [König et al. 2007, author manuscript](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KoenigEtAlIJSA07.pdf)
- [SECONDARY] Speer et al. (2014, *Human Performance*) summarise prior work as linking ATIC to structured interviews, assessment centres and personality inventories with correlations between .23 and .49. — [Speer et al. 2014](https://www.psychologie.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:ffffffff-f6f1-6828-0000-0000470c3e74/SpeerEtAl2014.pdf)
- [LAB] Oostrom, Melchers, Ingold & Kleinmann (2016, *J. Business and Psychology*), 101 students in a two-day selection simulation: ability to decipher situational demands related to performance in both the interview and work-related situations and partly explained the interview's validity. — [Oostrom et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-015-9410-0)
- [LAB] Jansen et al. (2013, *J. Applied Psychology*), N = 124: individual differences in assessing situational demands related to both selection performance and job performance. — [Jansen et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031257)
- [LAB] Conflicting on ATIC and score gains: Hickman, Liff, Willis & Kim (2025, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment*), N = 152 within-subject honest vs applicant-like AVIs: "Ability to identify criteria did not predict score improvement" on human or AI interview ratings. — [Hickman et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.70014)
- [LAB][ABSTRACT] AVI training vs practice: Roulin, Pham & Bourdage (2023, *J. Vocational Behavior* 145, 103912), Study 1: 202 Prolific participants randomly assigned in a 2 x 2 (training x practice) design, five-question mock AVI. Practice had little effect, whereas training was associated with better fairness perceptions and better interview performance. — [x-mol record of abstract](https://x-mol.com/paper/1682134717051944960); DOI [10.1016/j.jvb.2023.103912](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2023.103912)
- [LAB] Langer, König, Gebhard & André (2016, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment*): virtual interview training with automated real-time feedback on nonverbal behaviour produced better mock-interview performance than a parallel conventional training, mediated by rated nonverbal behaviour, and had "a positive influence on interview anxiety". — [Langer et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12150)
- [LAB] Tescari, Bangerter et al. (2025, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment*): two experiments (n = 91, n = 102). Giving explicit guidance about what past-behaviour questions expect did not increase the rate of story production, but in Experiment 2 it did raise performance ratings and narrative quality. — [Tescari et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.70004)

### Inferences
- The most trainable, best-evidenced lever is criterion knowledge: work out the competency behind each question (from the job description, the employer's published competency framework, and the question wording) and address it explicitly. Transparency experiments show the size of the prize when candidates know the dimension; ATIC studies show candidates differ naturally in guessing it.
- Coaching that works is content-focused and organisation-focused (Maurer 2001: answer organisation was the pathway). Maurer et al. (2008) deliberately distinguish this from coaching in "peripheral" score manipulation and find validity is preserved or improved, so this kind of preparation is not gaming.
- Repetition without guidance appears weak (Roulin et al. 2023 Study 1). Practice paired with feedback or with a framework for what a good answer contains is what the positive studies used (Tross & Maurer's skill-training tier; Langer 2016's real-time feedback).

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis of interviewee coaching effects on interview scores. The "Tews et al." meta-analysis named in the brief could not be located and should be treated as unverified.
- I could not retrieve results (r values) from Melchers et al. (2009, *Human Performance*) "I know what you want to know", the primary ATIC-interview study.
- Tross & Maurer's effect sizes by coaching tier were not retrievable (abstract only).
- No study tests STAR-format training specifically against a control with rated performance as the outcome.
- Roulin et al. (2023) Study 2 design and results not retrieved.

## 4. Behavioural (past-behaviour) vs situational questions: what raters reward

### Takeaway
Raters respond positively to genuine narrative answers to past-behaviour questions, yet candidates frequently fail to tell a specific story and fall back on generalities ("pseudo-stories"). Direct experimental evidence on the specific micro-features often advised (first-person "I" vs "we", quantified outcomes, stated reflection) is essentially absent; the single dataset that examined pronouns found "we" positive and "I" negative.

### Cited Findings
- [FIELD][ABSTRACT] Bangerter, Corvalan & Cavin (2014, *J. Business and Psychology* 29, 593-604): analysis of 62 real job interviews, coding five response types to past-behaviour questions; the abstract reports that recruiters respond positively to narrative responses and that both recruiters and applicants may need training to elicit and tell accurate stories. — [Bangerter et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-014-9350-0)
- [THESIS][SECONDARY] Orji (2025) summarising Bangerter et al. (2014): some interviewees "revert to pseudo-stories, describing how they typically behave instead of recalling a specific past event"; stories are "more persuasive and easier to remember, both of which contribute to better evaluations". — [Orji 2025](https://doi.org/10.35662/unine-thesis-3214)
- [LAB] Bangerter et al. (2023, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment*): story responses can be coded into situation, task/action and result utterances; an algorithm identified storytelling with 78% best accuracy. The paper states applicants "often experience difficulties producing such stories". — [Bangerter et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12428)
- [THESIS][LAB] In AVIs, initial responses contained more situation and task/action utterances; material added after the candidate had signalled completion skewed to results, values and opinion and lowered performance and persuasiveness ratings. — [Orji 2025](https://doi.org/10.35662/unine-thesis-3214)
- [FIELD][VENDOR] How human raters are instructed in the HireVue competency study: 15 competencies each scored on five-point BARS from "Novice" to "Expert", built from competency definitions and job-performance domains; raters received frame-of-reference training and calibration. Example prompt: "Tell us about a time you coached someone". — [Liff et al. 2024](https://www.fisherphillips.com/a/web/5pa7UFPYmcygooLJEwwDgW/88Pn9s/psychometric-properties-of-automated-video-interview-competency-assessments.pdf)
- [LAB] Situational questions: performance is partly a function of correctly reading what the situation demands (ATIC r = .23 with situational interview performance; Oostrom et al. 2016). — [Ingold et al. 2015](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/IngoldKleinmannKoenigMelchersvanIddekinge_inpress.pdf); [Oostrom et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-015-9410-0)
- [META][SECONDARY] Situational interview performance is only weakly related to cognitive ability (r = .09; Culbertson, Weyhrauch & Huffcutt 2017), as cited in a 2021 Frontiers paper. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.674815/pdf)
- [LAB] Pronouns and wording: Naim, Tanveer, Gildea & Hoque (2015 preprint; later *IEEE Trans. Affective Computing*): 138 mock interviews with 69 MIT undergraduates, rated by Mechanical Turk workers. Regression weights suggested higher ratings for speaking "as 'we' (vs. 'I')", more unique words, more total words, higher words per second, fewer fillers and more smiling; "I" carried a negative weight. — [Naim et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.03425)
- [LAB] Question type and faking in AVIs: behavioural and background questions attract slightly more faking than situational and job-knowledge questions. — [Canagasuriam & Roulin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1027/1866-5888/a000361)

### Inferences
- For past-behaviour questions, the evidence supports choosing one specific event and telling it as a story with situation, own actions and result, because (a) raters reward narratives, (b) many candidates do not produce them, and (c) BARS anchors are behavioural, so codable behaviour is what moves an anchored score.
- The common advice to say "I" not "we" is not supported by direct evidence that I could find. The only pronoun data (Naim et al.) point the other way, but come from crowd raters judging students on global impressions rather than trained raters scoring a competency with BARS, so they should not be over-read in either direction.
- For situational questions, the lever is identifying the dilemma and the competency being probed before answering.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed experiment was found that manipulates quantified outcomes, explicit "I" attribution, or a closing reflection/lesson and measures rated performance.
- The full text of Bangerter et al. (2014) was not retrieved, so the proportion of responses that were true stories and the size of the effect on hiring recommendations are not reported here.
- Culbertson, Weyhrauch & Huffcutt (2017) direct comparison of matched situational and behaviour-description questions: abstract not retrievable.

## 5. First impressions and thin slices: is it "decided in the first 15-30 seconds"?

### Takeaway
The claim does not hold for structured interviews. Early impressions correlate about r = .4 with final ratings in a live interview with a rapport phase, but the largest field study found only about 5% of interviewers reported deciding in the first minute, structure slows decisions, and the influence of initial impressions fades across later questions. None of this evidence is from AVIs, where each answer is typically scored separately and there is no rapport phase.

### Cited Findings
- [FIELD] Frieder, Van Iddekinge & Raymark (2016, *J. Occupational and Organizational Psychology* 89, 223-248): 166 interviewers, 691 applicants at a university career centre. Authors' practitioner point: "The belief that most interviewers make very quick decisions about job applicants may be overstated." Question consistency (a structure element) increased decision time; rapport building and interviewer experience shortened it. — [Frieder et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/joop.12118)
- [FIELD][SECONDARY] Same study, figures from the British Psychological Society digest: 4.9% of decisions within the first minute; about 30% within five minutes; 69.9% after five minutes or longer; 22.5% only after the interview ended. Decision times were self-reported. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/do-interviewers-really-make-hiring-decision-first-four-minutes)
- [FIELD/LAB] Barrick, Swider & Stewart (2010, *J. Applied Psychology* 95, 1163-1172): candidates making better initial impressions during rapport building received higher end-of-interview ratings (r = .42) and more internship offers (r = .22). Early competence judgments added ΔR² = .05 to outcomes from the same interviewer, a different interviewer, and an interviewer who skipped rapport building; liking and similarity did not carry over to other interviewers. — [Barrick et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019918)
- [LAB] Swider, Barrick & Harris (2016, *J. Applied Psychology* 101, 625-638): 163 mock interviews. "A significant portion of initial impressions' influence overlaps with job-related interview content" and "initial impressions are found to more strongly relate to interviewer evaluations of applicant responses earlier rather than later in the structured interview". — [Swider et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000077)
- [LAB] Naim et al. (2015): students rated highly on the first interview question were also rated highly overall. — [Naim et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.03425)

### Inferences
- An early-impression effect exists but is (a) moderate, (b) partly valid signal about competence rather than pure bias, (c) weaker under structure, and (d) decaying across questions. "First 15-30 seconds decide it" overstates the peer-reviewed evidence; the defensible version is that the first answer/first minute carries somewhat more weight than later material.
- In an AVI with per-question BARS scoring, the closest analogue is the opening of each answer and the first question in the set. There is no direct evidence on how much the first seconds of a recorded answer anchor its score.

### Gaps
- No study of thin-slice or primacy effects within recorded AVI answers rated by trained human reviewers was found.
- Frieder et al. rely on interviewers' self-reported decision time; real anchoring could occur earlier than interviewers report.

## 6. Scripted, memorised or read answers; gaze, speech rate, fillers, pauses

### Takeaway
The only direct AVI experiment found that reading ChatGPT-written answers word-for-word produced much higher content and overall ratings, no drop in delivery ratings, but lower perceived honesty. Beyond that, evidence on scripted delivery is thin: long silent delays before answering and slow speech are penalised, fluency and faster speech are associated with better ratings in correlational data, and the evidence that filler words damage global competence judgments is mixed.

### Cited Findings
- [LAB] Canagasuriam & Lukacik (2025, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment* 33, e12491; online 2024): preregistered, N = 245 Prolific participants, three conditions: no ChatGPT; ChatGPT-Verbatim ("read AI-generated responses word-for-word"); ChatGPT-Personalized (gave résumé/context and edited the output). "The ChatGPT conditions received considerably higher scores on overall performance and content than the non-ChatGPT condition. However, response delivery ratings did not differ between conditions and the ChatGPT conditions received lower honesty ratings." — [Canagasuriam & Lukacik 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12491)
- [VENDOR, not peer-reviewed] Contrasting claim: a HireVue white paper states ChatGPT-generated answers "generally score in the poor to intermediate range" on its assessments and that human reviewers can often tell when a candidate is reading a scripted answer. No methods are peer-reviewed. — [HireVue white paper 2025](https://www.hirevue.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2025_9_Do-Cheaters-Prosper_Whitepaper.pdf)
- [LAB] Gaze: vertical gaze offset from the camera was not penalised; horizontal offset lowered hirability; both lowered perceived social presence. — [Basch & Melchers 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-024-09981-4)
- [FIELD] Response delays: Brosy, Bangerter & Mayor (2016, *Discourse Processes*): in a corpus of job interviews, "response delays predicted the probability of inappropriate initial responses and decreased hireability ratings". — [Brosy et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2016.1150769)
- [LAB][ABSTRACT/SECONDARY] Feiler & Powell (2016, *J. Business and Psychology* 31, 155-171): mock interviews (press coverage reports about 119 students). Few behavioural cues related to rated anxiety; slower speech rate was the cue read as nervousness by both interviewers and interviewees; lower perceived assertiveness and interpersonal warmth mediated the anxiety-performance link. — [Feiler & Powell 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-015-9403-z); [press summary](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/821293)
- [LAB] Naim et al. (2015): in MIT mock interviews, higher words per second, more total and unique words, fewer filler words per second, fewer non-fluencies and less unvoiced time carried positive weights for overall rating and hiring recommendation; smiling carried a positive weight, especially for "friendly". Correlational, 69 students, crowd raters. — [Naim et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.03425)
- [FIELD] Vocal composite including speech rate, pitch variability and fewer pauses correlated r = .20 with interviewer judgments. — [DeGroot & Motowidlo 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.6.986)
- [THESIS][LAB] In AVIs, material added after the answer was complete contained "more pauses but not more fillers" and lowered ratings. — [Orji 2025](https://doi.org/10.35662/unine-thesis-3214)
- [LAB, conference paper, search-summary level only; full text not read] Kirkland et al. (2023, Interspeech): disfluency in synthesised spontaneous speech lowered perceived competence and confidence. — [Kirkland et al. 2023](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/kirkland23_interspeech.pdf)
- [LAB] Conflicting: a 2026 *Cognition* paper using spontaneous speech reports that disfluency does not reduce person-level perceived competence or trustworthiness, though disfluent speakers are judged less ready and less certain in the moment. — [Cognition 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010027726002660)
- [LAB] Voice vs text: the same words are judged more competent and hireable when heard than when read; having trained actors or untrained adults read written pitches aloud produced the same advantage. — [Schroeder & Epley 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615572906)

### Inferences
- A well-prepared answer delivered from a script was not penalised on delivery by human raters in the one direct test, and its content advantage was large. The identified cost is perceived honesty/authenticity, which in that study did not cancel the performance gain. This supports preparing content thoroughly; it does not establish that reading is safe in a real process with integrity checks, and it concerns AI-written text, which is a different (and potentially disqualifying) matter from the candidate's own prepared material.
- If notes are used, the gaze evidence favours placing them directly below or above the lens rather than to the side.
- Start speaking promptly and keep a brisk, even pace: delays and slow speech are the two delivery cues with direct evidence of a penalty. Chasing zero fillers has weaker support than avoiding long pauses.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study comparing memorised, read (teleprompter) and extemporaneous delivery of the same candidate-authored content on authenticity, warmth or competence ratings. Communication-science teleprompter findings surfaced only in non-academic guides and are not cited here.
- No interview-specific evidence on an optimal words-per-minute figure or on pitch variation in isolation.
- Fauville and Bohannon work on video gaze named in the brief was not retrieved.
- Effect sizes (d) for Canagasuriam & Lukacik were not retrievable (abstract only: "considerably higher").

## 7. Interview anxiety and what helps, especially when talking to a camera

### Takeaway
Self-reported interview anxiety relates to lower rated performance at about r = -.19 meta-analytically, operating through looking less assertive and less warm and speaking more slowly. Interventions with experimental support are training with feedback and reappraising arousal as excitement; the latter has not been tested in interviews. Video/AVI formats do not appear to raise anxiety in themselves, but unused answer time and re-recording are each associated with somewhat more anxiety.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Powell, Stanley & Brown (2018, *Canadian J. Behavioural Science* 50, 195-207): overall meta-analytic correlation between self-reported interview anxiety and interviewer-rated performance = -.19, moderated by anxiety measure and by mock vs real interview. — [Powell et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/cbs0000108)
- [LAB][ABSTRACT/SECONDARY] Feiler & Powell (2016): anxious interviewees were rated lower because they were perceived as less assertive and less interpersonally warm; slow speech was the one cue consistently read as anxiety. — [Feiler & Powell 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-015-9403-z); [press summary](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/821293)
- [SECONDARY] McCarthy & Goffin (2004, *Personnel Psychology* 57, 607-637) developed the Measure of Anxiety in Selection Interviews with five facets (communication, appearance, social, performance, behavioural) and validated it with real applicants; later sources cite anxiety-performance correlations across studies in the range -.19 to -.32. — [Powell et al. 2018 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2133770130); facets as used in [Melchers et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.603632)
- [LAB] Brooks (2014, *J. Experimental Psychology: General* 143, 1144-1158): across karaoke, public speaking and maths tasks, people told to reappraise anxiety as excitement (e.g., saying "I am excited" aloud) "feel more excited and perform better" than those trying to calm down. — [Brooks 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035325)
- [LAB][SECONDARY] In Brooks's videotaped speech study, participants who said "I am excited" gave longer speeches and were rated by independent evaluators as more persuasive, competent and relaxed than those who said "I am calm"; self-reported anxiety did not fall. — [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2013/12/performance-anxiety.aspx)
- [LAB] Langer et al. (2016): virtual interview training with automated feedback improved rated performance and "has a positive influence on interview anxiety". — [Langer et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12150)
- [LAB] Medium: no differences between face-to-face, telephone and videoconference interviews in subjective strain, heart-rate variability or state interview anxiety (N = 88), even though ratings were lower in the mediated formats. — [Melchers et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.603632)
- [LAB] Preparation time helped performance through active preparation, with "no mediation effects ... for strain". — [Basch et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12341)
- [LAB][SECONDARY] Roulin et al. (2023): using more re-recordings was associated with slightly (not significantly) more self-reported anxiety, per the authors' summary. — [Roulin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/1359432X.2022.2156862)
- [THESIS][LAB] Orji (2025): response time limits did not directly change anxiety; speaking time was negatively related to anxiety; after accounting for speaking time, longer limits were associated with higher anxiety. Women reported higher anxiety across conditions. Around 80% of interviewees kept talking after finishing, and this was associated with trait emotionality, interpreted as coping with the absence of listener feedback. — [Orji 2025](https://doi.org/10.35662/unine-thesis-3214)
- [LAB] Higher perceived social presence in an AVI was associated with lower interview anxiety (N = 151). — [Rizi & Roulin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12448)
- Conceptual: Constantin, Powell & McCarthy (2021) propose a tripartite (cognitive, behavioural, physiological) interview-anxiety framework; no intervention data. — [Constantin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12326)

### Inferences
- Because the anxiety penalty runs through observable assertiveness, warmth and pace, managing those behaviours (prompt start, steady pace, energy) addresses the pathway even if felt anxiety does not fall. Brooks's result has the same shape: performance improved without lower self-reported anxiety.
- Excitement reappraisal is cheap and has experimental support in an analogous evaluated-speech task, but its transfer to interviews or AVIs is untested.
- The "talking to a void" problem is real in the data (extended responding, lower social presence), and it shows up as rambling after the answer is done, which lowers scores (section 2). A pre-planned closing sentence is a reasonable inference, not a tested intervention.

### Gaps
- No randomised trial of an anxiety intervention (reappraisal, exposure, breathing) with AVI performance as the outcome was found.
- McCarthy & Goffin (2004) original correlations were not retrieved; the -.19 to -.32 range is secondary.
- Powell et al. (2018) moderator values (mock vs real) not retrieved.

## 8. Automated scoring: what drives machine scores, and does gaming work?

### Takeaway
Published scoring models are trained to imitate trained human raters using behaviourally anchored scales, and the one peer-reviewed study of HireVue's competency algorithms uses transcript text only (bag-of-words plus RoBERTa language-model embeddings), explicitly excluding tone and facial features. In academic models, what the candidate says dominates and word count is a recurring positive predictor. Faking moved AI scores least of any method tested (d = 0.14), but substantively better content (ChatGPT-authored) moved human-rated content scores a lot.

### Cited Findings
- [FIELD][VENDOR] Liff, Mondragon, Gardner, Hartwell & Bradshaw (2024, *J. Applied Psychology* 109, 921-948; four of five authors HireVue employees): AVI competency assessments built on real applicants' interviews. Convergent validity with human evaluator ratings averaged r = .66; discriminant correlations averaged r = .58; test-retest r = .72; subgroup differences Cohen's d ≥ -.14; criterion-related validity uncorrected sample-weighted r = .24 across five organisational samples (n = 1,124). — [Liff et al. 2024](https://www.fisherphillips.com/a/web/5pa7UFPYmcygooLJEwwDgW/88Pn9s/psychometric-properties-of-automated-video-interview-competency-assessments.pdf)
- [FIELD][VENDOR] Inputs: "The current AVI-CAs were built using word-based features only (i.e., words and NLP features) ... We explicitly excluded paraverbal (e.g., pitch, cadence, tone), and nonverbal (e.g., facial expressions) variables." Audio was transcribed by a third-party speech-to-text model into binary bag-of-words predictors plus 768 RoBERTa embedding predictors; the RoBERTa model was first fine-tuned on interview transcripts to predict human ratings of Communication. — [Liff et al. 2024](https://www.fisherphillips.com/a/web/5pa7UFPYmcygooLJEwwDgW/88Pn9s/psychometric-properties-of-automated-video-interview-competency-assessments.pdf)
- [FIELD][VENDOR] Training target: human evaluators' ratings on five-point BARS for 15 competencies, after frame-of-reference training; each human evaluator rated an average of 2,493 interview responses (Mdn = 2,470, SD = 967). — [Liff et al. 2024](https://www.fisherphillips.com/a/web/5pa7UFPYmcygooLJEwwDgW/88Pn9s/psychometric-properties-of-automated-video-interview-competency-assessments.pdf)
- [LAB] Hickman et al. (2022): models trained on interviewer ratings were valid; models trained on self-reports showed "little evidence of reliability or validity". Word count was a consistent positive predictor (extraversion and conscientiousness in all three samples); proportion of words longer than six letters was positive and LIWC "assent" words (agree, OK, yes) negative for conscientiousness. — [Hickman et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10288660)
- [LAB] Faking: Hickman, Liff, Willis & Kim (2025, *Int. J. Selection and Assessment*), N = 152, within-subject honest vs applicant-like simulated AVIs; AI trained to replicate question-level structured ratings. Score inflation: self-report scales d = 0.62; human interview ratings d = 0.22; AI interview ratings d = 0.14. AI ratings converged more with BARS-based structured human ratings than with less structured human ratings. More honest defensive impression management predicted improvement in AI and less-structured human scores; verbal ability and ATIC did not predict AI score gains. — [Hickman et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.70014)
- [LAB] Content upgrade: reading or adapting ChatGPT-written answers produced "considerably higher" human-rated overall and content scores with unchanged delivery ratings and lower honesty ratings (N = 245). — [Canagasuriam & Lukacik 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12491)
- [LAB] Large language models as raters: Zhang, Koutsoumpis, Oostrom et al. (2024, *IEEE Trans. Affective Computing*), 685 participants' simulated AVI responses: GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 achieved zero-shot validity similar to or better than a task-specific model for personality, but showed "uneven performance across different traits, insufficient test-retest reliability, and the emergence of certain biases". — [Zhang et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/taffc.2024.3374875)
- [LAB] Koutsoumpis et al. (2024): algorithm explained R² = .44 of human-rated interview performance and average R² = .32 of observer-rated traits vs .12 of self-reported traits; test-retest over seven months was "lower than desired reliability standards in personnel selection"; gender differences were sometimes amplified in favour of women. — [Koutsoumpis et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2023.108128)
- [LAB] Booth et al. (2021): the verbal modality alone performed almost as well as the multimodal model for predicting hireability and was the least biased. — [Booth et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3462244.3479897)
- [LAB] Hickman et al. (2022) mixed reliability: cross-sample generalisation of interviewer-trained models was mixed; a fourth sample (N = 99) tested test-retest reliability. — [Hickman et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10288660)
- [LAB] Being told an algorithm will score you led candidates to give shorter answers and use less deceptive impression management (Langer et al. 2020), whereas evaluator type had no effect on performance in Lukacik & Bourdage (2025). — [Langer et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1108/JMP-03-2019-0156); [Lukacik & Bourdage 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12511)

### Inferences
- Because these algorithms are trained to reproduce BARS-based human ratings from the transcript, the candidate's optimisation target is the same as for a trained human rater: say things that map onto the behavioural anchors of the competency being asked about. Delivery affects a text-only model only through transcription accuracy (clear audio, clear articulation).
- Shortening answers because "it's only a machine" is counterproductive: length is a positive predictor in academic models and candidates do shorten answers when told scoring is automated.
- Generic impression-management "faking" barely moves AI scores (d = 0.14), which argues against superficial gaming. The Canagasuriam & Lukacik result shows that scores are nonetheless highly responsive to genuinely better-structured, more relevant content.
- The convergent validity of r = .66 between algorithm and human raters means a given answer can receive a noticeably different score from the machine than from a person; and test-retest of .72 means the same candidate's score moves between attempts.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed test of keyword stuffing against a commercial AVI scoring model. Whether inserting competency keywords without supporting behavioural content raises scores is unknown; the bag-of-words component of the Liff et al. models makes some sensitivity to vocabulary plausible, but this is not tested in the paper.
- Liff et al. do not report the relationship between response length and score, nor any minimum-length rule.
- Liff et al. describe models built by HireVue researchers at one point in time; they do not establish what any particular employer's current configuration scores, or whether a human also reviews the video.
- No peer-reviewed evidence on how transformer-based scoring responds specifically to answer relevance or specificity as manipulated variables.

## 9. Rater fatigue, order and contrast effects when humans rate many recorded answers

### Takeaway
There is essentially no AVI-specific research on rater fatigue or sequence effects. Evidence from live interviews shows interviewers speed up their decisions after about four candidates, and experimental work with videotaped interviews shows assimilation and contrast effects that anchored rating scales reduce only partly.

### Cited Findings
- [FIELD] Frieder et al. (2016): decision time increased across the first few applicants, "but after about four applicants, decision time reached an asymptote and then began to decrease as interviewers evaluated additional applicants", consistent with more automatic processing; authors note this "may prevent applicants who appear later in the schedule from having a full opportunity to perform" and suggest limiting consecutive interviews to about four. — [Frieder et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/joop.12118)
- [LAB] Lübbe & Nitsche (2019): with systematically manipulated videotaped interviews, ratings were biased toward (assimilation) or away from (contrast) prior reference performances; BARS cut assimilation by 40% and reduced contrast only descriptively. — [Lübbe & Nitsche 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12230)
- [LAB] Ratings made from recordings were lower than live ratings of the same interviews. — [Basch et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-020-09714-3)
- [FIELD][VENDOR] Scale of rating workload in practice: human evaluators generating training labels for HireVue models each rated on average 2,493 responses. — [Liff et al. 2024](https://www.fisherphillips.com/a/web/5pa7UFPYmcygooLJEwwDgW/88Pn9s/psychometric-properties-of-automated-video-interview-competency-assessments.pdf)
- [FIELD] Typical AVI pools are small (median 10 candidates per AVI at one vendor), so many reviewers face short sequences rather than hundreds. — [Dunlop et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12372)

### Inferences
- Candidates cannot control their position in a reviewer's queue. The actionable implication of contrast/assimilation and of faster late-sequence decisions is that an answer whose relevance to the criterion is obvious early is more robust to a hurried or anchored rater. This is an inference from live-interview data, not a tested AVI finding.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on reviewer fatigue, playback speed, partial viewing, or serial-position effects among raters of asynchronous video responses.
- No evidence on whether real AVI reviewers watch answers to the end.

## Cross-cutting prioritisation for a candidate (inference from the cited evidence above)

### Takeaway
Ranked by strength of evidence and size of effect: (1) identify the competency each question targets and answer to it; (2) prepare organised, specific content and use prep time actively; (3) speak long enough to give a complete answer (about two of three minutes) and stop when done; (4) deliver promptly, at a brisk pace, audibly, with gaze not off to the side; (5) manage anxiety through its visible pathway. Visual polish beyond this has the weakest support.

### Cited Findings
- Transparency about dimensions: partial η² = .07 to .12 on structured interview scores. — [Klehe et al. 2008](https://jlupub.ub.uni-giessen.de/bitstream/jlupub/17122/1/KleheTransparency_2008.pdf)
- Better content with unchanged delivery gives "considerably higher" overall ratings. — [Canagasuriam & Lukacik 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12491)
- Speaking time predicts rated performance (β = .51); performance peaks at a three-minute limit with about 127 s of speech; padding lowers ratings. — [Orji 2025](https://doi.org/10.35662/unine-thesis-3214)
- Active preparation and re-record use raise ratings. — [Basch et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12341); [Roulin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/1359432X.2022.2156862); [Lukacik & Bourdage 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12511)
- Vocal and visual cue composites each relate about r = .2 to interviewer judgments. — [DeGroot & Motowidlo 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.6.986)
- Anxiety relates r = -.19 to rated performance. — [Powell et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/cbs0000108)
- HireVue-authored competency models use transcript features only. — [Liff et al. 2024](https://www.fisherphillips.com/a/web/5pa7UFPYmcygooLJEwwDgW/88Pn9s/psychometric-properties-of-automated-video-interview-competency-assessments.pdf)

### Inferences
- The effect sizes are not on a common metric and come from different designs, so the ranking is a judgment about relative magnitude and evidential strength rather than a computed comparison.
- The largest evidential weakness for the whole topic is external validity: nearly all AVI experiments use mock interviews with no job at stake.

### Gaps
- No study follows real applicants from a failed to a passed AVI and attributes the change to specific behaviours.
