# Question wording and decoding methods: what UK finance one-way video interviews actually ask, and how to read a question and answer every part of it in a short prep window

Research date: 26 November 2026. Adds to, and does not repeat, `Passing HireVue video interviews/answer_construction_and_rehearsal.md` (question lists in section 2, 30-second window in section 5) and `candidate_fail_to_pass_accounts.md`. Items already in those files (the eFinancialCareers November 2023 Barclays/Deutsche/UBS list, the Cambridge Careers bank, the Goldman 2018 "answer all parts" quote, the ATIC correlations, the Klehe transparency experiments) are referred to but not re-listed.

Labels used throughout:
- Wording status: **V-screen** = vendor states the wording was captured from the candidate's screen; **V-reported** = vendor states "wording is as reported" or "quoted as they were put" from a candidate's own sitting; **Recalled** = vendor states the wording is a recollection, not verbatim; **Agg** = wording as printed in an aggregated list (journalist or vendor pool compiled from Glassdoor, Wall Street Oasis, Reddit); **Snippet** = close paraphrase taken from a search-result summary of a Glassdoor review that I could not open.
- Type: MOT motivation; BEH past behaviour; SIT hypothetical/situational; STR habitual "how do you" (strengths style); COM commercial/markets; TEC technical; CASE exhibit or job-simulation task; HYB hybrid of two types; TWIST carries a constraint.
- Technique labels: **[EVIDENCE]** peer-reviewed or archival data; **[EXPERT]** practitioner, examiner, recruiter or careers-service guidance; **[ANALOGY]** borrowed from an adjacent field with no interview evidence; **[VENDOR]** commercial prep site.

## 1. Question bank: exact wording of reported questions, 2023 to 2026, with type, parts, length and source

### Takeaway
Wording captured from candidates' screens is much longer and more multi-part than the short forms that circulate on forums: across 136 vendor-verified 2027-cycle questions the median is 18 words, a quarter exceed 28 words, one in ten exceeds 51, and about 40% contain two or more explicit asks, while journalist-aggregated lists of the same banks have a median of 9 words and almost no sub-questions. The parts are usually signalled in plain sight ("In your response, please talk about:", "Describe the situation, your actions, and the outcome", "Please remember to answer both questions"), and coverage for asset managers, wealth managers and the Big Four is thinner and less verbatim than for banks.

### Inferences
- Forum-style lists compress questions to their headline. The same Goldman decision question that eFC-style lists would carry as "a time you made a decision without all the information" is, in the reported full wording, 58 words with six asks. A candidate who prepares from short lists is rehearsing for a shorter, simpler question than the one that appears, which is a direct route to answering only the headline.
- Six recurring structures cover almost every multi-part item in the bank: (1) story plus named sub-questions after "In your response, please..." (NatWest, Bank of America); (2) story plus the fixed formula "the situation, your actions, and the outcome" (JPMorgan, WTW); (3) story plus a reflective tail: "what did you learn", "what would you do differently now", "did you make the best decision, and how did you know", "why was this so important to you"; (4) double motivation: "why X and why Y", sometimes with a comparison ("among its competitors", "set us apart from other global investment banks"); (5) "what, and why" commercial items where the second half carries the marks; (6) past-plus-hypothetical hybrids, new in the 2027 cycle at Goldman ("...and how AI could have helped").
- Twist words in the bank that change which story qualifies: "outside of school or work", "not on your CV", "without being the leader", "without prior experience or guidance", "even when it was difficult", "at a significant personal or academic cost", "while others saw it as the way things are", "preferred less", "turned down", "that you struggled with", "while collaborating with others", "that affects the business division you are applying to", "apart from F1", "first respond", "one or two", "two trends", "three conviction trades".
- Hypothetical scenarios are concentrated at Goldman (confidentiality, policy breach, senior colleague says ignore it, disagreeing with teammates) and JPMorgan (calculations slightly off in a client meeting), and they often ask a second thing besides "what would you do": "who would you speak to", "with your team and/or the client".
- Asset-manager questions lean to habitual "how do you" wording (Schroders, Fidelity snippets, Deutsche TDI), often followed by "Give an example", so the correct response is a method first and a short instance second, the reverse of a STAR story.

## 2. Evidence that not answering the question asked is a real and common failure

### Takeaway
The best quantitative evidence is from real job interviews: when asked a "tell me about a time" question, applicants produced an actual story only 23% of the time, and the commonest response was a generalised "pseudo-story"; self-descriptions lowered hiring recommendations. Examiners, judges, recruiters and employers' own on-screen instructions all describe the same failure in their own domains, and experimental work shows that an answer to a similar-but-different question usually goes unnoticed by casual listeners but is caught about nine times in ten when the question text is in front of the evaluator, which is the position a per-question video reviewer is in.

### Cited Findings
- [EVIDENCE, field] Bangerter, Corvalan & Cavin (2014, *Journal of Business and Psychology* 29, 593-604), 62 real job interviews: "Stories were only produced 23 % of the time." Mean proportions of responses to past-behaviour questions containing each type (not mutually exclusive): story .23, pseudo-story .76, exemplification .15, values/opinions .29, self-descriptions .29. "Stories and pseudo-stories increased hiring recommendations, and self-descriptions decreased them." A pseudo-story is defined as "a description of a generic situation or recurrent set of similar situations, without unity of time or action". Discussion: "applicants rarely [respond by] producing a story, in fact less than one time in four." — [Bangerter et al. 2014, author copy](https://libra.unine.ch/bitstreams/c09ffd04-9bea-4a69-969b-d3c4ac890a0c/download)
- [EVIDENCE, field, snippet only] A follow-up real-interview sample (Brosy, Bangerter & Ribeiro) is reported as finding that only 50% of applicants' responses to past-behaviour questions were stories. I did not verify this figure in the full text. — [Brosy et al., PMC7379714](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7379714)
- [EVIDENCE, lab] Rogers & Norton (2011, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 17, 139-147), four experiments, 1,139 participants: "listeners can fail to detect dodges when speakers answer similar, but objectively incorrect, questions", and those speakers were rated as positively as speakers who answered the right question; listeners reacted most negatively when the answer was to a blatantly different question "or if they fumbled their words even while answering the correct question". When the question text stayed visible during the answer, "The ability to recognize a dodge more than doubled, from 39 percent without the text to 88 percent with the text." — [Rogers & Norton abstract](https://toddrogers.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/artful-dodger-answering-wrong-question-right-way); [APA release via ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/05/110505111945.htm); [Harvard Magazine](https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2012/02/the-art-of-the-dodge)
- [EVIDENCE, exam research] Sweiry, Crisp, Ahmed & Pollitt (2002, UCLES/Cambridge Assessment): "a student may end up answering the question that they expected to be asked rather than the question that the examiner was trying to ask." "Students will read what they expect to read. For example, they may overlook negative words (e.g. 'not', 'only', 'a few')." "even the first few words of a sentence or question can trigger further expectations of what will be read next... This is a serious factor as it can lead to students mis-reading what is written." "The more familiar the style or scenario of the question, the more powerful the schema that is evoked by it and the stronger the expectations of what is required." Citing Johnson-Laird: once readers have "constructed a meaning which we think is compatible with a given sentence, we are unlikely to continue searching for an alternative one. This effect is particularly pronounced in an exam, where students are often anxious to 'recognise' the tasks and get on with answering." — [Tales of the Expected](https://www.cambridgeassessment.org.uk/Images/109695-tales-of-the-expected-the-influence-of-students-expectations-on-exam-validity.pdf)
- [EXPERT, examiners] WJEC examiner reports summarised by TES: "Nearly every examiner emphasised the importance of candidates reading each question carefully"; sociology examiners "said teachers should emphasise the importance of answering the question set rather than the one they had hoped would come up." — [TES](https://www.tes.com/magazine/archive/carelessness-costs-exam-candidates-vital-marks)
- [EXPERT, examiners] ICAEW Level 7 Project Report examiners (August 2022), an exam the examiners themselves call "a written competency based interview": "A common reason for candidates scoring low marks for a question relates to them describing generic rather than specific situations." "Describing what 'the firm tends to do' or what 'their general approach is' will not satisfy the requirement to describe a situation." Checklist items: "Make sure they answer the right question"; "Check that they have answered all of the questions." Markers "assess each candidate's submission against each question's five sub-requirements". — [ICAEW examiners' report](https://icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/learning-and-development/apprenticeships/level-7-examiners-report-august-2022.ashx)
- [EXPERT, examiners, snippet] A Chartered Trading Standards Institute examiner's report: "the common mistake of not answering the questions asked, had occurred within this exam sitting." — [CTSI examiner report](https://www.tradingstandards.uk/media/documents/practitioners/cpcf-current-folder/stage-2/2020-examiners-reports/unit-4-food-examiners-report-written-exam.pdf)
- [EXPERT, recruiter] A recruiter writing on video interviews for graduates: when applicants read from scripts, "they will acknowledge the new question, but continue to ignore answering the question because they feel their pre-response will be better... but it's not the answer I was waiting for." — [Undercover Recruiter](https://theundercoverrecruiter.com/video-interview-tips-for-graduates/)
- [EXPERT, coaching article reposted by a university careers office] Morgan Stanley HireVue common mistakes include "Answering what you wish the question were instead of what was asked"; advice: "anchor your response to the specific question before you hit record." — [Leland via NAU](https://frankecareer.nau.edu/blog/2025/02/07/morgan-stanley-hirevue-questions-and-how-to-answer-them/)
- [EMPLOYER WORDING] M&G's 2027 on-screen prompt includes the sentence "Please remember to answer both questions in your response." The vendor notes the page underlines it. — [VENDOR: M&G page](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/m-and-g?p=summer)
- [VENDOR commentary on specific questions] NatWest: "the commonest way to lose them is to answer the headline well and ignore the list." BlackRock "What motivates you, and what drives you?": "an answer that gives one has answered half the question." Morgan Stanley "What strategies do you use...": "This is not asking for a story... a single STAR anecdote answers a different question." Goldman "without being the leader": "Answers that turn into a leadership story anyway miss the question entirely." M&G data question: the communication half "is the half people drop." — [NatWest](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/natwest?p=summer); [BlackRock](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/blackrock?p=spring); [Morgan Stanley](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/morgan-stanley?p=summer); [Goldman Sachs](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/goldman-sachs?p=summer); [M&G](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/m-and-g?p=summer)
- [EXPERT, advocacy] A Seventh Circuit intern who observed about seventy appellate arguments: "I cannot tell you how many times I heard the phrase, 'You're not answering my question'... When you dodge a judge's question, you lose credibility and frustrate the judge." Duke Law's collected practitioner tips: "The most serious error at oral argument is not listening carefully to the questions and thus failing to address the concerns raised by the judges." — [Marquette Law faculty blog, 2014](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/23563); [Duke Law moot court tips](https://law.duke.edu/life/mootcourt/tips)
- [EVIDENCE, analogy, snippet] Peter Bull's analyses of British political interviews: 45.6% of questions received an explicit reply in 33 interviews from 1987 to 1992; 38% across 26 interviews in the 2015 and 2017 elections. It shows how low reply rates fall when speakers arrive with prepared messages. — [LSE British Politics and Policy blog](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/politicsandpolicy/the-evasive-communicative-style-of-theresa-may/)
- [EXPERT, careers publisher] TargetJobs on pre-recorded interviews: "there won't be an interviewer to ask you follow-up questions. This can make it easy to blurt out an answer in the effort to cover multiple points in one go. By seeing your answers to practice questions in writing beforehand, you'll get a better sense of whether all of the points you want to make are relevant to what the questions is asking you." Its pre-recorded guide lists "Over-explaining: Stick to one clear point or story per question. Avoid long tangents and focus on what the employers is really asking." — [TargetJobs video interview tips](https://targetjobs.co.uk/careers-advice/interview-types/323741-how-graduate-recruiters-use-video-interviews); [TargetJobs pre-recorded interviews](https://targetjobs.co.uk/support/pre-recorded-video-interviews)
- [EXPERT, careers publisher] Prospects lists as common competency-interview mistakes "Giving abstract or hypothetical responses - Avoid saying 'I would usually…'" and "Using examples that don't relate to the role or required competencies". — [Prospects](https://www.prospects.ac.uk/careers-advice/interview-tips/competency-based-interviews)

### Inferences
- "Not answering the question" in a one-way interview takes four distinct forms, each visible in the sources: wrong form (habit or opinion given where a single event was asked, per Bangerter; or a story given where a method or hypothetical was asked); missing part (the second ask or a listed sub-question, per NatWest, M&G); ignored constraint (a leadership story for "without being the leader"); and substituted topic (the prepared story for a neighbouring competency, per Sweiry et al. and the recruiter anecdote).
- The Cambridge Assessment mechanism predicts that a well-rehearsed story bank raises the risk: the first few words ("Tell me about a time you worked in a team...") trigger the rehearsed schema, the reader stops parsing, and the tail ("...where one member was not contributing enough. How did you... keep the team on track, and make sure the work was shared fairly?") is not processed. Preparation and misreading are linked, which fits the candidate's self-diagnosis.
- Rogers & Norton cut both ways. A casual viewer would often not notice a near-miss answer, but a HireVue reviewer rates each response in a per-question field with the question as the heading (earlier project notes on the review page), and an AI model is trained per question against human ratings. Both are closer to the "question text visible" condition, where detection was 88%.
- The absence of follow-up probes is what makes the failure fatal in the recorded format: in a live interview a missed part is recovered by "and what did you learn?"; here nothing recovers it.

### Gaps
- I found no data from any employer, vendor or careers service on what proportion of graduate video-interview answers miss a part or answer a different question. The 23% storytelling rate is from live Swiss interviews, not UK one-way finance interviews.
- University careers-service pages specifically on this failure (Bath, Bristol, LSE) returned 403 or 404 and could not be quoted.
- The Rogers & Norton percentages come from the APA press release; the primary PDF returned 403.

## 3. Ability to identify criteria (ATIC): how candidates work out what a question targets, whether it can be trained, and how the measurement procedure converts into a drill

### Takeaway
ATIC research measures exactly the skill at issue: after each question the candidate writes what they think it was assessing plus behaviours that would show it, and raters score the fit with the intended dimension from 0 to 3. Candidates score higher on the specific questions whose dimension they identified, but the researchers themselves list trainability as an open question and I found no published study that trained ATIC, so the drill below is built from the measurement procedure and the authors' own training proposal, not from a tested intervention.

### Cited Findings
- [EVIDENCE] Definition and premise: "ATIC is defined as a person's ability to correctly perceive performance criteria when participating in an evaluative situation." Candidates in non-transparent procedures "face two tasks. First, they have to identify what is being measured. Second, they have to demonstrate behavior in line with their assumptions of what is being measured. If candidates misread the situational cues, their performance might suffer." — [Kleinmann, Ingold, Lievens, Jansen, Melchers & König 2011, preprint](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Measurement instruction (assessment-centre version, Jansen et al. 2010, quoted in the review): "In the previous exercise, you showed specific behaviors. Probably, you thought about what the assessors were assessing (i.e., on which dimensions they rated your behavior). Please write down dimensions that you think have been assessed during the exercise. Please also note down behaviors related to these dimensions…" Raters then rate each assumption's fit with the intended dimension "on a scale from 0 = no fit to 3 = fits completely"; the best-fitting assumption per dimension counts and scores are averaged. — [Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Interview version: "participants completed a questionnaire in which each interview question was listed and space was provided underneath each question. Participants were instructed to write the dimension that they believed was assessed with each interview question, and to provide behavioral examples for this dimension (e.g., they wrote down Assertiveness as the targeted dimension and provided an example that someone would speak up for their rights as an employee)." Two raters scored fit 0 to 3; inter-rater ICC .95. — [Ingold et al. 2015, author manuscript](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/IngoldKleinmannKoenigMelchersvanIddekinge_inpress.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Earlier interview procedure: participants "were asked to write down the hypotheses that they had entertained during the actual interview questions... regarding what a certain question or exercise was intended to assess. The questionnaires provided space for a maximum of two hypotheses for each interview question"; "All interview questions were repeated in the questionnaires to prevent memory problems." They were later given the true dimensions mixed with three distractors and behavioural examples, and matched their own hypotheses to them. — [König, Melchers, Kleinmann, Richter & Klehe 2007, author manuscript](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KoenigEtAlIJSA07.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Worked scoring example from the review's appendix: for an exercise targeting Organizing, Consideration of Others, Analytical skills and Persuasiveness, a candidate who wrote "Willingness to compromise", "Leadership: to take initiative, to structure the discussion...", "Assertiveness: to argue conclusively..." and "Appearance" scored 3, 2, 3 and 0 on the matching dimensions and 0 for Analytical skills, which they had not spotted. — [Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Within-person effect: "as shown by a reanalysis of data from König et al. (2007), candidates who identified the intended dimension in an interview question performed better on this question than on questions for which they did not successfully identify the dimension." Table 1 of the review: ATIC and structured-interview performance r = .27 (Melchers et al. 2004) and r = .35 (Melchers et al. 2009). — [Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Relation to cognitive ability: correlations "range between .20 and .30"; the authors link ATIC to the suggestion that able candidates "might be better at thinking through questions to give more appropriate answers", and found the ability-performance link was no longer significant once ATIC was included. — [Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Cue sources named by the researchers: "exercise instructions, other participants' behavior, reactions of raters, previous experiences in selection procedures, information gained through formal (books, test coaching programs, etc.) and informal (fellow candidates, discussion forums, etc.) coaching, inspection of the job advertisement, and information about the company." — [Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Why the dimension is not obvious: "when interviewees are asked about a hypothetical conflict situation... it is usually not obvious whether they should argue their point of view and negotiate fiercely or whether it would be more advantageous to indicate that they are endeavoring to understand the other party's point of view". — [König et al. 2007](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KoenigEtAlIJSA07.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE, authors' proposal, untested] Trainability: "A second practical and important issue for future research to consider is the trainability of ATIC. If we assume that ATIC is trainable, what would be the best approach? For instance, do people need feedback regarding their accuracy in reading situations or does experiencing selection procedures help to improve ATIC? ... Training could include such components as identifying cues in job advertisements, and studying instructions of AC exercises and interviews. Following this, trainees could be provided with feedback concerning the targeted dimensions and relevant cues." — [Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)
- [EVIDENCE] Telling candidates the dimension directly helps most when it is stated before each question: the transparency effect "was smaller in Klehe et al.'s (2008) Study 1 (which used a weaker transparency manipulation by providing relatively general information before the entire interview) than the effect in Study 2 (in which the targeted dimension was directly stated before each question)." — [Kleinmann et al. 2011](https://www.uni-saarland.de/fileadmin/upload/lehrstuhl/koenig/PDFs-Publikationen/KleinmannEtAl11.pdf)
- [EMPLOYER WORDING] Several 2027-cycle questions state the dimension before the question, which is the strong transparency condition: "At NatWest we make good decisions and solve problems... Please tell us about an important decision"; "'Do the right thing' is one of Morgan Stanley's core values. Describe a situation where..." — [VENDOR: NatWest](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/natwest?p=summer); [Morgan Stanley](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/morgan-stanley?p=summer)

### Inferences
- An ATIC drill that mirrors the measurement procedure, practicable tomorrow [built from EVIDENCE on measurement; effect on scores untested]: (1) take 20 questions from section 1 with the firm name hidden from none of them; (2) for each, start a 10-second timer and write one dimension word plus two behaviours that would show it (for example "integrity: self-report at once, escalate to compliance"); (3) check against a key; (4) score 0 to 3 for fit and log misses by cue type. The key comes from three places the bank shows are reliable: the value statement that prefaces the question, the listed sub-questions, and the firm's published values or competency list for the programme. The feedback step is the one the ATIC authors single out.
- Wording cues that signal the construct in this bank [my reading of the bank, not a research finding]: a prefacing value sentence names it outright; sub-questions name the scoring elements ("how you handled any objections" means influence is being scored, not the outcome); a reflective tail ("how did you know", "what would you do differently now", "what did you learn") signals self-awareness or learning as a scored element; "who would you speak to" signals escalation and compliance; superlatives ("best illustrates", "most proud") signal that the choice of example is itself assessed; a negated or restricting clause ("without being the leader", "not on your CV", "outside of school or work") signals that the default story is excluded; plural present-tense nouns ("strategies", "role do you typically take") signal method over anecdote; "among its competitors" signals comparison.
- Because performance is higher on questions whose dimension the candidate identified, the useful diagnostic for this candidate is per question, not per interview: after each practice answer, write what the question targeted, then compare with the key, and see whether the low-rated answers are the misidentified ones.
- ATIC and the 30-second window interact with cognitive load: the review links ATIC to "thinking through questions", which takes time. Moving the identification step out of the window (by pre-classifying every plausible question for a given firm against its values) is the only way to make it nearly free on the day.

### Gaps
- No training study of ATIC was found (search of the Kleinmann, König, Melchers, Ingold, Jansen and Klehe literature returned measurement and validity studies only). Whether the drill above raises interview scores is unknown. Hickman et al. (2025), already in the earlier notes, found ATIC did not predict score improvement between honest and applicant-like asynchronous interviews.
- The primary Melchers et al. (2009) "I know what you want to know" paper returned 403 again; its figures here come from the 2011 review's table.
- No study analyses which linguistic features of a question make its target easier or harder to identify.
- The typical proportion of interview questions whose dimension candidates identify correctly was not retrieved as a single figure.

## 4. Question-parsing methods from adjacent fields, and which survive a 30-second window

### Takeaway
Every adjacent field teaches the same three moves under different names: find the instruction word, mark the limits and parts of the task, and give the direct answer before anything else. None has been tested in interviews, and the classroom annotation routines have only teacher action-research behind them, but the examiners' device of answering under one mental sub-heading per sub-requirement and the advocate's "direct answer, then 'let me explain'" both compress into a few seconds.

### Cited Findings

**Exam command words and annotation routines**
- [EXPERT, exam board] "Command words are the words and phrases used in exams and other assessment tasks that tell students how they should answer a question." From Ofqual's official list as reproduced by AQA: Analyse "Look at the information provided and break it down to identify and interpret the main points being raised"; Assess "Make an informed judgment"; Define "Specify meaning"; Describe "Set out characteristics"; Discuss "Present key points about different ideas or strengths and weaknesses of an idea"; Evaluate "Judge from available evidence"; Explain "Set ou[t] purposes or reasons"; Identify "Name/state or characterise"; Justify "Support a case with evidence"; State "Express yourself clearly and briefly". — [AQA GCSE Economics command words](https://www.aqa.org.uk/resources/economics/gcse/economics/teach/command-words)
- [EXPERT, exam board] Subject-specific versions sharpen the distinction: Explain = "demonstrate knowledge and understanding... including the use of relevant examples"; Evaluate = "make a reasoned judgment about the strengths and weaknesses of an argument". — [AQA A-level Politics command words](https://www.aqa.org.uk/resources/a-level/politics/teach/command-words)
- [EXPERT, school resource] BUG as taught in a UK secondary school session: "Box the command words", "Underline the important parts – What do we know?", "Go back and check". The same handout lists the other clues in a question: "The number of marks – an indication of the amount of work you need to demonstrate and the time worth giving the question"; "Information before and within the question"; "Key subject terms". A variant, BUGS, reads "Box the command word(s) / Underline the key words / Glance back at the question as you go / Scribble and simplify" (snippet). — [Radcliffe School, Session 3: BUG the question](https://www.radcliffeschool.org.uk/s/Session-3-BUG-The-Question.pdf); [Westfield Academy, Approaching exam questions](https://www.westfieldacademy.co.uk/SiteAssets/Files/Revision/Approaching%20ExamQuestions.pdf)
- [EXPERT, weak evidence, snippet] The only outcome evidence found for BUG is one teacher's action research on a Year 11 cohort, reporting that "even those who did no or little annotation of the exam questions appeared to have benefitted with a better understanding of how to structure their answers". No controlled trial was found. — [WHP Trust action research](https://www.whptrust.org/component/k2/item/83-my-pupils-really-started-to-bug-me-action-research-on-a-year-11-exam-technique)
- [EXPERT, examiners] ICAEW's lead examiner "suggests that candidates structure their answers around the different parts (or sub-requirements) of each question, using sub-headings. This approach should make it easier for them to ensure each of the sub-requirements is covered. The use of sub-headings also aides the marking". Suggested headings for a behavioural question: "Description of the situation / How I demonstrated Continuous Improvement / Evaluation of my effectiveness / Description of the lessons that I learnt". — [ICAEW examiners' report, August 2022](https://icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/learning-and-development/apprenticeships/level-7-examiners-report-august-2022.ashx)

**Oral advocacy**
- [EXPERT] "If a judge asks a 'yes' or 'no' question, answer first with 'yes' or 'no' -- then elaborate. For example, reply with, 'Yes, Your Honor, in fact ...,' or 'No, Your Honor, rather ....'" "It is vital that you fully answer the question to the best of your ability when the judge asks it. Do not tell a judge that you will answer that particular question later". Practitioner quotes on the same page: "When asked a question, answer it directly. When possible start with a yes or no, but if the short answer requires a qualification... immediately follow the yes or no with that qualification." "Starting to give an answer before thinking about what to say. Advocates often rush into answering a question, fearing two seconds of silence, and then flounder or reverse course... It's always better to take a beat to be sure you know what you want to say before you start speaking." "Internalize the substance of those responses, rather than memorizing the words, so that you can shape your arguments to fit the actual questions the judges ask." — [Duke Law moot court tips](https://law.duke.edu/life/mootcourt/tips)
- [EXPERT] "The best way to handle questions is to answer directly—preferably with a yes or no when appropriate—and then say, 'Let me explain.'" — [Marquette Law faculty blog](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/23563)
- [EXPERT, snippet] An ABA appellate publication reports a judge's view that nothing frustrates a judge more than an attorney who fails to answer the question asked. — [ABA Appellate Issues, winter 2022](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/judicial/publications/appellate_issues/2022/winter/top-tips-for-top-notch-oral-argument-answers/)

**Competitive debating**
- [EXPERT] British Parliamentary rules: "The debate should commence 15 minutes after the motion is announced." A points-of-information rule of thumb: "Once you have accepted a point of information you can't just ignore it and carry on. You must deal with it or risk the adjudicator's wrath." Prep structure: "Think of the argument/Core-team-line first, then 3 main points to back it up and then the facts to back those up." — [British Parliamentary debating handbook hosted by the University of Vermont](https://www.uvm.edu/~debate/watch/wudcdvd/text/bphandbook.pdf)
- [EXPERT] Impromptu prep sequence from a debate federation: "Look for important words in the motion: a) Actors... b) Should or justified – need to prove why there is a moral or practical imperative c) Relevant themes"; "Figure out what kind of motion this is: a) Recognize the kind of motion and plan how you will meet the burdens for your side". Training advice: "Practice prepping motions on your own or with a partner with reduced time... The more you practice prepping for motions, the more patterns you'll notice for different debates, making it easier to prep." — [Saskatchewan Elocution and Debate Association](https://saskdebate.ca/resources/resources-for-students/prepping-for-impromptu-debates)

**Media training**
- [EXPERT] A media trainer answering the charge that bridging is evasion: "bridges should typically be preceded by a direct answer. When they're not, the response should still address the heart of the question in an authentic way." Quoting a critic: "Journalists (and the audience) aren't stupid. They know perfectly well when you're just rattling off your talking points." — [Throughline Group, 2015](https://www.throughlinegroup.com/2015/11/01/media-interview-bridging-is-bridging-an-outdated-practice/)

**Civil Service and consulting**
- [EXPERT, snippet] Government Communication Service candidate tips advise answering the question the panel actually asks rather than the one you would prefer, and say you can ask for it to be repeated; Home Office interview guidance suggests pausing or writing the question down. Both pages failed direct fetch, so no verbatim text. — [GCS recruitment tips for candidates](https://www.communications.gov.uk/career/recruitment-guidance/recruitment-top-tips-for-candidates); [Home Office careers, job interviews transcript](https://careers.homeoffice.gov.uk/?p=1456)
- [VENDOR] On KPMG's avatar-delivered recorded interview: "there is no nod to tell you to continue and no follow-up to rescue a vague answer. That makes structure more important, not less. Lead with your answer, give one example, land the result, and stop." Its mistakes table pairs "Long build-ups: The point never lands" with "Answer in the first sentence". (Answer-first and the pyramid principle are covered in the earlier notes.) — [Finbound KPMG guide](https://www.finbound.org/blog/kpmg-video-interview-guide)

### Inferences
- Transfer to a 30-second window, by method:

| Method | Field status | What transfers in 30 seconds | What does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Command-word recognition | [EXPERT] exam boards, no interview evidence | Classifying the prompt's verb in about 2 seconds, if a six-item interview "command list" has been memorised beforehand | Learning the list on the day |
| BUG / BUGS annotation | [EXPERT] with one uncontrolled classroom report | Done by eye or with one finger: locate the verb, count the question marks and "and"s, glance back before recording | Physically boxing and underlining a screen prompt; "scribble and simplify" |
| Sub-heading per sub-requirement (ICAEW) | [EXPERT] examiners | Speaking one signpost phrase per part in the order asked ("On what was missing...", "How I knew it was the right call...") | Nothing lost; costs no prep time because the order is given by the question |
| Direct answer, then "let me explain" (advocacy) | [EXPERT] judges and advocates | The opening clause of every answer | Asking the judge to clarify, which a recording cannot do |
| "Take a beat" before speaking (advocacy) | [EXPERT]; consistent with earlier notes on response delay | Using the last 3 seconds of the window, or the first second of recording, to settle the first sentence | Long silences once recording |
| Motion analysis: key words, actors, motion type, burden (debating) | [ANALOGY] built for 15 minutes | "Motion type" becomes question type; "burden" becomes "what must be true of my answer for this to count" | Full case construction |
| Reduced-time prep practice (debating) | [EXPERT] in debate; [ANALOGY] here | Practising with 20 seconds so that 30 feels long | None |
| Answer first, then bridge (media) | [EXPERT] | Only the "answer first" half | Bridging to a prepared message is the exact failure to avoid |
| Write the question down (Civil Service) | [EXPERT] for live panels | Not affordable at 30 seconds (see section 5); affordable at 60 seconds and above for the parts only | Copying the whole question |

- An interview "command word" list derived from the bank in section 1 [my synthesis by analogy with exam command words; untested]:
  - "Tell me about a time / Describe a situation / Give an example / Share an experience / When have you": one dated, specific event told as a story. A habit or philosophy is the wrong form.
  - "What would you do / How would you handle / Imagine / You are...": a decision and ordered steps, in the future or conditional tense, including who you would tell. A past story is optional support, not the answer.
  - "How do you / What strategies do you use / What role do you typically take / What is your approach": two or three methods in the present tense, each with one line of evidence. A single anecdote is the wrong form unless "Give an example" follows.
  - "Why X / What motivates you / What interests you": reasons specific to X. "Why X and why Y" needs both, and "among its competitors" or "set us apart" needs a comparison.
  - "What ... and why / what interested you about it / what do you think of it": the opinion is the answer; the description is the set-up.
  - "Walk me through / step by step / Outline / Explain / Define": ordered knowledge or process.
  - "Pitch": recommendation, thesis, evidence, risk.
- The sub-heading device is the strongest fit for the candidate's stated problem because it converts "answer every part" from something to remember into something audible: if a part has no spoken signpost, it was not answered.

### Gaps
- No controlled evidence exists for BUG or command-word teaching (an Education Endowment Foundation search was not run to exhaustion; nothing surfaced in general searches), and none of these methods has been tested in employment interviews.
- ESU's own guides (Mace Handbook, "Ten tips") returned 403; ESU-specific advice on answering points of information could not be quoted. WUDC training material beyond the hosted handbook was not located.
- UK Bar sources (Inns of Court College of Advocacy, pupillage advocacy guides) were not reached; advocacy guidance here is US moot-court and appellate practice.
- Civil Service guidance is from search summaries only.

## 5. The arithmetic of the window

### Takeaway
At the meta-analytic adult silent reading rate of 238 words per minute, a 20-word question takes about 5 seconds to read once, a 40-word question about 10 and a 60-word question about 15, so two full readings of the long Bank of America, Goldman or NatWest wordings consume most or all of a 30-second window. Handwriting runs at roughly 23 to 36 words per minute even when copying, so four keywords cost around 7 to 10 seconds: affordable with 60 seconds or more, not with 30.

### Cited Findings
- [EVIDENCE] Brysbaert (2019, *Journal of Memory and Language*): "Based on the analysis of 190 studies (18,573 participants), we estimate that the average silent reading rate for adults in English is 238 words per minute (wpm) for non-fiction and 260 wpm for fiction." "The average oral reading rate (based on 77 studies and 5965 participants) is 183 wpm." "Reading rates are lower for children, old adults, and readers with English as second language." "For silent reading of English non-fiction most adults fall in the range of 175–300 wpm". — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Reading time implied by those rates (my arithmetic):

| Question length | Example from section 1 | One read at 238 wpm | Range, 300 to 175 wpm | Two reads at 238 wpm | Read at oral rate, 183 wpm |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 words | "Why investment banking and why MS among its competitors?" | 2.5 s | 2.0 to 3.4 s | 5 s | 3.3 s |
| 20 words | Goldman hypothetical items | 5.0 s | 4.0 to 6.9 s | 10 s | 6.6 s |
| 40 words | Goldman "desire to excel", Bank of America research question | 10.1 s | 8.0 to 13.7 s | 20 s | 13.1 s |
| 60 words | Goldman decision question, NatWest items | 15.1 s | 12.0 to 20.6 s | 30 s | 19.7 s |
| 75 words | NatWest Q6, M&G data question | 18.9 s | 15.0 to 25.7 s | 38 s | 24.6 s |
| 100 words | M&G compliance question | 25.2 s | 20.0 to 34.3 s | 50 s | 32.8 s |

- [EVIDENCE] Handwriting speed: in a study of South African university students the mean copying speed was 22.85 words per minute (SD 4.15, range 13.35 to 34.10); the paper cites earlier work giving 26 wpm when students aged 17 to 25 write neatly and 36 wpm when writing as fast as possible. — [South African Journal of Occupational Therapy](https://journals.assaf.org.za/index.php/sajot/article/download/19695/22614/121466)
- [EXPERT, from earlier notes, for the speaking-rate constant only] Target delivery of 100 to 125 words per minute in a HireVue answer. — [Mergers & Inquisitions](https://mergersandinquisitions.com/hirevue-interview/)
- [VENDOR] Reported windows paired with long questions: Bank of America "30 seconds to read each question and up to three minutes to record" with 40-to-53-word prompts; Goldman Sachs 30 seconds with prompts up to 58 words; Schroders 2 minutes; WTW about 1 minute; BlackRock 180 seconds in the earlier notes. — [Bank of America](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/bank-of-america?p=summer); [Goldman Sachs](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/goldman-sachs?p=summer); [Schroders](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/schroders?p=summer); [WTW](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/wtw?p=summer)

### Inferences
- Four keywords at 23 to 36 wpm take about 7 to 10 seconds if written in full, less if abbreviated to initials; composing is slower than copying, so these are lower bounds. Saying a 14-word opening sentence silently at 100 to 125 wpm takes about 7 to 8 seconds.
- A realistic 30-second budget for a question of about 20 words: first read 5 s; second read for the verb, the limits and the count of parts 5 s; name the type and the parts 3 s; choose the story or method 5 s; compose and silently say the first sentence 8 s; breath and eyes to the lens 3 s. Total 29 s, with no writing. This revises the earlier notes' suggested routine, which put four written keywords inside the 30 seconds; the handwriting figure shows that step would take a third of the window.
- For a 40-to-60-word question in 30 seconds, a full second read is not affordable. The budget becomes: one deliberate read 10 to 15 s, during which the parts are counted on fingers as they pass; re-read only the sub-question clause (roughly the second half) 4 to 6 s; choose 4 s; first sentence 5 s. This is why the parts must be counted during the first read, not after it.
- With 60 seconds: both full reads fit for anything up to about 60 words (30 s), leaving time to write the parts as two-to-four initials (for example "M / E / T / K" for missing, evaluate, thought process, know) and to rehearse the first sentence. With 120 to 180 seconds (Schroders, BlackRock, M&G-style simulations): write the parts as a vertical list, put one keyword of content beside each, and draft the first and last sentence; the remaining time is better spent re-reading the question a third time than adding content.
- A prompt delivered by video or avatar (WTW, KPMG) is heard at speech rate and cannot be re-read at will, so the count of parts has to be taken on first hearing; jotting part-initials while listening is the one case where writing pays even in a short window.
- Readers with English as a second language should plan on the slower end of the range (Brysbaert reports lower rates without my having extracted a figure).

### Gaps
- No study measures how long people take to comprehend an interview question, as distinct from reading continuous prose at leisure; Brysbaert's inclusion criteria were reading for comprehension or pleasure, so times under exam-like pressure may differ in either direction.
- No source gives adult handwriting speed for jotting keywords while thinking; copying-speed data are a floor.
- I found no documentation of whether the question text stays on screen during recording on each bank's HireVue configuration, which decides whether "glance back" is available mid-answer.
- Bank prep-time figures for the 2027 cycle rest on the vendor and on the eFinancialCareers reports in the earlier notes.

## 6. First-sentence technique and closing by linking back

### Takeaway
Opening with a direct answer that reuses the question's own key words is unanimous practitioner advice across advocacy, examining and interview coaching, and it has an indirect experimental rationale (evaluators detect a mismatch far more often when the question is in front of them, so an opening that visibly matches the question works in the candidate's favour), but no study has tested echoing the question in interview answers. Advice to restate the whole question aloud is contested; the defensible version is to embed its key terms and limits in the first sentence.

### Cited Findings
- [EXPERT, advocacy] Open with the direct answer and then elaborate: "Yes, Your Honor, in fact ..."; "answer directly... and then say, 'Let me explain.'" — [Duke Law](https://law.duke.edu/life/mootcourt/tips); [Marquette Law faculty blog](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/23563)
- [VENDOR] "Lead with your answer, give one example, land the result, and stop"; "Answer in the first sentence." — [Finbound KPMG guide](https://www.finbound.org/blog/kpmg-video-interview-guide)
- [EXPERT, coaching article] "anchor your response to the specific question before you hit record." — [Leland via NAU](https://frankecareer.nau.edu/blog/2025/02/07/morgan-stanley-hirevue-questions-and-how-to-answer-them/)
- [EXPERT, snippet] Alison Green (Ask a Manager) suggests repeating a multi-part question so that you remember all its pieces, then signalling the order in which you will answer them. — [Ask a Manager](https://www.askamanager.org/?p=5421)
- [EXPERT, snippet] A media-interview writer argues that repeating the question at the start of the answer buys a few seconds to organise thoughts and makes a recorded answer stand alone. — [Christoph Trappe](https://christophtrappe.com/media-interviews-always-repeat-the-question-asked-for-better-content)
- [EXPERT, snippet, dissent] The Buckley School argues that "always repeat the question" should not be applied rigidly. — [Buckley School](https://www.buckleyschool.com/magazine/articles/questionable-presentation-advice-always-repeat-audience-questions/)
- [EXPERT, examiners] Sub-headings that mirror each sub-requirement help candidates cover every part and "also aides the marking"; "Subheadings that do not relate well to their content detract from the quality of submissions." — [ICAEW examiners' report](https://icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/learning-and-development/apprenticeships/level-7-examiners-report-august-2022.ashx)
- [EVIDENCE, indirect] Answers to a similar-but-wrong question passed unnoticed 61% of the time without the question text and only 12% of the time with it (detection 39% versus 88%); speakers who answered the right question but "fumbled their words" were rated negatively. — [APA release via ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/05/110505111945.htm)
- [EVIDENCE, indirect] In real interviews, story responses and pseudo-stories raised hiring recommendations and self-descriptions lowered them. — [Bangerter et al. 2014](https://libra.unine.ch/bitstreams/c09ffd04-9bea-4a69-969b-d3c4ac890a0c/download)

### Inferences
- A first-sentence template that follows from the sources [synthesis; untested]: direct answer + the question's key noun or verb + its limiting word + a count of parts when there are three or more. Examples against bank items:
  - Morgan Stanley Q77: "Investment banking because [reason]; and Morgan Stanley over its competitors for two things the others don't match: ..."
  - Goldman Q55: "The clearest time I moved a team's result without being the leader was ..."
  - Goldman Q36: "The decision was whether to [X] when I was missing [Y]. I'll cover what was missing, how I weighed what I had, and how I know it was the right call."
  - JPMorgan Q67: "I would raise it, first with my teammate and then with the client, and here is how."
  - Morgan Stanley Q80: "I use three strategies under pressure: ..."
- The echo serves three checks at once: it forces the candidate to have read the limiting word; it commits the answer to the right form (story, method, decision) before the rehearsed story can take over; and it gives a reviewer or a transcript-based model an immediate match to the question. It should be a clause, not a recitation, because the answer clock is running and the Buckley objection applies.
- Closing link-back: the evidence found supports a reflective close only where the question asks for one ("what did you learn", "how did you know", "what would you do differently now" appear as explicit asks in items 36, 68, 78, 106, 132). For those, the close is a scored part and must be spoken under its own signpost. For questions without a reflective ask, a one-line return to the question's key words ("so that is the time I ... without ...") is a self-check that every part was covered; I found no evidence that it raises ratings.
- Because disfluency while answering the right question was penalised in Rogers & Norton, the opening sentence is worth rehearsing silently in the prep window even at the cost of content planning.

### Gaps
- No experiment tests question-echoing, direct-answer openings or link-back closings in employment interviews or asynchronous video interviews.
- No bank or asset manager guidance on how to open an answer was found.
- UK school "restate the question" writing frames (for example RACE) were not researched for evidence.

## 7. Tailoring a prepared story to a question it was not written for, and what to do when no story fits

### Takeaway
UK careers guidance agrees that one example may serve several competencies provided the emphasis moves to a different aspect each time, and warns against leaning on one example throughout; the advocacy literature adds the principle that makes this workable under time pressure: internalise the substance, not the words, so the story can be reshaped to the actual question. Guidance on what to do when nothing fits is almost absent.

### Cited Findings
- [EXPERT] Prospects: "Yes, you can use the same example for different competency-based questions - but with some important caveats. It's okay to reuse an example if it clearly demonstrates multiple competencies... You also need to adapt the emphasis to the specific question. Even if the situation is the same, shift your focus. For a leadership question emphasise decision-making and motivating others. While for a problem-solving question highlight analysis and creative solutions. But avoid overusing one example across the whole interview as it can seem like you have limited experience." "It's a good idea to have five to eight STARR examples ready that you can adapt to different competencies." — [Prospects](https://www.prospects.ac.uk/careers-advice/interview-tips/competency-based-interviews)
- [EXPERT] University of Aberdeen Careers: "If you are using an example from one activity for more than one competency, try to focus on a different aspect of that activity to give a breadth of evidence." — [Aberdeen Careers resource](https://abdn.ac.uk/uploads/careers/resources/documents/4785.pdf)
- [EXPERT, snippet] Reed prefers different examples for different competencies and treats a reused situation as a fallback in which you focus on different aspects of it. — [Reed](https://www.reed.co.uk/career-advice/competency-based-interviews-what-you-need-to-know/)
- [EXPERT, advocacy] "Although you will not be able to actually memorize answers to these questions, you should know in advance how you plan to respond. Internalize the substance of those responses, rather than memorizing the words, so that you can shape your arguments to fit the actual questions the judges ask." — [Duke Law moot court tips](https://law.duke.edu/life/mootcourt/tips)
- [EXPERT, examiners] "Only use one situation to answer each question"; a small number of candidates "submitted identical answers to two questions"; candidates "will need to tailor the information to the specific requirements of each question." — [ICAEW examiners' report](https://icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/learning-and-development/apprenticeships/level-7-examiners-report-august-2022.ashx)
- [VENDOR] A Schroders candidate's advice relayed by the vendor: "five or six adaptable stories will cover it". WTW's seven-question set: "Prepare five genuinely different stories, because reusing one is obvious over that many answers." — [Schroders](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/schroders?p=summer); [WTW](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/wtw?p=summer)
- [VENDOR] On blanking: "If a question arrives and your mind goes blank, say what you are thinking in one calm sentence rather than filling the silence with filler. Reviewers would rather hear a short, complete answer than a long, uncertain one." — [Finbound KPMG guide](https://www.finbound.org/blog/kpmg-video-interview-guide)
- [EVIDENCE] In real interviews a generalised pseudo-story still raised hiring recommendations relative to other response types, while a bare self-description lowered them. — [Bangerter et al. 2014](https://libra.unine.ch/bitstreams/c09ffd04-9bea-4a69-969b-d3c4ac890a0c/download)

### Inferences
- A re-angling procedure consistent with the guidance [synthesis]: hold each story as components, not as a script: situation in one line; three or four separate actions, each tagged with the competency it shows (analysed, persuaded, escalated, organised, learned); the result; one lesson; one thing you would now do differently; one cost or risk you accepted. On the day the question selects which components are spoken and in what order: the question's sub-questions set the order, the action tagged to the targeted competency gets most of the time, and untagged actions are cut. The "lesson", "differently" and "cost" components exist because the bank shows they are asked for explicitly.
- A fit test before reusing a story for a twist question: say the limiting clause and the story's headline in one sentence ("...without being the leader, when I..."). If the sentence is false or strained, the story does not fit, whatever its quality.
- When no prepared story fits, an order of fallbacks that respects the evidence: (1) a smaller true event that meets the constraint beats a larger one that does not, since scale is not what the constraint tests; (2) for "how do you" and hypothetical prompts no story is required, so answer with method or decision and add one line of evidence; (3) if a past-behaviour prompt truly has no match, a specific partial instance told as a story, with the gap stated honestly and what you would do, is closer to what is asked than a general self-description, which is the response type that lowered recommendations in Bangerter's data. This ordering is my inference; no source prescribes it.
- A drill: one story against four different bank questions in a row (for example items 36, 53, 68 and 78), changing only the first sentence, the foregrounded action and the close, each within 30 seconds of prep. If two of the four first sentences are strained, the story bank has a hole in that area, which is more useful to learn in practice than in the recording.

### Gaps
- No careers service, recruiter or study was found that says what to do when no example fits, beyond general advice to prepare for common competencies.
- No evidence on whether reviewers penalise the same story appearing in two answers within one recorded interview.
- Guidance from Cambridge, LSE, Oxford, Warwick, Imperial, UCL, Bath and Durham careers services on re-angling stories was not retrieved (Warwick and UCL appeared in search results but were not opened).

## 8. How firms and platforms word their own guidance about answering the question

### Takeaway
The clearest employer guidance is inside the questions themselves: 2027-cycle prompts at M&G, NatWest, Bank of America, Deloitte, JPMorgan and WTW tell candidates what the answer must contain, sometimes in so many words ("Please remember to answer both questions in your response"). Published careers-page guidance is generic by comparison, and HireVue's own candidate tips say nothing about covering every part.

### Cited Findings
- [EMPLOYER WORDING, via vendor] M&G: "Please remember to answer both questions in your response." — [M&G page](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/m-and-g?p=summer)
- [EMPLOYER WORDING, via vendor] NatWest: "In your response, please talk about:" followed by two to five listed items, or "Please also tell us:". Vendor's description: "Each question quotes a NatWest value, asks a main question, then lists two to five sub-questions on screen". — [NatWest page](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/natwest?p=summer)
- [EMPLOYER WORDING, via vendor] Bank of America: "In your response, please share how you prepared for the meeting, what made it detailed or complicated, and how you presented the information..."; "In your response, please describe the situation, the actions you took to influence your stakeholders, how you handled any objections, and the final outcome." — [Bank of America page](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/bank-of-america?p=summer)
- [EMPLOYER WORDING, via vendor and eFC] JPMorgan and WTW: "Explain the situation, your actions, and the outcome." / "Describe the situation, your actions, and the outcome of the situation." / "Explain the circumstances, the steps you took to address it, and the lessons you gained from the experience." — [JPMorgan page](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/jpmorgan?p=summer); [eFinancialCareers, 9 Sep 2026](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/hirevue-questions-banking); [WTW page](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/wtw?p=summer)
- [EMPLOYER WORDING, via vendor] Deloitte: "Please include examples of when you have demonstrated these qualities in your response."; "If you are applying for a role with a Professional Qualification, please reference this in your answer." — [Deloitte page](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/deloitte?p=summer)
- [EMPLOYER WORDING, via vendor] Goldman Sachs writes the sub-questions as separate sentences: "Why were you driven to succeed? What actions did you take to ensure your success? What obstacles did you face and how did you overcome them?" The vendor's reading: "Goldman has handed you the marking scheme in the sub-questions, so answer all three in that order". — [Goldman Sachs page](https://financefluency.co.uk/interviews/firms/goldman-sachs?p=summer)
- [FIRM-OFFICIAL, from earlier notes] Goldman Sachs recruiters, 2018: "Be specific and be sure to answer all parts of the question". — [eFinancialCareers, 20 Jul 2018](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2018/07/video-interview-goldman-sachs)
- [FIRM-OFFICIAL] Evelyn Partners: "Prepare as you would for a face-to-face interview, in terms of research, practicing your responses using the STAR method, and getting familiar with how to convey your motivation and enthusiasm. Refer to your notes if needed, however, aim to come across naturally." Nothing on covering all parts. — [Evelyn Partners](https://www.evelyn.com/careers/early-careers/the-assessment-process/)
- [PLATFORM-OFFICIAL] HireVue candidate tips: "Most on-demand interviews ask you to give your answer in just a few minutes. It's helpful to think about how you will illustrate your skills clearly in that timeframe, and to practice them out loud." No instruction about answering the question asked or all parts. — [HireVue interview tips](https://www.hirevue.com/candidates/interview-tips)
- [VENDOR, attributing to KPMG] "KPMG UK says an AI avatar presents the questions but people assess the answers... Speak clearly, answer the question asked, and let your reasoning show." The phrase "answer the question asked" is the vendor's, not a verified KPMG quotation. — [Finbound KPMG guide](https://www.finbound.org/blog/kpmg-video-interview-guide)
- [EXAMINER-OFFICIAL, analogous] ICAEW tells candidates outright that markers score "each question's five sub-requirements" and to "Check that they have answered all of the questions." — [ICAEW examiners' report](https://icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/learning-and-development/apprenticeships/level-7-examiners-report-august-2022.ashx)

### Inferences
- Where a firm lists sub-questions, the list is the best available statement of the scoring elements, and it is free information that a candidate reading only the headline discards. Treating "In your response, please..." as the most important line on the screen is the single cheapest change available.
- Firms that word prompts with "Describe the situation, your actions, and the outcome" are asking for STAR in the question; firms that add other tails (lessons, how you knew, what you would do differently, why it mattered to you) are asking for something STAR does not automatically deliver, so a STAR-only habit will miss the last part.
- The fact that M&G felt the need to print "Please remember to answer both questions" suggests the employer has seen second questions skipped often enough to design against it.

### Gaps
- JPMorgan's interview-tips page returned no readable guidance text, and no current careers-page guidance on answering all parts was found for Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Citi, Barclays, UBS, Deutsche Bank, HSBC, BlackRock, Schroders, Fidelity International, PwC, Deloitte, EY or KPMG.
- The employer wording quoted here reaches us through one vendor's transcription of candidates' screens; no firm has published these prompts.
