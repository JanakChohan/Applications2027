## 1. Why a memorised answer gets delivered to the wrong question (mental set, shallow reading, goal neglect, interference, anxiety)

### Takeaway
Four independent, well-replicated mechanisms each predict exactly the reported failure: a strongly primed solution captures attention and blocks search for a better one (Einstellung); questions are normally read only to a "good-enough" partial match, so a question that resembles a prepared one is processed as if it were that one; a task requirement that the person can state is nevertheless dropped when the overall task model is complex (goal neglect); and anxiety specifically degrades inhibition and attention-shifting. Direct evidence exists for exams (students answer the question they expected) and, from the listener's side, for interviews and debates (fluent answers to a similar-but-wrong question often go unnoticed unless the question text stays visible). No study has tested these mechanisms on candidates in job interviews.

### Cited Findings

**Mental set / Einstellung**
- [LAB][ANALOGY: chess] Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet (2008, *Cognition* 108, 652-661): expert players who had found a familiar solution reported that they were looking for a better one, "but their eye movements showed that they continued to look at features of the problem related to the solution they had already thought of". The mechanism is attentional: the first schema activated directs where information is sought. (Abstract unavailable through the APIs; wording from the Semantic Scholar summary and from Sheridan & Reingold's description.) — [Bilalić et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2008.05.005); [described in Sheridan & Reingold 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/)
- [LAB][SECONDARY][ANALOGY: chess] Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet (2008, *Cognitive Psychology* 56, 73-102): the presence of a familiar solution reduced experts' problem-solving to the level of players "three standard deviations lower in skill level"; a control group given the same position without the familiar solution found the optimal one. More expert players were less prone to the effect. — [Bilalić et al. 2008b](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2007.02.001); effect size as reported in [Sheridan & Reingold 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/)
- [LAB][FULLTEXT via PMC][ANALOGY: chess] Sheridan & Reingold (2013, *PLoS ONE*): 17 experts and 17 novices, eye tracking. When the familiar move was good-but-suboptimal, 8 of 17 players in each group chose it, and those who chose it kept looking at it for the whole trial. **Boundary condition:** when the familiar move was a clear blunder, "the magnitude of the Einstellung effect was severely reduced"; all experts and most novices disengaged. The authors' reading is that obvious feedback that the familiar idea does not work triggers a new search. — [Sheridan & Reingold 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790829/)

**Shallow ("good-enough") reading of the question; the Moses illusion**
- [REVIEW] Ferreira, Bailey & Ferraro (2002, *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 11, 11-15): "the meaning people obtain for a sentence is often not a reflection of its true content"; language processing "is sometimes only partial and ... semantic representations are often incomplete". — [Ferreira et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8721.00158)
- [LAB] Moses illusion (Erickson & Mattson 1981): asked "How many animals of each kind did Moses take on the Ark?", "most people answer 'Two'", failing to notice it was Noah. — [Beucler, Voudouri & De Neys 2026, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0001530); original: [Erickson & Mattson 1981](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371%2881%2990165-1)
- [LAB][SECONDARY] Rates: a later paper reports that participants who demonstrably knew the correct fact still gave the illusory answer in about 52% of cases in the standard condition, and about 26.5% when attention was directed to the critical word. I could not open the full text to confirm which original experiment each figure belongs to. — [Memory & Cognition 2021, s13421-020-01128-z](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-020-01128-z)
- [LAB][SECONDARY] Mechanism: the illusion results from a partial-match strategy (the question is accepted when enough features match stored knowledge), and "with adequate focus on the relevant word, the Moses illusion is greatly diminished". The effect disappears when the substituted term is semantically unrelated (e.g. Nixon for Noah). — [Kamas, Reder & Ayers 1996](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03201094); [Reder & Kusbit 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596x%2891%2990013-a)
- [LAB] Beucler, Voudouri & De Neys (2026, *JEP: LMC*), three experiments with first answers given "under cognitive load and time pressure": people "do not necessarily need to deliberate to avoid the illusion", and those who fall for it show reduced confidence even at the intuitive stage; performance was driven by how semantically related the wrong term was to the right one. — [Beucler et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0001530)

**Direct exam evidence: students answer the question they expected**
- [FIELD-like experiment][ABSTRACT] Crisp, Sweiry, Ahmed & Pollitt (2008, *Educational Research* 50, 95-115): 339 students aged 15-16 in four English schools sat a science test built from adapted GCSE questions. "Through classroom preparation and exposure to past papers ... students develop expectations about examinations: what will be asked, how it will be asked"; "Where a question and a student's expectations do not match, the student may get the answer wrong for the wrong reasons." Rewording three of four questions to disrupt the expectation raised mean scores and made the questions easier on Rasch analysis; for the fourth "there was only a small effect suggesting that, in some cases, students' expectations are too strong to be suppressed successfully during an exam". The authors propose expectations act at three levels: subject, question and sentence. — [Crisp et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/00131880801920445)

**Goal neglect**
- [LAB][ABSTRACT] Duncan et al. (2008, *JEP: General* 137, 131-148): "In goal neglect, a person ignores some task requirement though being able to describe it." Neglect is not produced by real-time demands of another task component; "the key factor is complexity in the whole body of knowledge specified in task instructions". As the "task model" grows more complex, "different task components compete for representation, and vulnerable components may be lost". — [Duncan et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.137.1.131)

**Proactive interference and change detection**
- [LAB][ABSTRACT-level summary] Wahlheim & Jacoby (2013, *Memory & Cognition* 41, 1-15): when a cue is re-paired with a new response (A-B then A-D), performance reflects "a mixture of facilitation and interference effects", and what separates the two is "detection and recollection of change": noticing that the new item differs from the old one is what turns prior learning from a source of interference into a help. — [Wahlheim & Jacoby 2013](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0246-9)

**Anxiety and attentional control**
- [THEORY] Eysenck, Derakshan, Santos & Calvo (2007, *Emotion* 7, 336-353): "anxiety impairs efficient functioning of the goal-directed attentional system and increases the extent to which processing is influenced by the stimulus-driven attentional system"; the affected executive functions are "inhibition and shifting"; performance quality may be preserved "when it leads to the use of compensatory strategies (e.g., enhanced effort)". — [Eysenck et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/1528-3542.7.2.336)
- [META][SECONDARY from PubMed abstract] Shi, Sharpe & Abbott (2019, *Clinical Psychology Review* 72, 101754): 58 studies, N = 8,292; high- vs low-anxiety groups differ in attentional control at Hedges' g = -0.58; the deficit is in efficiency rather than effectiveness, and affects inhibition and switching but not updating. — [Shi et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2019.101754)
- [META][ABSTRACT] Moran (2016, *Psychological Bulletin* 142, 831-864): 177 samples, N = 22,061; self-reported anxiety relates to lower working-memory capacity, g = -.334 (complex span g = -.342; simple span g = -.318; N-back g = -.437). — [Moran 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000051)

**Evidence from the listener/rater side: does a fluent answer to the wrong question get caught?**
- [LAB][ABSTRACT] Rogers & Norton (2011, *JEP: Applied* 17, 139-147), four studies: "listeners can fail to detect dodges when speakers answer similar-but objectively incorrect-questions". Detection rose (a) when listeners were told to judge relevance, (b) "when speakers answered a question egregiously dissimilar to the one asked", and (c) "when listeners' attention was directed to the question asked by keeping it visible during speakers' answers" (Study 4). When listeners detected a dodge "they rated speakers more negatively" (Study 2). Listeners "rated speakers who answered a similar question in a fluent manner more positively than speakers who answered the actual question but disfluently" (Study 3). Stimuli were debate-style answers, not job interviews. — [Rogers & Norton 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023439)
- [FIELD] In a corpus of real job interviews, longer response delays "predicted the probability of inappropriate initial responses and decreased hireability ratings" (already cited in the earlier file; relevant here because it is the only interview study I found that codes an answer as inappropriate to the question). — [Brosy, Bangerter & Mayor 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2016.1150769)

**The same failure is documented in competitive speech**
- [PRACTITIONER, conference proceedings, not peer-reviewed research] Richardson (2010, Proceedings of the National Developmental Conference on Individual Events) describes US collegiate impromptu/extemporaneous competition: competitors bring "examples that were neatly packaged weeks earlier", and "the arguments and examples would be irrelevant to an understanding of the quotation"; "'limited preparation' really means advanced preparation ... 'impromptu' rewards the use of examples fully pre-prepared". This is a coach's critique, with no data. — [Richardson 2010](https://cornerstone.lib.mnsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1021&context=ndcieproceedings)

### Inferences
- The candidate's self-diagnosis is mechanistically coherent. A scripted "perfect" answer is the strongest candidate response in memory; under Einstellung it captures attention, and under partial matching a question that shares topic words with a prepared one is accepted as "that question". The Bilalić finding that people *believe* they are searching for alternatives while their attention stays on the first idea matches the report of only realising afterwards that the answer did not fit.
- The Moses-illusion and Sheridan & Reingold boundary conditions point the same way: the error is most likely when the asked question is *similar* to a prepared one, and least likely when it is obviously different. So the highest-risk questions are near-neighbours of rehearsed ones (e.g. a "tell me about a time you failed" script meeting "tell me about a time you changed your mind").
- Three manipulations reduced the error in the lab: directing attention to the critical word (Kamas et al.), making change detection explicit (Wahlheim & Jacoby), and keeping the question visible (Rogers & Norton, on the listener side). The common element is an explicit comparison between the question as asked and the answer about to be given. That is an inference about a routine; no study has tested it on interviewees.
- Goal neglect predicts that adding more self-instructions (smile, structure, firm research, keywords, timing) raises the chance that the one requirement that matters most (answer this question) is the one dropped. Duncan's result is about total instruction complexity, not momentary load.
- Rogers & Norton cuts both ways for a HireVue reviewer. A fluent near-miss can pass unnoticed when the rater is forming a general impression, but detection and penalties rise when the rater is asked to judge relevance against a criterion or has the question text in view. Structured rating forms that list the question and competency resemble the "detection" conditions more than the "social evaluation" conditions. Whether the question is on screen in the reviewer's HireVue view is not established in these notes.
- Attentional control theory predicts that anxiety hurts exactly the two operations needed in the 30 seconds: inhibiting the prepotent prepared answer and shifting to a different story.

### Gaps
- No study tests Einstellung, the Moses illusion, goal neglect or proactive interference on candidates answering interview questions. Everything in this section except Brosy et al. (2016) and Rogers & Norton (2011) is analogy.
- I could not retrieve Luchins (1942) or the functional-fixedness literature (Duncker; Adamson), so they are not cited. Functional fixedness in particular (seeing a prepared story as "the teamwork story" and failing to see it as a usable "conflict" or "failure" story) is a plausible framing with no evidence retrieved here.
- Kahneman & Frederick's (2002) "attribute substitution" account (answering an easier question in place of the one asked) is conceptually the closest judgment-and-decision-making idea, but I retrieved only the chapter record, not its text, so I report it as a pointer only: [Kahneman & Frederick 2002](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511808098.004).
- Exact Erickson & Mattson (1981) percentages by experiment were not verified against the original.
- No evidence on how often candidates in AVIs answer a different question from the one asked, or how human AVI raters score such answers.

## 2. Choking under pressure: accounts, which tasks each affects, and the tested remedies

### Takeaway
There are two routes to choking and they hit different skills: worry and outcome pressure consume working memory and hurt attention-demanding tasks (such as reading a novel question and selecting material), while being watched or recorded prompts self-monitoring and hurts fluent, proceduralised skills (such as speaking). Of the remedies, practising under mild pressure (meta-analytic g ≈ 0.67) and pre-performance routines (g ≈ 0.64 to 0.70) have the best support but come from sport and policing; arousal reappraisal has a small meta-analytic effect (d ≈ 0.23, larger for public-performance tasks); expressive writing has failed replication and a 2025 meta-analysis finds no reliable effect; pre-performance rituals rest on a retracted paper; power posing is not supported.

### Cited Findings

**The two accounts and what each damages**
- [LAB] Baumeister (1984, *JPSP* 46, 610-620), six experiments: pressure "increases the conscious attention to the performer's own process of performance and ... this increased conscious attention disrupts the automatic or overlearned nature of the execution". — [Baumeister 1984](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.3.610)
- [LAB][ANALOGY: golf putting, mental arithmetic] Beilock & Carr (2001, *JEP: General* 130, 701-725): choking occurred in a proceduralised skill (putting) but not in a non-proceduralised arithmetic task; it "was unchanged by dual-task training but eliminated by self-consciousness training" (practising while being made self-aware). — [Beilock & Carr 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.130.4.701)
- [LAB][ANALOGY: maths] Beilock & Carr (2005, *Psychological Science* 16, 101-105): "only individuals high in working memory capacity were harmed by performance pressure", and only on the problems with the highest working-memory demand; pressure "harms individuals most qualified to succeed by consuming the working memory capacity that they rely on". — [Beilock & Carr 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2005.00789.x)
- [LAB][ANALOGY: category learning] DeCaro, Thomas, Albert & Beilock (2011, *JEP: General* 140, 390-406), four experiments: "pressure that induces distraction (involving performance-contingent outcomes) hurts rule-based category learning heavily dependent on attentional control. In contrast, pressure that induces explicit monitoring of performance (monitoring by others) hurts information-integration category learning thought to run best without heavy demands on working memory." Experiment 3 used this to "design interventions to eliminate choking". — [DeCaro et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023466)

**Pre-performance routines**
- [META][ANALOGY: sport] Rupprecht, Tran & Gröpel (2021/2024, *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 17, 39-64): 112 effect sizes. Pre-post designs SMC = 0.31 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.44); experimental designs Hedges' g = 0.64 (0.45 to 0.83) under low pressure and g = 0.70 (0.24 to 1.16) under pressure. "These effects were not moderated by the type of PPR, age, gender, skill level, or intervention characteristics." A routine is defined as "a set of task-relevant thoughts and actions an athlete systematically engages in prior to performance execution". — [Rupprecht et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984x.2021.1944271)
- [RETRACTED] The best-known non-sport evidence that a pre-task ritual improves performance by lowering anxiety (Brooks, Schroeder, Risen, Gino, Galinsky, Norton & Schweitzer 2016, *OBHDP* 137, 71-85) has a formal retraction notice (2024). It should not be cited as support. — [Retraction notice](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2024.104377)

**Practising under mild pressure / while observed**
- [META][ANALOGY: sport and policing] Low et al. (2021, *Sport, Exercise, and Performance Psychology* 10, 149-163): 14 studies (10 sport, 4 law enforcement), n = 394, comparing pressure training with control on post-tests under pressure. After removing one outlier, g = 0.67 (95% CI 0.43 to 1.12), I² = 17%. Benefits held for novices and experienced performers; no clear moderators. "Coaches and instructors should create pressurized training environments rather than relying on greater amounts of training." The authors call for retention tests and real-competition outcomes. — [Low et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/spy0000202)
- [LAB/quasi-field][ANALOGY: basketball, darts] Oudejans & Pijpers (2009, *QJEP* 62, 1631-1647): 17 expert basketball players practised free throws for five weeks with or without induced anxiety; 17 expert dart players practised high or low on a climbing wall. "Only after training with anxiety did performance no longer deteriorate during the anxiety posttest", "despite higher levels of anxiety, heart rate, and perceived effort". Small samples. — [Oudejans & Pijpers 2009](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210802557702)
- [LAB][SECONDARY][ANALOGY: darts] Oudejans & Pijpers (2010, *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 11, 44-50): novices who trained with *mild* anxiety maintained performance when later tested at higher anxiety, while controls deteriorated. Details taken from a secondary reprint of the abstract. — [Oudejans & Pijpers 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2009.05.002)
- [LAB] Beilock & Carr (2001): "self-consciousness training" (practising under scrutiny) eliminated choking in putting, whereas dual-task training did not. — [Beilock & Carr 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.130.4.701)

**Expressive writing before a test [REPLICATION PROBLEM]**
- [LAB + FIELD] Original: Ramirez & Beilock (2011, *Science* 331, 211-213), two lab and two randomised classroom experiments: a brief writing exercise about worries immediately before an exam "significantly improved students' exam scores, especially for students habitually anxious about test taking". — [Ramirez & Beilock 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199427)
- [REPLICATION FAILED] The Social Sciences Replication Project (Camerer et al. 2018, *Nature Human Behaviour*) included this paper among 21 Nature/Science experiments; overall 13 of 21 (62%) replicated at about 50% of the original effect size, and for the 8 that did not replicate "the mean relative effect size is 0.3%". The FORRT replication atlas codes the Ramirez & Beilock replication as failed: the pooled test "fails to reach significance in the same direction as the original". — [Camerer et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z); [FORRT atlas entry](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1199427/)
- [FIELD] Myers, Davis & Chan (2021, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*): across four real exams in a psychology course, neither expressive writing nor an instructional intervention "was effective at reducing test anxiety or improving exam performance". — [Myers et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-021-00309-x)
- [META] O'Meara & Lovett (2025, *Anxiety, Stress & Coping*): 21 studies, N = 1,457. Effect on anxiety r = -.05 (n.s.); effect on test performance r = .09 (p = .02), falling to r = .06 (p = .06) after removing one extreme outlier. "The available research literature fails to support single sessions of expressive writing as a treatment for test anxiety." — [O'Meara & Lovett 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2552857)

**Arousal reappraisal**
- [META] Bosshard & Gomez (2024, *Scientific Reports* 14, 7923): 44 effect sizes from randomised trials of stress-arousal-reappraisal and stress-is-enhancing-mindset interventions. Overall d = 0.23 (p < .001); reappraisal-only d = 0.22 (k = 33); mindset-only d = 0.18 (n.s., k = 6); mixed interventions d = 0.45 (k = 5). Effect "tended to be larger for public performance tasks than cognitive written tasks" (d = 0.34, k = 14 vs d = 0.20, k = 30). Authors: "not 'silver bullets'". — [Bosshard & Gomez 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-58408-w)
- [LAB + FIELD follow-up][FULLTEXT][ANALOGY: GRE] Jamieson, Mendes, Blackstock & Schmader (2010, *JESP* 46, 208-212): 60 students recruited, 28 in the final sample (the paper notes "our 50% attrition rate"). Participants told that arousal improves performance scored higher on practice GRE maths (M = 738.57 vs 683.57, F(1,26) = 4.35, p = .047, d = .82) and on the real GRE maths one to three months later (M = 770.00 vs 705.71, F(1,26) = 6.85, p = .015, d = 1.03); no difference on verbal. With 14 per cell these effect sizes are very imprecise, and the meta-analytic estimate above (d ≈ 0.2) is the better guide. — [Jamieson et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2009.08.015)
- [LAB] Jamieson, Nock & Mendes (2012, *JEP: General* 141, 417-422): participants told to think of arousal during a stressful task as functional showed "increased cardiac efficiency and lower vascular resistance" and "decreased attentional bias" for negative information, relative to two control conditions. — [Jamieson et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025719)
- Brooks (2014) "I am excited" is covered in the earlier file and falls within the public-performance subgroup above.

**Self-talk in the second or third person**
- [LAB][ANALOGY: first-impression and public-speaking tasks] Kross et al. (2014, *JPSP* 106, 304-324), seven studies, total N = 585: using one's own name and non-first-person pronouns (rather than "I") during pre-task introspection led participants to perform "better according to objective raters" in a first-impressions task (Study 2) and a public-speaking task (Study 3), with less distress and less post-event rumination, and to appraise stressors "in more challenging and less threatening terms". Effects were not moderated by trait social anxiety. — [Kross et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035173)

**Slow breathing**
- [META] Fincham, Strauss, Montero-Marin & Cavanagh (2023, *Scientific Reports* 13, 432): 12 RCTs, 785 adults; breathwork vs non-breathwork controls on self-reported stress g = -0.35 (95% CI -0.55 to -0.14); anxiety g = -0.32 (k = 20); most studies at moderate risk of bias; authors "urge caution ... to avoid a miscalibration between hype and evidence". Outcome is felt stress, not task performance. — [Fincham et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)
- [LAB] Laborde et al. (2021/2022, *Journal of Psychophysiology* 36, 13-27): 78 participants, within-subject; after 3 × 5 minutes of slow-paced breathing vs watching television, performance was better on Stroop interference accuracy, operation span and card-sorting perseverative errors (not Stroop reaction time); the gain "was not mediated by cardiac vagal activity". Dose is 15 minutes, far longer than a 30-second window. — [Laborde et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1027/0269-8803/a000279)

**Power posing before an interview [REPLICATION PROBLEM]**
- [LAB] Cuddy, Wilmuth, Yap & Carney (2015, *J. Applied Psychology* 100, 1286-1295): participants who held expansive poses before a mock interview speech "performed better and were more likely to be chosen for hire", mediated by "nonverbal presence" and not by verbal content. — [Cuddy et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0038543)
- [META-SCIENCE] Simmons & Simonsohn (2017, *Psychological Science* 28, 687-693) p-curved the 33 studies cited in support of power posing: the distribution of p-values "is indistinguishable from what is expected if (1) the average effect size were zero, and (2) selective reporting ... were solely responsible for the significant effects"; "the existing evidence is too weak ... to advocate for people to engage in power posing". — [Simmons & Simonsohn 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616658563)

**How anxious speakers prepare**
- [LAB][ANALOGY: public speaking] Daly, Vangelisti & Weber (1995, *Communication Monographs* 62, 383-397): "People with high levels of speech anxiety engage in a variety of preparation actions that may limit the effectiveness of their presentations"; when anxiety is partialled out, the relationship between preparation process and speech quality "is much smaller". Anxiety damages preparation as well as delivery. — [Daly et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637759509376368)
- [FIELD] Schneider, Powell & Bonaccio (2019, *IJSA* 27, 328-336): among real applicants for residence-assistant posts, interview anxiety had "near-zero correlations with job performance". Interview anxiety lowers interview scores without signalling a weaker employee. — [Schneider et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12263)

### Inferences
- A one-way video answer combines both pressure types in sequence. The 30-second window is a working-memory task under outcome pressure (parse a novel question, inhibit the prepared script, pick a story): the distraction route. The recording itself is "monitoring by others" applied to a fluent skill (speaking): the explicit-monitoring route. DeCaro et al. imply the two phases need different protections: an externalised, low-load procedure for the first, and attention directed away from one's own delivery for the second. This mapping is my inference from category-learning experiments.
- Beilock & Carr (2005) implies the candidate's profile (high verbal ability, relies on working memory for quality) is the profile most exposed to distraction-type choking.
- The remedy with the best match to the candidate's situation is pressure training: practising with the camera recording, a visible countdown, one take, and a consequence (e.g. the recording is sent to someone), since both Beilock & Carr (2001) and Oudejans & Pijpers find that acclimatisation requires the pressure cue to be present in practice and that mild pressure is sufficient. Evidence is from sport and policing with small samples.
- A pre-performance routine is supported as a category (g ≈ 0.6 to 0.7) and the meta-analysis found that the type of routine did not moderate the effect, so the content can be chosen for the task (see section 5 on if-then plans). Evidence is from closed motor skills, not speech.
- Arousal reappraisal and distanced self-talk are cheap, fit in seconds, and have small positive effects in evaluated-speech tasks. Expressive writing, pre-task rituals and power posing should be dropped from any recommendation.
- Slow breathing lowers felt stress; the only performance evidence found used 15 minutes. It may fit before the interview session rather than inside the 30-second window. That placement is an inference.

### Gaps
- No randomised trial of any of these remedies with interview or AVI performance as the outcome (the earlier file reached the same conclusion; the additional searching here did not change it), other than the unsupported power-posing study.
- I did not find the study-level replication effect size for Ramirez & Beilock in Camerer et al.'s supplementary table; only the coded outcome.
- No pre-performance-routine study outside sport was found once the retracted rituals paper is excluded.
- I did not review the ego-depletion literature and no claim above depends on it.
- No study isolates the effect of a brief (one to three breaths) slow exhale on speaking performance.

## 3. Perfectionism, self-focus, avoidance goals, maximising versus satisficing, and recognition-primed choice

### Takeaway
Aiming high is not the problem; fear of falling short is. Meta-analytically, perfectionistic strivings relate positively to achievement (r ≈ .24) while perfectionistic concerns relate slightly negatively (r ≈ -.08; -.15 once strivings are controlled) and predict later anxiety. Experimentally induced self-focus and "catering" to what the evaluator is presumed to want both worsen rated performance. Under real time pressure, experts overwhelmingly act on the first workable option rather than comparing options, and in sport the first option generated is on average better than later ones. None of this has been tested on the choice of an interview answer.

### Cited Findings
- [META][FULLTEXT] Madigan (2019, *Educational Psychology Review* 31, 967-989): 37 studies, N = 8,901, 156 effect sizes. Perfectionistic strivings and academic achievement r+ = .24 (95% CI .21 to .27); perfectionistic concerns r+ = -.08 (95% CI -.12 to -.05); the two dimensions overlap r+ = .32; residual concerns (controlling strivings) r+ = -.15 (95% CI -.19 to -.12). Results vary by instrument: the "discrepancy" subscale (gap between standards and performance) showed the larger negative effect, r+ = -.16. — [Madigan 2019, accepted manuscript](https://ray.yorksj.ac.uk/3797/1/Madigan%20%28in%20press%29.pdf); [journal](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09484-2)
- [META][ANALOGY: sport] Hill, Mallinson-Howard & Jowett (2018, *Sport, Exercise, and Performance Psychology* 7, 235-270): 52 studies, 697 effect sizes. Strivings show "a small-to-medium relationship with better performance"; concerns show small-to-medium relationships with maladaptive motivation and emotion "and were unrelated to performance". "Perfectionistic concerns are clearly maladaptive for athletes whereas perfectionistic strivings are complex and ambiguous." — [Hill et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/spy0000125)
- [META] Smith et al. (2018, *Anxiety, Stress & Coping* 31, 4-20): 11 longitudinal studies; concern over mistakes (r+ = .11), doubts about actions (r+ = .13) and personal standards (r+ = .08) predicted increases in anxiety after controlling baseline anxiety. — [Smith et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2017.1384466)
- [META][ABSTRACT, truncated] Van Yperen, Blaga & Postmes (2014, *PLoS ONE* 9, e93594), across work, sport and education: "approach goals (either mastery or performance) were associated positively with performance attainment, whereas avoidance goals (either mastery or performance) were associated negatively with performance". I did not retrieve the coefficients. — [Van Yperen et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0093594)
- [LAB][ANALOGY: conversation, adolescents] Leigh, Chiu & Clark (2021, *PLoS ONE* 16, e0247703): 57 high and low socially anxious adolescents each held one conversation while instructed to focus on themselves and use safety behaviours, and one while focusing externally. "Self-focus and safety behaviours increased feelings and appearance of anxiety and undermined performance for all participants", as rated by self, partner and independent assessors. — [Leigh et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0247703)
- [FIELD + LAB][ABSTRACT; see caution] Gino, Sezer & Huang (2020, *OBHDP* 158, 83-100): people commonly "cater to the target's interests and expectations" in first meetings such as interviews; in a field study of entrepreneurs pitching to investors "catering harmed investors' evaluations, while being authentic improved them"; in experiments catering "feels instrumental and increases anxiety" and lowered performance relative to authentic and control conditions. **Caution:** another paper co-authored by the first author in the same journal has been retracted (see section 2), and I verified this paper only at abstract level; I did not check whether it has been re-examined. Treat as suggestive. — [Gino et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.01.003)
- [FIELD] Moore, Lee, Kim & Cable (2017, *J. Applied Psychology* 102, 1493-1513): in two field samples, among candidates the organisation rated in the top 10%, those with a stronger drive to "self-verify" (present themselves accurately) were significantly more likely to receive an offer. In a mock structured interview, text analysis showed they used language differently and an expert rater perceived them "as less inauthentic and less misrepresentative". The benefit is conditional on being a strong candidate. — [Moore et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000223)
- [LAB/survey] Schwartz et al. (2002, *JPSP* 83, 1178-1197): across seven samples maximising correlated negatively with happiness, optimism, self-esteem and life satisfaction and positively with depression, "perfectionism, and regret"; maximisers were less satisfied with decisions and more affected by upward social comparison. — [Schwartz et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.5.1178)
- [FIELD] Iyengar, Wells & Schwartz (2006, *Psychological Science* 17, 143-150): final-year students high in maximising "secured jobs with 20% higher starting salaries" than low maximisers, but were less satisfied and "experienced more negative affect throughout the job-search process", mediated by "fixation on realized and unrealized options". Maximising produced objectively better outcomes in a search with months available; the cost was affective. — [Iyengar et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01677.x)
- [FIELD, retrospective interviews][ANALOGY: firefighting] Klein, Calderwood & Clinton-Cirocco (1986/2010, *J. Cognitive Engineering and Decision Making* 4, 186-209): 26 experienced fireground commanders (mean 23 years), 156 decision points. "In less than 12% of them was there any evidence of simultaneous comparisons and relative evaluation of two or more options. In over 80% of the decision points" commanders recognised the situation as typical and took the action typical for it, "without any need to consider more than one option". — [Klein et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1518/155534310x12844000801203)
- [LAB][ANALOGY: handball][partly SECONDARY] "Take the first": Johnson & Raab (2003, *OBHDP* 91, 215-229) proposed that in ill-defined tasks people should take the first option they generate. Raab & Johnson (2007, *JEP: Applied* 13, 158-170), a two-year longitudinal study with eye tracking, found athletes of different expertise "generated the same number of options on average" but that options "differed in quality" by expertise; a database summary of the paper states that "the initial option was generally better than later ones" and that participants "often chose the first option". Laborde & Raab (2013) report replicating the take-the-first pattern in 90 players. — [Johnson & Raab 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0749-5978%2803%2900027-x); [Raab & Johnson 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898x.13.3.158); [Laborde & Raab 2013](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.35.4.339)
- [REVIEW] Hutton & Klein (1999): in "high stress, time-pressured, high stakes, or uncertain environments, the decision maker rapidly assesses the situation and implements a workable course of action", in contrast to exhaustive comparison of options. — [Hutton & Klein 1999](https://doi.org/10.1002/%28sici%291520-6858%281999%292:1%3C32::aid-sys3%3E3.0.co;2-p)

### Inferences
- The evidence does not say "lower your standards". It separates high standards (helpful or neutral) from concern over mistakes and the gap between the ideal and the delivered answer (harmful, anxiety-producing). A candidate who scripts "perfect" answers and then experiences every unscripted sentence as a shortfall is operating on the concerns/discrepancy side.
- Scripting to "what the firm wants to hear" after hours of firm research is close to what Gino et al. call catering. Their result (more anxiety, worse rated performance) and Moore et al.'s field result (accurate self-presentation helps strong candidates convert) point the same way, with the caution noted on the Gino paper.
- Thirty seconds is a recognition-primed-decision situation, not a maximising one. The RPD and take-the-first findings suggest committing to the first story that genuinely fits the question, then checking it once against the question, rather than scanning the whole story bank for the best one. There is an important condition: in RPD the first option is good because it is generated *from the situation*. A first option generated from the prepared script (Einstellung, section 1) is the failure case. So the sequence that the two literatures jointly imply is: read the question for what it asks, then take the first story that the question itself brings to mind. This is inference; no study tests it for interview answers.
- Iyengar et al. is a reminder that maximising is not irrational where time allows (preparation, building the story bank). The evidence only supports switching to satisficing inside the time-pressured window.

### Gaps
- No study of perfectionism (strivings or concerns) and interview or AVI performance was found.
- No study of maximising vs satisficing in selecting an answer under a preparation time limit.
- Klein, Wolf, Militello & Zsambok (1995) on chess players' first-generated move could not be retrieved (no abstract available), so its specific result is not cited.
- Van Yperen et al.'s effect sizes for performance-avoidance goals were not retrieved.

## 4. Practice design that transfers to unseen questions

### Takeaway
Rehearsing fixed answers to fixed questions is blocked, constant practice: it maximises fluency on those items and feels effective, but the transfer literature consistently favours mixed, varied retrieval. Interleaving beats blocking by about g = 0.42 overall (but not for all materials), retrieval practice transfers to new questions at about d = 0.40 mainly when practice is varied and elaborated, and retrieving the same knowledge in answer to *different* questions transfers better than answering the same question repeatedly. People spontaneously apply a relevant prior story to a new problem only about 30% of the time unless prompted; comparing two cases to extract the shared principle roughly triples transfer. All of this is from learning and negotiation research, not interviews.

### Cited Findings
- [LAB] Kornell & Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science* 19, 585-592): interleaving exemplars of different categories produced better induction than massing them, "even though massing apparently created a sense of fluent learning: Participants rated massing as more effective than spacing, even after their own test performance had demonstrated the opposite". — [Kornell & Bjork 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x)
- [META] Brunmair & Richter (2019, *Psychological Bulletin* 145, 1029-1052): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes. Overall interleaving effect g = 0.42; paintings g = 0.67; mathematical tasks g = 0.34; expository texts and tastes "ambiguous with nonsignificant overall effects"; **words g = -0.39 (blocking better)**. Stronger interleaving effects when categories are similar to each other. — [Brunmair & Richter 2019, preprint](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); [journal](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)
- [FIELD RCT] Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020, *J. Educational Psychology* 112, 40-52): preregistered cluster-randomised trial, 54 seventh-grade maths classes over four months. On an unannounced test a month later the interleaved group scored 61% vs 38% for blocked, d = 0.83. In interleaved assignments "no 2 consecutive problems require the same strategy". — [Rohrer et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)
- [FIELD] Sana & Yan (2022, *Psychological Science* 33, 782-788): 155 secondary students; concepts on interleaved quizzes were later recalled better than concepts on blocked quizzes (63% vs 54%, d = 0.35), which in turn beat unquizzed concepts (47%, d = 0.30). — [Sana & Yan 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976211057507)
- [META][ANALOGY: motor learning] Brady (2004, *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 99, 116-126): contextual interference (random vs blocked practice schedules), 139 effect sizes from 61 studies; overall ES = .38; basic lab research .57 vs applied research .19; adults .50 vs younger learners .10. — [Brady 2004](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.99.1.116-126)
- [FIELD-like][ANALOGY: badminton] Goode & Magill (1986): 30 learners practising three serves three days a week for three weeks; the random-schedule group "performed better on both retention and transfer than the blocked group", replicating Shea & Morgan (1979). — [Goode & Magill 1986](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.1986.10608091)
- [LAB] Butler (2010, *JEP: LMC* 36, 1118-1133), four experiments: repeated testing "produced superior retention and transfer on the final test relative to repeated studying", including to "new inferential questions from different knowledge domains". — [Butler 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019902)
- [LAB] Butler, Black-Maier, Raley & Marsh (2017, *JEP: Applied* 23, 433-446), four experiments: participants answered "either the same question three times or three different questions" about a concept; two days later, on new application questions, "variability during retrieval practice produced superior transfer". "Repeatedly retrieving and applying knowledge to different examples is a powerful method for acquiring knowledge that will transfer to a variety of new contexts." — [Butler et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000142)
- [META] Pan & Rickard (2018, *Psychological Bulletin* 144, 710-756): 192 transfer effect sizes, 122 experiments, N = 10,382. Testing yields transferable learning relative to re-exposure, d = 0.40 (95% CI 0.31 to 0.50). Transfer is strongest "to application and inference questions" and with "elaborated retrieval practice"; weakest "to rearranged stimulus-response items". After bias correction, intercepts often indicate "no positive transfer when none of the aforementioned moderators are present". — [Pan & Rickard 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)
- [LAB][FULLTEXT] Gick & Holyoak (1983, *Cognitive Psychology* 15, 1-38): after reading one analogous story, "only about 30% of our subjects produced the convergence solution" to a new problem without a hint, "as opposed to about 75% ... when a hint was given"; "only a third or less of the subjects who could potentially apply the analogy spontaneously noticed it". Summarising a single story, or being handed the principle, did not help; when "two prior analogs were given, subjects often derived a problem schema as an incidental product of describing the similarities", and "the quality of the induced schema was highly predictive of subsequent transfer". — [Gick & Holyoak 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285%2883%2990002-6)
- [LAB][SECONDARY from publisher page] Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner (1999, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 6, 586-597): business students who compared two negotiation cases "were nearly three times more likely to incorporate the strategy" into a later live negotiation than students who analysed the same two cases separately. — [Loewenstein et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03212967); [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/1999/analogical-encoding-facilitates-knowledge-transfer-in-negotiation)
- [LAB] Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003, *J. Educational Psychology* 95, 393-408): "a marked advantage for comparing two cases over studying the 2 cases separately"; more comparison support increased transfer "in a face-to-face dynamic negotiation exercise". — [Gentner et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393)
- [FIELD-like][ANALOGY: classroom speeches] Menzel & Carrell (1994, *Communication Education* 43, 17-26): 119 students' videotaped speeches. Rated quality correlated with total preparation time, silent and out-loud rehearsal, and "number of rehearsals for an audience"; in regression the significant predictors were GPA, total preparation time, **number of rehearsals for an audience**, and (negatively) state anxiety. Correlational. — [Menzel & Carrell 1994](https://doi.org/10.1080/03634529409378958)
- [LAB][ANALOGY: second-language speakers] Foster & Skehan (1996, *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 18, 299-323): giving speakers planning time before a speaking task had "strong effects ... on fluency and clear effects also on complexity"; **the most accurate performance came from the less detailed planners**, which the authors attribute to trade-offs in "limited capacity attentional resources". Planning helped narrative and decision tasks more than personal-information tasks. Participants were language learners, so transfer to native-language answers is uncertain. — [Foster & Skehan 1996](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0272263100015047)

### Inferences
- For someone who has rehearsed fixed answers to fixed questions, the literature predicts three specific problems: (i) the practice feels effective because it is fluent (Kornell & Bjork), (ii) it never practises the step that fails in the interview, namely identifying what an unfamiliar question is asking and selecting material for it (the Rohrer logic: blocked practice removes the "which strategy?" decision), and (iii) each story is bound to one retrieval cue, so a differently worded question does not retrieve it (Gick & Holyoak: only about 30% spontaneous transfer from a single stored case).
- The design implied by Butler et al. (2017), Rohrer et al. (2020) and Gentner et al. (2003) is: keep a small set of stories; practise by drawing an unseen question at random; decide which story fits and why; answer; and vary the question each time the same story is used. Comparing two of one's own stories to articulate what competency each evidences is the analogue of schema induction. This is a translation of the findings, not a tested interview-preparation protocol.
- Brunmair & Richter's negative effect for word lists is a caution against over-generalising: interleaving helps discrimination between similar categories, which is the relevant demand here (telling apart similar-sounding questions), but it does not help rote verbal memorisation. That fits the goal of dropping word-for-word scripts rather than interleaving them.
- Menzel & Carrell's "rehearsals for an audience" predictor, with section 2's pressure-training evidence, favours practice in front of a person or a live recording over silent or solo rehearsal.
- Foster & Skehan's finding that less detailed planners were the most accurate is consistent with using the 30 seconds for a skeletal plan (point, story, close) rather than composing sentences. It comes from language learners.

### Gaps
- No experiment compares blocked/scripted versus interleaved/varied-question interview preparation. The earlier file's coaching and practice evidence (Maurer, Tross & Maurer, Langer) does not manipulate practice schedule.
- "Encoding variability" and "indexing one story under several cues" (case-based reasoning) were not retrieved as primary sources; the support offered here is indirect (Butler et al. 2017; Gick & Holyoak 1983).
- Shea & Morgan (1979) was not retrieved directly; it is represented by a replication and a meta-analysis.
- No evidence on how many varied practice questions are needed before transfer appears.

## 5. Implementation intentions ("if-then" plans) as a way to automate a 30-second routine

### Takeaway
If-then plans have a medium-to-large average effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65 in the 2006 meta-analysis; d between 0.27 and 0.66 across outcome types in a 2024 update of 642 tests) and work better when phrased as a contingency, when motivation is high and when the plan is rehearsed. In the one study on test anxiety, the wording mattered sharply: a plan to ignore the distraction helped anxious students, while a plan to "focus harder" made them worse. No study has applied if-then plans to interview answering.

### Cited Findings
- [META][SECONDARY] Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69-119): "over 8,000 participants in 94 independent studies reported an effect size of d = 0.65", representing "the additional facilitation of goal achievement by implementation intentions compared to goal intentions alone". Figures as restated by the first author in a later chapter. — [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0065-2601%2806%2938002-1); restated in [Gollwitzer & Oettingen chapter](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/eb48c8cf-7855-478e-90ec-1ea6d44f8252/content)
- [META] Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024, *European Review of Social Psychology* 36, 162-194): 642 independent tests; implementation intentions "proved effective for cognitive, affective, and behavioural outcomes (.27 ≤ d ≤ .66)". "Effect sizes were larger when plans had a contingent (if-then) format, participants were highly motivated to pursue the goal, and plans were rehearsed." The taxonomy of plan contents includes cues such as "task juncture" and responses such as "cognitive procedures, ignore- or inner speech-responses". — [Sheeran et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)
- [LAB][FULLTEXT][ANALOGY: maths test with distraction] Parks-Stamm, Gollwitzer & Oettingen (2010, *Learning and Individual Differences* 20, 30-33): 51 undergraduates did 15 minutes of maths problems with distracting video clips, after forming either a temptation-inhibiting plan (of the form "if I hear a distraction, then I will ignore it") or a task-facilitating plan (focus harder on the exam when distracted). Plan × test-anxiety interaction β = .38, t(49) = 2.77, p < .01. Among high test-anxious students, the ignore plan produced far more completed problems than the focus-harder plan (M = 77.67, SD = 11.59 vs M = 54.36, SD = 15.71; t(21) = 3.82, p = .001). Within the focus-harder condition, high-anxious students did worse than low-anxious (54.36 vs 68.69, p = .03). No plan effect among low-anxious students. Small sample, single study. — [Parks-Stamm et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2009.09.001); [author PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/10_Parks_Stamm_Gollwitzer_Implementation_Intentions_Test_Anxiety.pdf)
- [FIELD][ANALOGY: dieting, competitive tennis] Achtziger, Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2008, *PSPB* 34, 381-393): two field experiments in which if-then plans were "geared at controlling potentially interfering inner states" (cravings; "disruptive thoughts, feelings, and physiological states" in athletes); "forming if-then plans enhanced the rate of goal attainment", showing that plans can shield ongoing performance as well as initiate action. — [Achtziger et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167207311201)
- Mechanism as stated by the authors: if-then plans work "by increasing the accessibility of the situational cue specified in the if-component and by automating the response specified in the then-component". — [Parks-Stamm et al. 2010](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/10_Parks_Stamm_Gollwitzer_Implementation_Intentions_Test_Anxiety.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 30-second window has clear "task junctures" that can serve as cues (the question appears; the countdown hits a number; the record light comes on), which is the cue type the 2024 taxonomy lists. A plan of the form "when the question appears, then I read the last clause again and say what it is asking in my own words" binds the anti-Einstellung check from section 1 to a cue, so it does not depend on remembering to do it under load. This specific plan is my construction; it is untested.
- Parks-Stamm et al. is a direct warning about wording for an anxious performer: "then I will concentrate harder" or "then I will give a perfect answer" is the type of plan that backfired; a concrete procedural or ignore-type response is the type that helped. One small study.
- The moderator finding (rehearsed plans work better) and section 4 point the same way: the routine has to be run on every practice question, not just written down.
- The routine should be short. Duncan's goal-neglect result (section 1) argues against a multi-step checklist; one or two if-then links is the form the evidence supports.

### Gaps
- No implementation-intention study uses interview, AVI or impromptu-speaking performance as an outcome.
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006) was verified through the authors' own later restatement, not the original chapter text.
- The 2024 meta-analysis abstract gives a range of d by outcome class, not a single pooled value or a value for performance-under-pressure outcomes.

## 6. Impromptu-speaking pedagogy and improvisation training

### Takeaway
The research base here is thin. Improv training has small controlled studies showing gains in divergent thinking, tolerance of uncertainty, positive affect and self-rated performance confidence, an uncontrolled study showing reduced social anxiety in adolescents, and one quasi-experiment showing a "significant but modest" gain in rated public-speaking competence. I found no peer-reviewed study of improv, Toastmasters Table Topics or debate training with interview performance as the outcome, and no controlled study of how to structure a sub-one-minute impromptu answer.

### Cited Findings
- [FIELD, uncontrolled][ABSTRACT] Felsman, Seifert & Himle (2019, *The Arts in Psychotherapy* 63, 111-117): 268 middle and high school students in a ten-week school improv programme, "single group pre/post design". Those who screened positive for social phobia at baseline "reported reduced symptoms of social anxiety at post-test"; the change predicted increases in "social skills, hope, creative self-efficacy, comfort performing for others, and willingness to make mistakes". No control group, so regression to the mean cannot be excluded. — [Felsman et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aip.2018.12.001)
- [LAB, randomised][ABSTRACT] Felsman, Gunawardena & Seifert (2020, *Thinking Skills and Creativity* 35, 100632): two experiments (n = 74; n = 131). Twenty minutes of improv exercises, versus a matched control involving social interaction, improved divergent thinking, "boost[ed] positive affect and increase[d] uncertainty tolerance". The authors note prior work "has generally lacked the rigor of randomized experiments". Outcomes are questionnaires and a divergent-thinking task, not speaking performance. — [Felsman et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tsc.2020.100632)
- [Controlled trial, waiting-list] Schwenke et al. (2020/2021, *J. Creativity in Mental Health* 16, 31-48): 30 intervention vs 28 waiting-list controls, six-week improv course; "significant improvement in participants' creativity and psychological well-being ... but no evidence for enhanced acceptance". — [Schwenke et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/15401383.2020.1754987)
- [Quasi-experiment] Casteleyn (2019, *L1-Educational Studies in Language and Literature* 19): improv training focused on eye contact, voice and body language for secondary students, with a control group and pre/post videos scored by comparative judgement. "The intervention had a significant but modest effect", with caveats about age, group size and instructor that were not controlled. — [Casteleyn 2019](https://doi.org/10.17239/l1esll-2019.19.03.04)
- [Conference proceedings] Seppänen (2021): across six improv interventions (n = 161), improvements relative to controls in self-reported "interpersonal confidence, performance confidence, and tolerance of failure", with performance confidence persisting. Self-report only. — [Seppänen 2021](https://doi.org/10.15405/epiceepsy.21101.1)
- [PRACTITIONER critique] In US collegiate limited-preparation events, coaching practice has drifted toward pre-prepared "canned" examples that are fitted to whatever prompt appears (see section 1). — [Richardson 2010](https://cornerstone.lib.mnsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1021&context=ndcieproceedings)
- [LAB][ANALOGY: L2 speakers] Short pre-task planning improves fluency and complexity of spoken answers, and less detailed planning was associated with the most accurate speech. — [Foster & Skehan 1996](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0272263100015047)
- [Search result, weak] A web search for peer-reviewed studies of Toastmasters "Table Topics" or impromptu-speaking instruction returned only a conference work-in-progress (one 95-minute impromptu lesson associated with lower self-reported communication apprehension in engineering students) and small uncontrolled ESL classroom studies. I did not read these in full and do not rely on them. — [ASEE work-in-progress paper](https://peer.asee.org/wip-reducing-public-communication-apprehension-through-a-targeted-intervention.pdf)

### Inferences
- What improv has evidence for is the disposition side: tolerating not knowing what comes next and being willing to make mistakes (Felsman 2019, 2020; Seppänen 2021). That is the opposite pole from perfectionistic concerns (section 3), so the mechanism is plausible for this candidate, but no study shows it improves the content or relevance of an answer.
- The debate background is double-edged on the evidence available. Richardson's account suggests competitive limited-prep formats can train the very habit at issue (fitting pre-packaged material to any prompt, rewarded for fluency). Rogers & Norton (section 1) show that fluent near-miss answers are rewarded by listeners who are not checking relevance, which is how a debating audience often listens. A structured-interview rater scoring against a competency is a different listener.
- Common structures taught for impromptu answers (point-reason-example-point and similar) are covered descriptively in the earlier file `answer_construction_and_rehearsal.md`; I found no experiment testing any of them.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of Toastmasters Table Topics, debate training or extemporaneous-speaking coaching on impromptu answer quality.
- No study of improv training with interview or AVI ratings as the outcome.
- No evidence on the optimal split of a 30-second preparation window.
- Felsman et al. (2019) is uncontrolled; the 2020 experiments measure immediate effects of a 20-minute session only.

## 7. Why a strong live debater may underperform to a camera with no audience

### Takeaway
Three documented mechanisms apply: speakers depend on moment-to-moment listener feedback and tell stories measurably worse when it is withdrawn; a camera and self-view raise self-focused attention and cognitive load, and experimentally induced self-focus lowers rated performance; and being recorded for later evaluation is the "monitoring" type of pressure that disrupts fluent skills. The one interview experiment on self-view found higher cognitive load but no direct drop in rated performance. No study compares the same speakers live versus in an asynchronous recording.

### Cited Findings
- [LAB] Bavelas, Coates & Johnson (2000, *JPSP* 79, 941-952): 63 unacquainted dyads; narrators told a close-call story. When listeners were distracted from the content, "listeners made fewer responses, especially specific ones, and the narrators also told their stories significantly less well, particularly at what should have been the dramatic ending". Listeners are "co-narrators". — [Bavelas et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.941)
- [LAB] Kraut, Lewis & Swezey (1982, *JPSP* 43, 718-731): 76 speakers summarised a film to listeners who gave varying feedback. "When speakers received more feedback, their narratives were more comprehensible"; feedback works by "coordinating what the speaker said with what the listener needed to know". — [Kraut et al. 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.43.4.718)
- [META] Bond & Titus (1983, *Psychological Bulletin* 94, 265-292): 241 studies, nearly 24,000 participants. Presence of others has small effects (0.3% to 3% of variance): it "increases the speed of simple task performance and decreases the speed of complex task performance", "impairs complex performance accuracy and slightly facilitates simple performance accuracy", and these effects "are surprisingly unrelated to the performer's evaluation apprehension". — [Bond & Titus 1983](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.94.2.265)
- [LAB] Being monitored by others selectively harms skills that "run best without heavy demands on working memory and attentional control". — [DeCaro et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023466)
- [LAB][INTERVIEW-SPECIFIC] Horn & Behrend (2017, *Personnel Assessment and Decisions* 3): 2 (picture-in-picture self-view vs none) × 2 (evaluative vs non-evaluative framing) experiment in a synchronous video interview. The self-view window "did not directly affect interview performance or applicant reactions, but did increase cognitive load". — [Horn & Behrend 2017](https://doi.org/10.25035/pad.2017.005)
- [SURVEY] Fauville, Luo, Queiroz, Bailenson & Hancock (2021, SSRN; journal version 2023 in *Computers in Human Behavior Reports*): large convenience sample (10,591 in the preprint abstract; 10,322 in the university release; 9,787 in the journal version). Five nonverbal mechanisms predicted videoconference fatigue: mirror anxiety, feeling physically trapped, hyper-gaze, and the cognitive load of producing and of interpreting nonverbal cues. Mirror anxiety "mediates the gender difference in fatigue". Outcome is fatigue, not performance. — [Fauville et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3820035); [Stanford summary](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2021/04/zoom-fatigue-worse-women)
- [SURVEY][SECONDARY from press release and abstract record] Kuhn (2022, *Computers in Human Behavior* 128, 107110): two studies of regular virtual-meeting attendees. No simple correlation between self-view frequency and attitudes; the effect is "moderated by trait public self-consciousness": for highly self-conscious people more self-view went with worse attitudes. Attitudes only; no performance measure. — [Kuhn 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.107110); [WSU release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2022/01/03/not-everybody-hates-looking-at-themselves-on-zoom/)
- [LAB] Experimentally instructed self-focus "undermined performance for all participants" in conversation, on partner and independent-assessor ratings (N = 57 adolescents). — [Leigh et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0247703)
- [LAB] In a real-time video conversation with a confederate (27 high, 36 low socially anxious participants), holding a negative self-image in mind raised anxiety during the conversation for the highly socially anxious. — [Meral & Vriends 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/s1352465821000424)
- [FIELD, qualitative] Rivera (2015, *American Journal of Sociology* 120, 1339-1389), elite professional service firms: evaluators "use subjective feelings of excitement and enthusiasm toward candidates", akin to interaction-ritual "emotional energy", to make hiring decisions in live interviews. This is energy built jointly in interaction. — [Rivera 2015](https://doi.org/10.1086/681214)
- Already in the earlier file and consistent with the above: AVIs are perceived as lowest in social presence ([Basch et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1108/jmp-09-2018-0398), 154 working adults, perceptions only); higher perceived social presence went with lower interview anxiety; about 80% of AVI interviewees keep talking after their answer is complete, interpreted as coping with absent listener feedback.

### Inferences
- A debater's fluency is tuned by a live room: timing, emphasis and when to stop are steered by listener responses (Bavelas; Kraut). An AVI removes that channel entirely, so the debater loses a control signal they normally rely on, and Bavelas's result predicts the loss shows up most at the ending of a story, which is where STAR answers carry the result and reflection.
- Social facilitation itself is a small effect and does not explain much. The larger issues are the missing feedback loop and the shift of attention to self (self-view, the knowledge of being recorded).
- Horn & Behrend's null on performance with a positive effect on load suggests self-view is a tax, not a catastrophe, in general; Kuhn and the social-anxiety studies suggest the tax is larger for people high in public self-consciousness or anxious self-image. Whether the candidate is in that group is unknown.
- Practical implication, as inference: reduce self-focus cues where the platform allows (hide or cover the self-view after framing is checked) and rehearse to a camera so the absence of feedback is itself practised (section 2, pressure training). Supplying a stand-in listener during practice recordings would test whether feedback loss is the active ingredient for this candidate; that is a diagnostic suggestion, not a finding.

### Gaps
- No within-person comparison of live versus asynchronous-recorded speaking performance in debaters or anyone else.
- No experiment on self-view in an asynchronous interview (Horn & Behrend was synchronous).
- Fauville et al. and Kuhn measure fatigue and attitudes, not rated performance.
- I could not retrieve the abstract of Vriends et al. (2017, "How do I look? Self-focused attention during a video chat of women with social anxiety") and do not report its results.

## 8. Charisma-type delivery: smiling, humour, warmth versus competence, authenticity, charisma tactics, vocal enthusiasm

### Takeaway
The evidence does not support "be more charming" as a lever. Smiling's effect depends on the job and on timing: in the one experiment, more smiling predicted lower hireability for a serious-demeanour job, and smiling less in the middle of the interview than at the start and end was best; genuine-looking dynamic smiles beat fake ones. Humour raises perceived status and competence only when it is appropriate and visibly lands, and a recorded answer has no audience to make it land; an inappropriate joke lowers competence and status by about a full scale point. In real interviews, perceived competence, not warmth, carried the effect of impression management on ratings. Charisma training produces moderate gains in rated charisma (D ≈ 0.6) through mostly verbal, content-structuring tactics. Confident vocal delivery (louder, more varied volume) persuades without costing sincerity.

### Cited Findings

**Smiling**
- [LAB][ABSTRACT] Ruben, Hall & Schmid Mast (2015, *J. Social Psychology* 155, 107-126). Pre-test: "Newspaper reporter applicants were expected to be more serious than applicants for other jobs." Study 1 (participants randomly assigned as applicant or interviewer for a reporter job): "Smiling was negatively related to hiring, and smiling mediated the relation between applicants' motivation to make a good impression and hiring. Hiring was maximized when applicants smiled less in the middle of the interview relative to the start and end." Study 2 (observers told the same clips were for one of four jobs): "Participants rated more suitability when applicants smiled less, especially for jobs associated with a serious demeanor." Coefficients and sample sizes not retrieved. — [Ruben et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224545.2014.972312)
- [LAB][SECONDARY from repository abstract] Krumhuber, Manstead, Cosker, Marshall & Rosin (2009, *J. Nonverbal Behavior* 33, 1-15): observers watched clips of female interviewees with dynamic authentic smiles, dynamic fake smiles or neutral expressions. Authentically smiling interviewees "were judged more suitable and were more likely to be short-listed and selected for the job"; authentic smiles led to more favourable ratings than fake smiles; the timing dynamics of the smile mattered for both human and synthetic faces. — [Krumhuber et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10919-008-0056-8)
- [META] Martín-Raugh, Kell, Randall, Anguiano-Carrasco & Banfi (2023, *J. Organizational Behavior* 44, 132-156): 63 studies, N = 4,868. Strongest nonverbal correlates of interview ratings: professional appearance ρ = .62, eye contact ρ = .45, head movement ρ = .43. Results "were largely unaffected by interview structure, modality, or duration"; effects were stronger in experimental than observational designs, and "certain nonverbal cues (e.g., facial expressions and professional appearance)" mattered more for women than men. — [Martín-Raugh et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.2670); design moderator and gender detail from the [authors' SPSP summary](https://spsp.org/news/character-and-context-blog/mart%C3%ADn-raugh-job-interview-nonverbal-cues)
- Earlier file, for scale: in field data a visual-cue composite including smiling correlated r = .21 with interviewer judgments; in machine-learning models nonverbal cues carried about 9% of the weight for rated conscientiousness and "variability in smile intensity" predicted rated extraversion.

**Humour**
- [LAB/vignette][FULLTEXT] Bitterly, Brooks & Schweitzer (2017, *JPSP* 112, 431-455), eight experiments. Study 2b (N = 274, job-interview vignette): asked "Where do you see yourself in five years?", the candidate gave a serious answer or the joke "Celebrating the fifth year anniversary of you asking me this question"; success was manipulated by stating whether the manager laughed. Status (7-point): serious 4.15, successful joke 4.95, failed joke 4.22 (η² = .11); competence: 4.73, 5.21, 4.63 (η² = .06); confidence: 4.73, 6.10, 5.52 (η² = .17). The failed appropriate joke did not differ from the serious answer on status (p = .66): it signalled confidence but added no competence. Study 3a (N = 274, inappropriate joke): status serious 4.58 vs inappropriate-but-laughed-at 3.86 vs inappropriate-and-silence 3.30 (η² = .22); competence 5.08 vs 3.92 vs 3.27 (η² = .31). Study 1a used a live confederate in a lab group (160 adults) and found a funny, appropriate remark raised status. — [Bitterly et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000079); [author PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/2016-54537-001.pdf)
- [LAB/vignette][FULLTEXT for the figures quoted] Bitterly & Schweitzer (2019, *OBHDP* 151, 73-89), five studies. In an interview vignette on a "greatest weakness" question, participants said they were more likely to hire a candidate who disclosed the weakness with a joke ("I really don't have a good memory. So remind me… what was the question?") than one who disclosed it plainly (M = 5.06 vs 4.16 on a 7-point scale, t(299) = 4.88, p < .001) or declined to name a weakness (M = 3.60). Mechanism: the joke lowers the perceived truth of the negative disclosure. The abstract: "when a target discloses negative information ... the use of humor can boost perceptions of warmth and competence". Vignette participants were told explicitly that the remark was intended as humour. — [Bitterly & Schweitzer 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2019.01.005); [author PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/The-impression-management-benefits-of-humorous-self-disclosures-How-humor-influences-perceptions-of-veracity..pdf)
- [LAB with hiring managers] Gloor, Cooper, Bowes-Sperry & Chawla (2022, *J. Applied Psychology* 107, 932-952), three experiments with hiring managers: a positive applicant pun reduced evaluators' interpersonal anxiety and its downstream effects on willingness to hire "for female applicants", but "the opposite was true for male applicants when gender was salient". — [Gloor et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000937)

**Warmth versus competence in hiring**
- [FIELD] Amaral, Powell & Ho (2019, *IJSA* 27, 315-327), "using real employment interviews": "competence mediates the relationship between self-promotion and interview ratings, but warmth did not mediate the relationship between ingratiation and interview ratings in the way we expected". — [Amaral et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12260)
- [FIELD] Barrick, Swider & Stewart (2010): early impressions of *competence* carried over to other interviewers' ratings and to offers; liking and similarity did not (detail in earlier file). — [Barrick et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019918)
- [FIELD, qualitative] Rivera (2012, *American Sociological Review* 77, 999-1022): 120 interviews with evaluators at elite professional service firms plus observation of a hiring committee. "Employers sought candidates who were not only competent but also culturally similar to themselves in terms of leisure pursuits, experiences, and self-presentation styles", and such concerns "often outweighed concerns about absolute productivity". This concerns live, later-round interviews. — [Rivera 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122412463213)
- [META, earlier file] Self-promotion relates more strongly to structured-interview ratings than ingratiation (r = .26 vs .13; Levashina et al. 2014 as reported in Peck & Levashina 2017).
- [SECONDARY, mixed] A search on warmth-versus-competence weighting in hiring experiments returned studies in which competence was the dominant dimension (particularly for male candidates) and Cuddy's general claim that warmth is judged first and weighs more in overall person evaluation. I did not retrieve the text of Cuddy, Glick & Beninger (2011) and cannot say what it concludes about hiring specifically. — [Cuddy et al. 2011 record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.riob.2011.10.004)

**Authenticity and "trying too hard"**
- [FIELD] Strong candidates with a stronger drive to present themselves accurately were more likely to receive offers, and were perceived as "less inauthentic and less misrepresentative" in a mock structured interview. — [Moore et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000223)
- [FIELD + LAB; see caution in section 3] Catering to the evaluator's presumed preferences lowered investor evaluations of pitches and raised the speaker's anxiety. — [Gino et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.01.003)
- [LAB + field; same first-author caution applies to a co-author] Sezer, Gino & Norton (2018, *JPSP* 114, 52-74), nine studies: humblebragging is "less effective than straightforward bragging", reducing liking and perceived competence "because it is seen as insincere". — [Sezer et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000108)
- [REVIEW] Steinmetz, Sezer & Sedikides (2017): self-presenters "commonly mismanage their impressions without recognizing it", through failed perspective-taking. — [Steinmetz et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12321)
- [LAB] Roulin, Bangerter & Levashina (2015, *Personnel Psychology* 68, 395-444): five studies, 246 professional and 270 novice interviewers. "Interviewers' attempts to detect applicants' IM were often unsuccessful", but "interviewers' perceptions of IM use by applicants were related to their evaluations". What the rater believes the candidate is doing matters more than what the candidate is actually doing. — [Roulin et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12079)

**Charisma training and which tactics moved ratings**
- [FIELD experiment + LAB][ABSTRACT; tactic list SECONDARY] Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2011, *Academy of Management Learning & Education* 10, 374-396). Study 1: 34 middle managers randomised to training or control; co-worker ratings three months later (343 raters at time 1, 321 at time 2). Study 2: 41 MBA students gave a speech, were trained, and redelivered it six weeks later; 135 independent assessors rated the videos. "The training had significant effects on ratings of leader charisma (mean D = .62)." The trained tactics, per the British Psychological Society's summary, were nine verbal (metaphor; stories and anecdotes; contrasts; lists; rhetorical questions; expressing moral conviction; reflecting the group's sentiments; setting high expectations; communicating confidence) and three nonverbal (animated voice, facial expression, gesture). The paper does not, as far as I could verify, report which individual tactic drove the effect. — [Antonakis et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.5465/amle.2010.0012); [BPS summary](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/charisma-involves-teachable-behaviours)
- [FIELD + LAB experiments] Antonakis, d'Adda, Weber & Zehnder (2022, *Management Science* 68, 6355-6381): temporary workers who heard a charismatic version of a motivational speech "increase[d] their output by about 17% relative to workers who listen to a standard speech", comparable to high-powered financial incentives; in lab public-goods games more charismatic elements raised contributions "by up to 19%", but "the effectiveness of charisma varies and appears to depend on the social context". The speeches were delivered by an actor; the outcome is listener effort, not ratings of the speaker. — [Antonakis et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2021.4219)

**Vocal enthusiasm and confidence**
- [LAB] Van Zant & Berger (2020, *JPSP* 118, 661-682), four experiments plus replication: speakers trying to persuade through voice alone "tend to speak louder and vary their volume", "both of which signal confidence and, in turn, facilitate persuasion"; such attempts "succeed because they make communicators seem more confident without undermining their perceived sincerity", even when listeners detect the attempt. — [Van Zant & Berger 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000193)
- Earlier file, for scale: a vocal-cue composite correlated r = .20 with interviewer judgments in field data; the same words are judged more competent when heard than when read.

**Asynchronous-interview-specific**
- [SURVEY] Potential applicants expect less opportunity to use impression management in asynchronous than in videoconference or face-to-face interviews, and this (with lower social presence) mediated lower fairness perceptions. — [Basch et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1108/jmp-09-2018-0398)
- [LAB] In a mock AVI (N = 175), participants who used more preparation time performed better "while engaging in slightly less honest impression management"; those who re-recorded more performed better and used less deceptive IM. — [Roulin et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/1359432x.2022.2156862)
- [META] The nonverbal-cue relationships in Martín-Raugh et al. were "largely unaffected by ... modality". — [Martín-Raugh et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.2670)

### Inferences
- Smiling: the supportable position is "a genuine smile at the opening and close; do not hold a smile through the substantive middle". For analytical finance roles, which sit nearer the serious-demeanour end, Ruben et al. suggest continuous smiling is more likely to cost than to help. Their mediation result is pointed: the more applicants *wanted* to impress, the more they smiled, and that smiling lowered hiring. Evidence is one lab study with students plus one perception study.
- Humour: in every Bitterly study the benefit required the joke to be appropriate *and* the audience to laugh; the laughing manager is what converted confidence into competence. In a recording there is no laugh, and the candidate cannot see whether it landed, so the realistic best case is the "failed appropriate joke" cell (no status gain, some confidence signal) and the downside is the inappropriate cell (competence down by more than one point on a seven-point scale). Gloor et al. add a gender-contingent risk. The evidence supports not planning humour in a one-way interview, with the narrow exception that light self-deprecating humour around a weakness disclosure helped in vignettes.
- Warmth vs competence: in the only field mediation test (Amaral et al.), competence carried the effect; in Barrick et al., competence impressions transferred across interviewers and liking did not. In an AVI the rater is always a "different interviewer" with no rapport stage, which is the condition under which liking effects were absent. Rivera's cultural-fit findings concern live later rounds and do not obviously transfer to a first-round recording scored against a rubric.
- "Sounding natural": the support is indirect. Authentic self-presentation helped strong candidates in the field (Moore), catering raised anxiety and lowered evaluations (Gino, with caution), and tactics perceived as insincere backfire (Sezer; Roulin). There is still no direct test of memorised versus extemporaneous delivery of the same content on authenticity ratings (a gap already recorded in the earlier file).
- Charisma tactics: nine of the twelve trained tactics in Antonakis et al. are ways of organising *content* (a story, a contrast, a three-part list, a rhetorical question, stated conviction). For this candidate that is useful: they are debate-native devices that can be applied to whatever story fits the question, they need no smiling or humour, and they are compatible with rubric-based scoring because they add structure rather than replace substance. The size (D ≈ 0.6 on rated charisma) is for leaders' speeches, not interview answers.
- Voice: speaking somewhat louder with more variation in volume is the one delivery adjustment with experimental evidence that it raises perceived confidence without a sincerity cost.

### Gaps
- Ruben et al. (2015): sample sizes and coefficients not retrieved (publisher and repository copies blocked). No study of smiling by job type in finance roles.
- Martín-Raugh et al. (2023): the meta-analytic estimate for smiling specifically was not in any accessible summary; only the top three cues are reported in the abstract.
- No study of candidate humour in real (non-vignette) selection interviews, and none in asynchronous interviews.
- Cuddy, Glick & Beninger (2011) not retrieved; no quantitative synthesis of warmth versus competence weights in hiring was found.
- Antonakis et al. (2011) does not appear to decompose the effect by tactic; I could not confirm this from the full text.
- No study of charisma training with interview ratings as the outcome.
- I did not check the post-publication status of Gino, Sezer & Huang (2020) or Sezer, Gino & Norton (2018).

## 9. Thanking the viewer, small talk or rapport statements at the start of an answer

### Takeaway
I found no study that measures the effect of a candidate's greeting, thanks or rapport statement on ratings in an asynchronous video interview. The live-interview evidence shows that first impressions formed during a rapport-building stage carry into later ratings (r = .42) and offers (r = .22), but the part that transfers across interviewers is the impression of competence, not liking, and the effect fades over the course of the structured questions. An AVI has no rapport stage and the rater is not a conversational partner.

### Cited Findings
- [FIELD] Barrick, Swider & Stewart (2010, *J. Applied Psychology* 95, 1163-1172): candidates making better initial impressions during rapport building "received more internship offers (r = .22) and higher interviewer ratings (r = .42)". Initial competence evaluations added ΔR² = .05 to outcomes from the same interviewer, a separate interviewer, and "another interviewer who skipped rapport building"; "assessments of candidate liking and similarity were not significantly related to other judgments when ratings were provided by different interviewers". — [Barrick et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019918)
- [LAB] Swider, Barrick & Harris (2016, *J. Applied Psychology* 101, 625-638): 163 mock interviews. Rapport building "may contaminate interviewers' evaluations" (citing Levashina et al. 2014); "a significant portion of initial impressions' influence overlaps with job-related interview content"; initial impressions "more strongly relate to interviewer evaluations of applicant responses earlier rather than later in the structured interview". — [Swider et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000077)
- [META, earlier file] Other-focused tactics (ingratiation, which is where thanking and complimenting sit) relate only weakly to structured-interview ratings (r = .13), and in real interviews warmth did not mediate the ingratiation-rating link. — [Amaral et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsa.12260)
- [AVI, different question] The only AVI rapport-type manipulation a targeted search surfaced was on the employer side: a relatedness-supportive introductory video raised applicants' need satisfaction and organisational attraction. It did not concern applicant behaviour or ratings of applicants. I saw this at search-summary level only. — [search-surfaced record](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/psychology/98320886-7a1e-5188-86ed-e89ee21a301b/i-can-t-get-no-need-satisfaction-using-a-relatedness-need-supportive-intervention-to-improve-applicant-reactions-to-asynchronous-video-interviews)
- [LAB] Material that does not answer the question is noticed more, and penalised, when the rater is attending to relevance or has the question in view. — [Rogers & Norton 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023439)

### Inferences
- There is no evidential basis for opening an AVI answer with thanks or small talk. The live-interview first-impression effect runs through early evidence of competence, which in a recorded answer means the first substantive sentence. This agrees with the earlier file's finding that delays before answering and slow starts are penalised.
- Because first-impression effects were strongest on the earliest structured questions (Swider et al.), if any single answer deserves the most careful opening it is the first question of the set. That is an extrapolation from live mock interviews.
- A brief natural greeting on the first question only is unlikely to matter either way; the evidence is silent. Repeating a thanks formula at the start of every answer spends scarce seconds on content that no cited study rewards.

### Gaps
- No experiment manipulating greeting, thanks, or a rapport statement in an asynchronous interview with rater scores as the outcome.
- No evidence on whether AVI raters form a "first impression" on the first answer that carries across later answers in the way Swider et al. found for live interviews (the earlier file's section on rater order effects is the nearest material).
- No evidence on closing statements ("thank you for your time") either.
