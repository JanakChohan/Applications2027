## 1. Structured interview design: how a question is written to target one competency, and what the scoring key looks like for each question type

### Takeaway
A structured-interview question is written backwards from a named competency: subject-matter experts draft the question so that it can only be answered with evidence of that competency, then write the "representative responses" that define each score level before any candidate is seen. The published keys show a consistent pattern: past-behaviour keys score the difficulty of the situation handled and the specificity of personal action; situational keys score the choice made in a dilemma, where "refuse and comply" is only the middle band and the top band adds proactive steps; strengths keys score energy and authenticity as well as content; and motivation questions are scored as fit against a pre-defined profile of the job, the organisation and the location.

### Cited Findings

**How questions are written to target a competency (designer's procedure)**
- The US Office of Personnel Management (OPM) practical guide says interview questions should be "Reflective of competencies derived from a job analysis", "Realistic of the responsibilities of the job", "Open-ended", "Clear and concise", and that when experts write behavioural questions "Each question should measure at least one of the specified competencies" and "should be written to elicit specific details about a situation, task, or context, the actions the person took or did not take, and the impact of these actions." Verbatim from PDF. — [OPM, Structured Interviews: A Practical Guide (Sept 2008), archived copy](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- OPM tells question writers to "use superlative adjectives in the questions (e.g., most, last, worst, least) to help the candidate focus on specific incidents." Verbatim. — [OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- OPM states the two formats "measure different aspects of job performance." Behavioural premise: "the best predictor of future behavior on the job is past behavior under similar circumstances." Situational premise: "a person's intentions are closely tied to his/her actual behavior." Verbatim. — [OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- Situational questions are built from critical incidents: experts "write examples of effective and ineffective behaviors (i.e., critical incidents) which reflect the competencies and associated tasks", and a separate group of experts must independently agree which competency each incident illustrates. Verbatim. — [OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- Latham's own account (2021): the situational interview rests on job analysis by the critical incident technique, "the context, behavior, and outcomes described in a critical incident are turned into a question"; each question must contain a dilemma, "the dilemma confronting an individual is having to choose between two or more exclusive courses of action"; the premise is that "intentions predict behavior"; subject-matter experts write a behavioural scoring guide; two or more raters score each answer independently; questions are pilot-tested and those where most people give the same answer are dropped; the experiments used a 1 (unacceptable) to 5 (outstanding) scale. Short quotes verbatim, rest as summarised by the fetch tool. — [Latham & Itzchakov, Frontiers in Psychology, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.674815/full)
- Example of a Latham-style dilemma question quoted in that paper (from Klehe & Latham 2005): a student project group faces a tight deadline; one member who led successfully last term controls the minutes and information flow and seems to record only ideas supporting her own position; "What would you do?" The stated dilemma is meeting the deadline and getting a high grade versus making sure teammates' input counts. Paraphrased by the fetch tool. — [Latham & Itzchakov 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.674815/full)
- Campion, Palmer & Campion (1997, Personnel Psychology 50(3), 655-702) identify "Fifteen components of structure" that "may enhance either the content of the interview or the evaluation process in the interview"; a dissertation quoting the paper says the authors regarded asking the same questions as "the most basic way to convert the interview from a conversation into a scientific measurement" (snippets; full paper not retrieved). — [ProQuest record](https://www.proquest.com/docview/220138496); [secondary quote](https://mountainscholar.org/bitstreams/abda339c-5ee2-4466-8acc-cbd0207ef2f9/download)
- Levashina, Hartwell, Morgeson & Campion (2014, Personnel Psychology 67, 241-293) review eight topics including "comparing situational versus past-behavior questions", "developing rating scales" and "probing, follow-up, prompting, and elaboration on questions"; a slide summary of the paper reports that research on probing is close to non-existent (snippets; full paper not retrieved). — [USU Digital Commons record](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/manage_facpub/423/); [slide summary](https://fr.slideshare.net/brijenk/the-structured-employment-interviewnarrative-and-quantitative-review-of-the-research-literature)
- SHL's Universal Competency Framework, which many graduate competency lists are mapped to, defines competencies "clearly and objectively, in terms of observable behaviors that are instrumental in the delivery of desired results (Bartram, 2005)" and says they are "often aligned to the organization's values, vision, or strategy". Its top tier is "8 Broad Factors": "Leading & Deciding", "Supporting & Co-operating", "Interacting & Presenting", "Analyzing & Interpreting", "Creating & Conceptualizing", "Organizing & Executing", "Adapting & Coping", "Enterprising & Performing"; beneath them sit "20 Competencies" and "96 Skills". SHL's design advice: "each skill or competency should capture a single, distinct capability" and "Support each skill with positive and negative behavioral indicators". Verbatim from PDF. — [SHL, A Guide to SHL's Universal Competency Framework (2025)](https://www.shl.com/assets/premium-content/shl-universal-competency-framework-ucf-guide.pdf)
- UK Civil Service recruiter guidance: "it is recommended that no more than four behaviours are prioritised as critical to the role. This will allow the panel to sufficiently test the criteria at interview stage"; "Interview questions should be designed to allow the candidate ample opportunity to provide the required level of evidence"; "Panel members should observe, record, classify, evaluate and score each question independently". Verbatim from PDF. — [Cabinet Office, Guidance: Application of Success Profile Guides (EO to Grade 6)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5f746b1ae90e0740caae75a7/Guidance-Application_of_Success_Profile_Guides_during_delegated_grade__EO-Grade_6__recruitment_v0b.pdf)

**(a) Past-behaviour questions (Janz's behaviour description interview): published scoring keys**
- Janz (1982) is the standard citation for the behaviour description format in which candidates describe past experiences that show job-relevant skills; a 2024 study reports that only about 41-48% of candidates describe a maximal-performance experience when asked a standard past-behaviour question, which the authors treat as a scoring problem and address by rewording questions to target top-end experiences (snippet). — [Personnel Assessment and Decisions, Jan 2024, DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/db6fe9dfb112448497863e3a2acd6f0d)
- OPM published key, competency "Interpersonal Skills". Question: "Describe a situation in which you had to deal with individuals who were difficult, hostile, or distressed. Who was involved? What specific actions did you take and what was the result?" Level definitions (verbatim): Level 5 Expert "Applies the competency in exceptionally difficult situations. Serves as a key resource and advises others."; Level 4 Advanced "Applies the competency in considerably difficult situations. Generally requires little or no guidance."; Level 3 Intermediate "Applies the competency in difficult situations. Requires occasional guidance."; Level 2 Basic "Applies the competency in somewhat difficult situations. Requires frequent guidance."; Level 1 Awareness "Applies the competency in the simplest situations. Requires close and extensive guidance." — [OPM guide, Table 2](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- Question-specific behavioural examples in the same key (verbatim): Level 1 "Refers employees to the appropriate staff member to resolve their issues."; Level 3 "Remains courteous and tactful when confronted by an employee who is frustrated by a payroll problem." and "Restores a working relationship between angry co-workers who have opposing views."; Level 5 "Diffuses an emotionally charged meeting with external stakeholders by expressing empathy for their concerns." and "Presents shortcomings of a newly installed HR automation system in a tactful manner to irate senior management officials." Interviewers are told to use these "as a general guide (not an absolute)". — [OPM guide, Table 2](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- A shorter OPM slide version of the same key anchors the scale on tension: 1 Low "Handles interpersonal situations involving little or no tension or discomfort"; 3 Average "... involving a moderate degree of tension or discomfort"; 5 Outstanding "... involving a high degree of tension or discomfort". Verbatim from PDF. — [OPM, Structured Interview: Example Question](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/examples/structured-interview-example.pdf)
- University of South Carolina HR published key, competency "PERFORMANCE UNDER STRESS: The degree to which the applicant possesses the ability to react calmly and rationally under stress." Question: "Describe a situation in which you had to perform under stress. What did you do, and why did you do these things? What in your background demonstrates that you possess the ability to react appropriately in stressful situations?" Anchors (verbatim): "5 OUTSTANDING - The applicant described a situation of direct relevance in which he or she performed successfully under stress. The applicant's examples provided clear evidence of calm, rational performance."; "3 SATISFACTORY - The applicant has had some exposure to situations involving stress and seems to have handled himself/herself reasonably well. The applicant is able to articulate reasonable strategies for handling such situations."; "1 MARGINAL - The applicant has not been exposed to situations involving mental or physical stress but is able to articulate reasonably strategies for handling such situations."; "0 UNACCEPTABLE - The applicant has handled himself/herself poorly in stressful situations. Or, the applicant is unable to articulate reasonable strategies for handling such situations. Or, something in the applicant's background indicates potential problems in this area. Or, his or her performance during the interview indicates difficulty reacting appropriately under stress." Scores 2 and 4 are "Intermediate score". — [USC HR, Rating Guide (rev. 09/2016)](https://www.biomedical.sc.edu/about/offices_and_divisions/human_resources/docs/rating_guide.pdf)
- Same guide on why keys exist: "Too often, panel members have varying opinions of what constitutes a 'good' answer to the interview question and, as a result, come to different conclusions about the candidates." Verbatim. — [USC HR Rating Guide](https://www.biomedical.sc.edu/about/offices_and_divisions/human_resources/docs/rating_guide.pdf)
- A 1993 study built standard scoring anchors for 168 generic past-behaviour questions and reported average inter-rater reliability of 0.65 (snippet). — [Development of Standard Anchors for Scoring Generic Past-Behaviour Questions, catalogue record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........1e427ad2bb72efbc93b78accd4534ec2)
- UK Civil Service definition of what a behaviour question collects: behaviours are "the actions and activities that people do which result in effective performance in a job"; "As behaviours can be observed and measured, they can help us to predict what your performance in the role could be." The descriptors work as an indicator list, not a checklist: "There is no expectation that you need to demonstrate every part of each example to be successful." Verbatim. — [gov.uk, Success Profiles: Civil Service behaviours](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-civil-service-behaviours)

**(b) Situational ("what would you do if") questions: published benchmark answers**
- OPM published key for a situational integrity question (verbatim). Competency "Integrity/Honesty: Contributes to maintaining the integrity of the organization; displays high standards of ethical conduct and understands the impact violating these standards would have on an organization, self, and others; is trustworthy." Question: "You are investigating a group of auto dealership managers suspected of money-laundering activities. During the course of an interview with one suspect, the suspect offers to help you buy a car at a price you know is well below market value. What would you do?" Benchmarks: "Unsatisfactory: Accept the offer." "Satisfactory: Say no to the offer and continue the investigation; document the incident in your report." "Superior: Probe the dealership managers to determine how they are able to offer a car at such a reduced price; attempt to get contact information of others involved; say no to the offer; and document the details of the incident." — [OPM guide, Table 3](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- OPM's procedure for writing those benchmarks: for each scenario each expert "individually determine[s] how actual employees at each proficiency level might behave (i.e., what their answers would be)"; scales should "Have at least three proficiency levels, but aim for five to seven levels". Verbatim. — [OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- OPM's published probes for a situational question show the full set of content an assessor wants covered (verbatim). Situation: "What do you consider the most critical issue in this situation?", "What other issues are of concern?" Action: "What would you say?", "What is the first thing you would do?", "What factors would affect your course of action?", "What other actions could you take?" Outcome: "How do you think your action would be received?", "What would you do if your action was not received well?", "What do you consider as benefits of your action?" — [OPM guide, Table 4](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf)
- Other OPM situational examples (verbatim): "A very angry client walks up to your desk. She says she was told your office sent her an overdue check five days ago. She claims she has not received the check. She says she has bills to pay, and no one will help her. How would you handle this situation?" and "You have been assigned to work on a project with some of your coworkers. While on the job, you notice several of them goofing off. You know you are falling behind schedule to complete the work by the deadline. What would you do?" — [OPM guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130218014229/http://apps.opm.gov/ADT/ContentFiles/SIGuide09.08.08.pdf); [OPM structured interviews overview](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/structured-interviews.pdf)
- The UK Civil Service also assesses behaviours hypothetically: "Alternatively, you may be asked how you would behave in a given situation." Verbatim. — [gov.uk, Civil Service behaviours](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-civil-service-behaviours)

**(c) Motivation and fit questions**
- DDI describes motivational fit as how well a job's responsibilities, the organisation's culture and operations, and the location match what fulfils and drives a candidate, with three components: job fit, organisation fit and location fit; each Success Profile should define these "objectively" so interviewers compare candidates consistently; DDI cites studies finding motivational fit a strong predictor of job satisfaction, absenteeism and turnover, and warns that "job fit" can become a loose label for bias (snippet; summarised). — [DDI blog, Motivational fit](https://www.ddiworld.com/blog/motivational-fit)
- OPM describes job-fit measures as comparing applicant preferences with job or organisational characteristics, citing Kristof (1996) for person-organisation fit (snippet). — [OPM, Job-Fit Measures](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/job-fit-measures)
- UK Civil Service Success Profiles has five elements, and motivation is not a separate one. Definitions (verbatim): Ability "the aptitude or potential to perform to the required standard"; Behaviours "the actions and activities that people do which result in effective performance in a job"; Strengths "the things we do regularly, do well and that motivate us"; Experience "the knowledge or mastery of an activity or subject gained through involvement in or exposure to it". Motivation sits inside the Strengths definition. — [gov.uk, Success Profiles: candidate overview](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-candidate-overview)
- Civil Service strengths are assessed differently from behaviours: three elements must apply (performance, engagement, use); there are "no right or wrong answers"; and "Do not rehearse your answers - because we are looking for your initial response." Verbatim for the quotes. — [gov.uk, Success Profiles: Strengths](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-strengths)
- Recruiters are told to hide which strengths are tested: "it is recommended that the specific strengths being assessed are not explicitly referenced in recruitment materials such as adverts, candidate information packs and interview invitation letters. A well written person specification will indicate requirements that relate to the chosen strengths without being so explicit that candidates prepare their responses prior to the interview stage." The assessment matrix shows Strengths assessed only at interview, while Behaviours can be assessed at sift, judgement test, interview and presentation. Strengths use a 4-point scale; experience, technical and behaviours use the 7-point scale. Verbatim from PDF. — [Cabinet Office guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5f746b1ae90e0740caae75a7/Guidance-Application_of_Success_Profile_Guides_during_delegated_grade__EO-Grade_6__recruitment_v0b.pdf)
- The Civil Service Strengths Dictionary lists 36 named strengths, among them Adaptable, Analytical, Authentic, Catalyst, Challenger, Courageous, Decisive, Disciplined, Explainer, Improver, Learner, Mission, Organiser, Precise, Preventer, Problem solver, Relationship builder, Resilient, Responsible, Service focused, Team player. Names verbatim, definitions paraphrased by the fetch tool (e.g. Preventer: anticipates and addresses risks before they occur; Mission: driven by meaning and a longer-term purpose). — [gov.uk, Success Profiles: Strengths](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-strengths)
- Cappfinity's definition as quoted by Warwick careers: "A strength is something we perform well at, is energising and we do often." A Cappfinity slide deck gives the three dimensions as energy (how energised you are by using the strength), performance (how well you perform) and use (how often you use it) (slide content from snippet). — [Warwick careers blog](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/careers/blog/how_to_answer_strengths_based_questions_in_interviews); [Capp slide deck, 2019](https://www.slideshare.net/firmnetwork/firmday-birmingham-19th-june-2019-capp-strengthsbased-assessment)
- Warwick's account of what strengths assessors read: language used, tone and pace, body language, rich specific examples, visible energy, authenticity; answer quality runs from generic answers with few specific actions (weaker), to several specific actions outlined (better), to several specific actions described in detail with clear energy and enthusiasm (best). Employers it names as strengths users include Barclays, HSBC, EY, Morgan Stanley and PwC. Paraphrased by the fetch tool. — [Warwick careers blog](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/careers/blog/how_to_answer_strengths_based_questions_in_interviews)

**(d) Open "tell me about yourself" / CV walk-through**
- The Civil Service definition of what an experience question collects (verbatim): assessors look for "previous examples of things you have achieved", "your knowledge in a particular field which is relevant to the job role", and "How you performed in a particular field - not how long you served". Experience is scored on the same 7-point evidence scale as behaviours. — [gov.uk, Success Profiles: Experience](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-experience); [Cabinet Office guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5f746b1ae90e0740caae75a7/Guidance-Application_of_Success_Profile_Guides_during_delegated_grade__EO-Grade_6__recruitment_v0b.pdf)
- A generic public-sector key that is used for open questions shows what the top band needs (verbatim): "5 Outstanding: Perfect answer. All points addressed. All points relevant. Good examples. Candidate displayed that (s)he did research for the position and the place of employment. Response is well thought-out and well presented." "3 Good: Some points covered. Relevant information given. Some examples given... Complete response and minimal probing is required to attain an answer." "1 Poor: A few good points but main issues missing. No examples/irrelevant examples given." "0 Unsatisfactory: No answer given or answer completely irrelevant. No examples given. Used inappropriate language. Vague/incomplete response." — [Oregon Employment Department, Interview Scoring Criteria (2023)](https://www.oregon.gov/employ/agency/documents/interview-scoring-2023-09.pdf)

### Inferences
- A decoder that follows from the published keys (inference, built from the findings above):

| Question form | Construct being collected | What counts as evidence | What moves the score up |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Tell me about a time..." | One named behaviour or competency | A single real incident: context, own actions, result | Difficulty of the situation handled and independence shown (OPM levels 1 to 5 are defined by difficulty and guidance needed, not by eloquence); "direct relevance" and "clear evidence" (USC level 5) |
| "What would you do if..." | Judgement or integrity through stated intention | The choice made in the dilemma and the reasoning | Doing the compliant thing is the middle band; the top band adds proactive steps that serve the organisation's objective (OPM "Superior") |
| "What motivates you / what do you enjoy / what are you good at" | Strengths: performance, energy, use | Spontaneous, specific examples plus visible enjoyment | Detail of specific actions and authentic energy (Warwick/Cappfinity); the Civil Service wants the "initial response" |
| "Why this firm / role / industry" | Motivational fit: organisation fit, job fit, sector commitment | Stated preferences that match a pre-written profile of the job and firm, backed by research | Specificity to this employer; in a generic key, "displayed that (s)he did research for the position and the place of employment" is a level-5 marker |
| "Tell me about yourself / walk me through your CV" | Experience and achievements, plus communication and motivation read holistically | Achievements and relevant knowledge, "not how long you served" | Selection and relevance; coverage of all points |

- Because OPM's behavioural levels are defined by how difficult the situation was and how much guidance was needed, choosing the example is a scoring decision. A tidy story about a low-stakes situation is capped near the middle regardless of delivery. This matches the Civil Service 6-7 band wording in the earlier notes ("exceeding expectations at this level").
- Latham's rule that questions where most people give the same answer are discarded implies that any scenario question still in use has an answer most candidates get only partly right. The "obvious" response is designed to be the 3, not the 5.
- The 2024 finding that fewer than half of candidates offer a maximal-performance example suggests assessors routinely score answers that under-represent the candidate; picking the strongest example that fits, not the first that comes to mind, is itself a differentiator.
- Strengths-style questions are the one type where designers actively try to defeat preparation (Civil Service hides the strengths and asks for the initial response). Firms that use Cappfinity-style strengths questions in a recorded format are likely looking for the same spontaneity and energy cues, which a human reviewer can see even though a transcript-only algorithm cannot.

## 2. What recruiters, graduate-recruitment heads and assessors say, in their own words, they are looking for in each question type

### Takeaway
Named bank recruiters describe the motivation family of questions as a test of three things: wanting this job as well as being able to do it, being able to say why this firm rather than another, and knowing the specific business area applied to. For strengths and weaknesses they name self-awareness and the line between confidence and arrogance; for commercial questions they name staying current and focus on the business area. On-the-record quotes from named finance recruiters are mostly from 2012-2013; recent firm statements are thinner and say what a stage "covers" rather than how it is marked.

### Cited Findings

Verbatim short quotes. — [Warwick careers blog](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/careers/blog/how_to_answer_tell_me_about_yourself_in_an_interview)

### Inferences
- Across named recruiters the motivation questions split into three separately checkable claims: (1) sector commitment ("keen", "long-term potential"), (2) firm differentiation ("versus another firm"), and (3) business-area knowledge ("the business area for which you have applied"). An answer to "why this firm" that only evidences (1) has answered a different question, which is the failure Ouseley names.
- Recruiters' language about weaknesses and strengths is about calibration (self-awareness, neither over- nor under-confident). The construct is the accuracy of the candidate's self-assessment and what they did about it, not the weakness chosen.
- Fidelity and SJP both stress authenticity in the recorded stage ("natural abilities", "authentic voices"), and Schroders says the video "is not about being polished" (earlier notes). For buy-side and wealth firms the recorded stage reads as a strengths-and-motivation screen more than a competency exam.
- Commercial-awareness questions are scored on linkage, not recall: the careers-service definitions all contain a "how X affects the employer" clause, so a news item with no stated effect on the firm or its clients is incomplete evidence.

### Gaps
- No Institute of Student Employers material on what specific question types assess surfaced in any search; ISE content appears to sit behind membership.
- No named UK bank or asset-manager recruiter quote from 2023-2026 on what "tell me about yourself", "why this firm" or a weakness question is scored on was found; the on-record recruiter quotes are 2012-2013 and partly from US or APAC heads of the same firms.
- LSE, Oxford, Cambridge, Imperial and UCL careers pages quoting employers on these questions were not reached; Leicester's motivation-questions page returned 403.
- JPMorgan's and Morgan Stanley's careers pages on interview preparation redirected or returned 503, so no fresh statement from either on what interviews assess is included.

## 3. Target firms' published frameworks, and what each says its video or early-careers interview assesses

### Takeaway
Every target firm publishes the headings its questions are most plausibly written against, and they cluster into five recurring families: client first, integrity or doing the right thing, excellence or performance, collaboration or partnership, and ownership or accountability, with "speaking up" written into several. Statements about what the video stage itself assesses are sparse: Lazard (motivation, cultural fit, interest in the firm and industry), L&G (motivational questions), Fidelity International (why the programme, skills, team fit), Schroders (strengths against role and values) and SJP (authentic voice) are the explicit ones found.

## 5. Scenario and integrity questions in UK financial services: the expected answer pattern

### Takeaway
The authoritative sources converge on one pattern: act honestly and at once, tell your manager and, where needed, Compliance, protect the client, do not conceal, and document. The FCA rules separate the mistake (a matter of due skill, care and diligence) from how it is handled afterwards (a matter of integrity), and firm codes make reporting an obligation rather than a choice. The one published three-level benchmark for an integrity scenario shows that simply doing the compliant thing is "Satisfactory"; "Superior" adds proactive steps that protect the organisation's wider objective.

### Cited Findings

**FCA Individual Conduct Rules (COCON 2.1), verbatim**
- "1. You must act with integrity." "2. You must act with due skill, care and diligence." "3. You must be open and cooperative with the FCA, the PRA and other regulators." "4. You must pay due regard to the interests of customers and treat them fairly." "5. You must observe proper standards of market conduct." "6. You must act to deliver good outcomes for retail customers." Rule 6 applies where the firm's activities are covered by the Consumer Duty. ### Inferences
- High versus low on "you sent confidential information to the wrong client" (inference from COCON, the JPM and Goldman codes, ICO guidance and the OPM key):
  - Low: tries to fix it quietly (recall and hope), delays telling anyone, contacts the recipient independently without telling the manager, or minimises it. Under COCON this turns a Rule 2 lapse into a Rule 1 problem, because the concealment, not the slip, is the integrity issue.
  - Middle (the "Satisfactory" analogue): tells the manager promptly and follows instructions.
  - High (the "Superior" analogue): states the priority (the client whose information was exposed), acts in sequence and at once: stop further spread (attempt recall), tell the line manager immediately, bring in Compliance or the data-protection function because it is a potential reportable breach, let the firm decide how the recipient and the affected client are contacted, record what happened, then say how the cause would be fixed (for example a recipient check before sending). It names the trade-off openly: personal embarrassment against client interest and the firm's reputation (Goldman Principles 1, 2, 12 and 14).
- High versus low on "a colleague is cutting corners": low answers either ignore it or go straight to a hotline without establishing facts; middle answers "report it to my manager"; high answers distinguish severity (a shortcut on formatting versus skipping a control or misleading a client), raise it with the colleague where that is safe and appropriate, escalate to the manager or Compliance where a policy, client or regulatory issue is involved, and note that firm codes make reporting an obligation ("you can also be held responsible for not reporting"). UBS's "courageously raising concerns" and Fidelity's "speaking up" are the value hooks.
- High versus low on "two urgent deadlines clash": this is a judgement and communication dilemma rather than an integrity one. Low answers pick one silently or promise both and work all night; middle answers "prioritise by importance". High answers gather the facts (true deadlines, consequences, who is waiting), tell both stakeholders early, let the seniors resolve the priority if the candidate cannot, propose a workable plan (sequence, partial deliverable, help), and then deliver and confirm. The Civil Service indicators "talk to relevant people to get advice and information when unsure how to proceed" and "explain how decisions have been reached" are the nearest published anchors; aberdeen's "balance pace with perfection" is the nearest firm wording.
- Across all three, the top band shares a shape that mirrors OPM's probes: name the critical issue, state the first action, give the reasoning and what would change it, and say what happens next if the first action does not work. In a one-way recorded interview there is no interviewer to ask those probes, so an answer that stops at "I would tell my manager" leaves the higher-band evidence unsaid.
- Because situational-question designers discard items that everyone answers the same way, a scenario question that looks to have one obvious answer is probably keyed on the secondary elements: order of actions, who is told, client focus, documentation and prevention.

## 6. Hybrid and multi-part questions: how assessors treat an answer that covers only part of the question

### Takeaway
Where a key exists, coverage is written into it: a generic 0 to 5 scale reserves the top score for "All points addressed", gives "Some points covered" a 3 and "main issues missing" a 1, and a published key for a three-part hybrid question scores a candidate who answers only the hypothetical part as "Marginal". Live interviewers can rescue a partial answer with probes; a one-way recorded interview has no probes, so an unaddressed part is simply missing evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Generic scoring criteria used by a US state agency (verbatim): 5 "Perfect answer. All points addressed. All points relevant."; 4 "All or most points covered."; 3 "Some points covered. Relevant information given... Complete response and minimal probing is required to attain an answer."; 2 "Some points covered, not all relevant."; 1 "A few good points but main issues missing."; 0 "No answer given or answer completely irrelevant... Vague/incomplete response." — [Oregon Employment Department, Interview Scoring Criteria (2023)](https://www.oregon.gov/employ/agency/documents/interview-scoring-2023-09.pdf)
- A published key for a hybrid, three-part question ("Describe a situation in which you had to perform under stress. What did you do, and why did you do these things? ### Inferences
- Parts of a multi-part question are rarely decoration: in the OPM and USC examples each part elicits a different piece of the evidence the key needs (situation, action, reasoning, result, or past example plus stated strategy). Skipping a part removes the evidence for one band of the scale. On the USC key, a fluent answer to only the "how would you" part lands at 1 of 5.
- In a hybrid question that pairs a past example with a forward-looking part ("...and what would you do differently?" or "...and how would you apply that here?"), the past example carries the higher bands; the forward-looking part alone is the "able to articulate reasonable strategies" level. Both must be present for the top band.
- In a live structured interview the missing part can be recovered by a probe ("minimal probing is required" is still a 3). In an asynchronous recording nothing recovers it, so the practical rule is to restate the parts in the opening line and close each one explicitly; a reviewer scoring "all points addressed" can then tick coverage quickly.
- Not every bullet of a published competency needs to appear (Civil Service), but every part of the question asked does (Goldman; Oregon key). Those are different kinds of completeness and candidates often confuse them: breadth across the competency is optional, coverage of the question is not.
- For two-in-one motivation questions ("Why this firm and why this division?"), the recruiter quotes in section 2 imply each half is a separate claim (firm differentiation; business-area knowledge), so an answer weighted wholly to one half is a partial answer in the Oregon sense.

### Gaps
- No source states a numerical rule for how much one unanswered part reduces a score, and no finance firm publishes how its reviewers treat partial answers.
- No research was found that compares scores for complete versus partial answers to multi-part questions, in live or asynchronous interviews.
- HireVue's own guidance on multi-part prompts was not found; the inference about asynchronous interviews rests on the absence of probes in that format.
