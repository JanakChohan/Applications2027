## 1. UK law: the duty to adjust recruitment, whether dyslexia counts, proof, and what to do if refused

### Takeaway
An employer must make reasonable adjustments to its selection process for a disabled applicant once it knows, or should know, about the disability and the disadvantage. Dyslexia is named in the statutory guidance as an impairment that can be a disability, the test is about effect and not about holding a diagnosis, and ACAS says in terms that a diagnosis is not needed and that proof is not legally required. An employer may lawfully ask health questions before an offer only for limited purposes, one of which is adjusting the assessment.

### Cited Findings

**The definition of disability**

- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) Section 6(1): "A person (P) has a disability if— (a) P has a physical or mental impairment, and (b) the impairment has a substantial and long-term adverse effect on P's ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities." — [Equality Act 2010 s.6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/6)
- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) "'substantial' means more than minor or trivial". — [Equality Act 2010 s.212](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/212)
- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) "The effect of an impairment is long-term if— (a) it has lasted for at least 12 months, (b) it is likely to last for at least 12 months, or (c) it is likely to last for the rest of the life of the person affected." — [Equality Act 2010 Sch.1 para 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/schedule/1/paragraph/2)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) The statutory guidance on the definition lists, among impairments from which a disability can arise: "developmental, such as autistic spectrum disorders (ASD), dyslexia and dyspraxia". First published May 2011; the HTML version says the content "has not been changed or updated from the original publication". — [Equality Act 2010 Guidance on the definition of disability, para A5](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/equality-act-guidance/disability-equality-act-2010-guidance-on-matters-to-be-taken-into-account-in-determining-questions-relating-to-the-definition-of-disability-html)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) Para A3: "It is not necessary for the cause of the impairment to be established, nor does the impairment have to be the result of an illness." — [same guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/equality-act-guidance/disability-equality-act-2010-guidance-on-matters-to-be-taken-into-account-in-determining-questions-relating-to-the-definition-of-disability-html)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) Para B2: "The time taken by a person with an impairment to carry out a normal day-to-day activity should be considered when assessing whether the effect of that impairment is substantial. It should be compared with the time it might take a person who did not have the impairment to complete an activity." — [same guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/equality-act-guidance/disability-equality-act-2010-guidance-on-matters-to-be-taken-into-account-in-determining-questions-relating-to-the-definition-of-disability-html)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) Para B10: "In some cases, people have coping or avoidance strategies which cease to work in certain circumstances (for example, where someone who has dyslexia is placed under stress). If it is possible that a person's ability to manage the effects of an impairment will break down so that effects will sometimes still occur, this possibility must be taken into account when assessing the effects of the impairment." — [same guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/equality-act-guidance/disability-equality-act-2010-guidance-on-matters-to-be-taken-into-account-in-determining-questions-relating-to-the-definition-of-disability-html)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) Para D3 lists "reading and writing" as day-to-day activities and says they "can include general work-related activities, and study and education-related activities, such as ... carrying out interviews, preparing written documents, and keeping to a timetable". The Appendix's illustrative list of factors it "would be reasonable to regard as having a substantial adverse effect" includes "persistent and significant difficulty in reading or understanding written material where this is in the person's native written language". — [same guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/equality-act-guidance/disability-equality-act-2010-guidance-on-matters-to-be-taken-into-account-in-determining-questions-relating-to-the-definition-of-disability-html)
- `[THIRD-PARTY]` (search summary of case reports) *Paterson v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis* [2007] IRLR 763 (EAT): a senior police officer found to be dyslexic in 2004 was held to be disabled. The tribunal had found only a "minor or trivial impact"; the EAT disagreed and held that taking assessments or examinations, and reading and comprehension, are normal day-to-day activities. Decided under the Disability Discrimination Act 1995, so persuasive on the similar 2010 Act wording. — [Personnel Today](https://www.personneltoday.com/hr/dyslexia-and-the-disability-discrimination-act-paterson-v-the-commissionr-of-police-of-the-metropolis); [Thompsons](https://www.thompsonstradeunion.law/news/employment-law-review/weekly-issue-39/adverse-effects)

**The duty to adjust the selection process**

- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) Section 39(1): "An employer (A) must not discriminate against a person (B)— (a) in the arrangements A makes for deciding to whom to offer employment". Section 39(5): "A duty to make reasonable adjustments applies to an employer." — [Equality Act 2010 s.39](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/39)
- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) Section 20(3): "The first requirement is a requirement, where a provision, criterion or practice of A's puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage in relation to a relevant matter in comparison with persons who are not disabled, to take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to avoid the disadvantage." — [Equality Act 2010 s.20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/20)
- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) Section 20(6): "Where the first or third requirement relates to the provision of information, the steps which it is reasonable for A to have to take include steps for ensuring that in the circumstances concerned the information is provided in an accessible format." Section 20(7): the employer is not "entitled to require a disabled person ... to pay to any extent A's costs of complying with the duty." — [Equality Act 2010 s.20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/20)
- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) Knowledge: "A is not subject to a duty to make reasonable adjustments if A does not know, and could not reasonably be expected to know— (a) in the case of an applicant or potential applicant, that an interested disabled person is or may be an applicant for the work in question; (b) ... that an interested disabled person has a disability and is likely to be placed at the disadvantage". — [Equality Act 2010 Sch.8 para 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/schedule/8/paragraph/20)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) EHRC Code para 6.20: "The Act does not prevent a disabled person keeping a disability confidential from an employer. But keeping a disability confidential is likely to mean that unless the employer could reasonably be expected to know about it anyway, the employer will not be under a duty to make a reasonable adjustment. If a disabled person expects an employer to make a reasonable adjustment, they will need to provide the employer – or someone acting on their behalf – with sufficient information to carry out that adjustment." — [EHRC Employment Statutory Code of Practice](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/sites/default/files/employercode.pdf)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) EHRC Code para 16.49: "If an employer fails to ask about reasonable adjustments needed for the recruitment process, but could reasonably have been expected to know that a particular disabled applicant or possible applicant is likely to be disadvantaged compared to non-disabled people, they will still be under a duty to make a reasonable adjustment at the interview." — [EHRC Code](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/sites/default/files/employercode.pdf)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) EHRC Code para 16.54: "Employers should make adjustments where a test or assessment would put a disabled applicant at a substantial disadvantage, if such adjustments would be reasonable ... Examples of adjustments which may be reasonable include: providing written instructions in an accessible format; allowing a disabled person extra time to complete the test; permitting a disabled person the assistance of a reader or scribe during the test; allowing a disabled applicant to take an oral test in writing or a written test orally." — [EHRC Code](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/sites/default/files/employercode.pdf)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) EHRC Code para 6.28 lists reasonableness factors: "whether taking any particular steps would be effective in preventing the substantial disadvantage; the practicability of the step; the financial and other costs ...; the extent of the employer's financial or other resources; ... the type and size of the employer." Para 6.29: the test "is an objective one and will depend on the circumstances of the case." — [EHRC Code](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/sites/default/files/employercode.pdf)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) gov.uk lists "changing the recruitment process so a candidate can be considered for a job" as a reasonable adjustment. — [gov.uk Reasonable adjustments for workers with disabilities or health conditions](https://www.gov.uk/reasonable-adjustments-for-disabled-workers)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) ACAS: "Job applicants can ask for reasonable adjustments for any part of the recruitment process." "Nobody has to tell their employer – or potential employer – they're disabled." Requests can be made "in writing – for example in a letter or email". "An employer does not have to make adjustments that are unreasonable." — [ACAS Asking for reasonable adjustments](https://www.acas.org.uk/reasonable-adjustments/asking-for-reasonable-adjustments)

**Diagnosis and proof**

- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) ACAS: "A worker does not need a diagnosis to be considered disabled under the Equality Act 2010. An employer should offer workers support whether or not they have a diagnosis for their neurodivergence. This includes making reasonable adjustments." — [ACAS Adjustments for neurodiversity](https://www.acas.org.uk/reasonable-adjustments/adjustments-for-neurodiversity)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) ACAS: "An employer might ask a worker for proof that they have a condition. For example, an NHS letter confirming a diagnosis. However, a worker: is not legally required to provide this; might not have any proof they can give; might have their own reasons for not sharing a diagnosis". — [ACAS Adjustments for neurodiversity](https://www.acas.org.uk/reasonable-adjustments/adjustments-for-neurodiversity)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) ACAS tells employers, when advertising a job, to "make it clear applicants can ask for reasonable adjustments and they do not need a diagnosis" and "give examples of reasonable adjustments"; and for interviews, "where possible, allow applicants to see the questions before the interview". — [ACAS Making your organisation neuroinclusive](https://www.acas.org.uk/neurodiversity-at-work/making-your-organisation-neuroinclusive)
- `[THIRD-PARTY]` (search summary) Law-firm commentary: a tribunal does not require a diagnosis, but a claimant "will need to adduce some form of evidence to satisfy the tribunal that they meet the definition". — [Irwin Mitchell](https://www.irwinmitchell.com/news-and-insights/expert-comment/post/102kbjg/do-you-need-to-wait-for-a-diagnosis-before-making-reasonable-adjustments-for-a-ne)

**Pre-offer health questions (section 60)**

- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) Section 60(1): "A person (A) to whom an application for work is made must not ask about the health of the applicant (B)— (a) before offering work to B, or (b) ... before including B in a pool of applicants". — [Equality Act 2010 s.60](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/60)
- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) Section 60(6): "This section does not apply to a question that A asks in so far as asking the question is necessary for the purpose of— (a) establishing whether B will be able to comply with a requirement to undergo an assessment or establishing whether a duty to make reasonable adjustments is or will be imposed on A in relation to B in connection with a requirement to undergo an assessment". — [Equality Act 2010 s.60](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/60)
- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) Section 60(2): a breach of s.60(1) "is enforceable as an unlawful act under Part 1 of the Equality Act 2006 (and ... is enforceable only by the Commission under that Part)." Section 60(3): "A does not contravene a relevant disability provision merely by asking about B's health; but A's conduct in reliance on information given in response may be a contravention". Section 60(5) shifts the burden of proof where an applicant claims the employer acted on the answer. — [Equality Act 2010 s.60](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/60)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) EHRC Code para 10.29: "It is lawful for an employer to ask questions relating to reasonable adjustments that would be needed for an assessment such as an interview or other process designed to assess a person's suitability for a job. This means in practice that any information on disability or health obtained by an employer for the purpose of making adjustments to recruitment arrangements should, as far as possible, be held separately. Also it should not form any part of the decision-making process about an offer of employment, whether or not conditional." — [EHRC Code](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/sites/default/files/employercode.pdf)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) EHRC Code para 10.30 example: "An application form states: 'Please contact us if you are disabled and need any adjustments for the interview'. This would be lawful under the Act." — [EHRC Code](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/sites/default/files/employercode.pdf)

**Recruitment case law on adjustments**

- `[THIRD-PARTY]` (search summary; judgment linked) *AECOM Ltd v Mallon* [2023] EAT 104: an applicant with dyspraxia was put at a substantial disadvantage by a requirement to create an online profile and complete an online form; the employer had constructive knowledge and should have made adjustments. — [National Archives judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/eat/2023/104); [RPC commentary](https://www.rpclegal.com/thinking/employment/adjusting-your-recruitment-process-for-a-candidate-with-a-disability/)
- `[THIRD-PARTY]` (search summary; judgment linked) *Government Legal Service v Brookes* UKEAT/0302/16: an applicant with Asperger's asked for a different format for a multiple-choice situational judgement test and was told none was available; she succeeded and was awarded £860. Sources differ on how the finding is characterised (indirect discrimination, with the adjustments claim succeeding on similar reasoning). — [EAT judgment](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/59f73e7be5274a3485f7ce7d/The_Government_Legal_Service_v_Ms_T_Brookes_UKEAT_0302_16_RN.pdf); [Personnel Today](https://www.personneltoday.com/hr/recruitment-multiple-choice-psychometric-test-discriminatory-holds-eat)
- `[THIRD-PARTY]` (fetched commentary, dated November 2026) *Bryce v Coventry City Council* [2026] EAT 153: a candidate with Asperger's and dyslexia "requested adjustments to the recruitment process, including extra time to provide examples of how he met the job criteria. The Council made some adjustments: it provided the questions 24 minutes before the interview and the interviewers agreed at the outset to remind Mr Bryce of different parts of a question and rephrase any he misunderstood". The EAT upheld the finding that the Council had made reasonable adjustments and that asking only yes/no questions was not a reasonable adjustment; it also held the tribunal erred in relying on an interviewer's neurotypical assumptions about whether he was stressed. — [DAC Beachcroft](https://www.dacbeachcroft.com/en/What-we-think/Assumptions-about-how-neurodiverse-candidate-handled-stress-undermined-discrimination-defence); [EAT judgment](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ac8b817ab368efeb4c9342c/Mr_R_Bryce_v_Coventry_City_Council__2026__EAT_153.pdf)

### Inferences
- A candidate without a diagnosis can still ask. The legal test turns on effect (a substantial, long-term adverse effect on activities such as reading), and ACAS says neither a diagnosis nor proof is legally required. In practice some firms ask for documents (see section 3: Lazard asks for "supporting documentation"; Deloitte and EY say none is needed), so the practical position varies by firm even though the legal position does not.
- The employer's duty only bites once it knows. A candidate who says nothing and fails a 30-second reading window has no adjustment claim on that attempt; the request has to be made before the assessment is taken.
- A candidate who asks for an adjustment is not volunteering a "health question" answer that the firm may use in selection: the Code says such information should be held separately and form no part of the decision.
- Tribunals look at whether what the employer did was reasonable, not whether the candidate got their preferred adjustment (*Bryce*). A specific, proportionate request (more preparation time, the question in a readable format) is more likely to be treated as reasonable than a request to change what is being assessed.
- Time limits doubled for acts on or after 1 October 2026, which gives more room to try to resolve a refusal informally first.

### Gaps
- No tribunal decision was found that deals with a one-way (asynchronous) video interview and dyslexia specifically.
- The full text of *Paterson* was not read; it is reported here from secondary summaries.
- Whether a single failed attempt taken without requesting an adjustment can be re-opened is a matter of firm discretion; no source addressed it.

## 2. HireVue's own accommodation features

### Takeaway
HireVue's platform has one built-in accommodation, a candidate-side "request additional time" link on the first page of the interview, which the employer can switch off; everything else (longer preparation time, untimed answers, more retakes, questions in advance, a live or written alternative) is a setting or decision that belongs to the employer. HireVue tells candidates to make any other request to the hiring company, and to do it before starting.

### Inferences
- The preparation window is not fixed by HireVue. A 30-second window is the employer's choice of Think Time, and BlackRock's published three minutes shows how widely it varies. That means "more preparation time" is technically a one-field change for the employer.
- The in-platform link must be clicked on the first page, before any question is seen. Once the interview has begun it cannot help.
- HireVue's help text says the in-platform request gives "more time during timed questions" but does not say how much, or whether it lengthens preparation time, answer time or both. A candidate whose difficulty is reading the question in the preparation window should not assume the button fixes that; a direct request to the firm for extended preparation time is the surer route.
- On current documentation the question text stays beside the video during preparation, and nothing says it is removed when recording starts. Earlier project notes recorded this as undocumented per firm, and that remains true: no source confirms it for each bank's configuration.
- Features the brief asked about that HireVue does not document as candidate-side options: larger text (no setting found; browser zoom is the only route), replaying a text question (not applicable; audio/video questions have a Play control), and native closed captions (HireVue says it has not implemented these).
- Where an assessment includes games, requesting the accommodation removes the games and the automatic score. No source says this helps or harms the outcome; it does mean a human route.

### Gaps
- The amount of extra time the in-platform accommodation grants is not published.
- No HireVue page says whether a candidate's accommodation request is flagged to the human evaluator who watches the video.
- The current HireVue accessibility statement on hirevue.com could not be fetched (Cloudflare block); the version read is dated September 2022 and may be out of date, particularly on captions.
- No documentation of whether question text can be enlarged inside the HireVue interface.

- `[FIRM-OFFICIAL]` (same page) "The KPMG Student Recruitment team works in collaboration with MyPlus." — [same page](https://kpmgcareers.co.uk/graduate/applying-to-kpmg/need-support-let-us-know)

### Inferences
- The US-headquartered banks' published wording is generic and uses "accommodation"; a UK candidate writing to those inboxes will need to spell out the adjustment wanted, because the pages give no menu to choose from.
- Three firms publish wording that maps directly onto the stated difficulty (reading the question in a short preparation window): Deloitte ("Extended response and preparation time"), Legal & General ("Interview questions may be shared in advance where appropriate") and UBS (25% or 50% for the pre-recorded video interview). These are useful precedents to cite when asking other firms, since they show peers treat the request as ordinary.
- Deloitte's rule that requests do not carry forward between stages, and Cambridge's warning (section 4) that the person sending the test invitation may not know about a disclosure on the form, both point to re-stating the request at each stage.
- Several firms' video stage is not HireVue (PwC uses SHL; M&G's video questions sit in Cappfinity). The adjustment mechanics in section 2 apply only where the platform is HireVue.
- Lazard's "supporting documentation" wording, and the reference to extra time "at work/school/university in the past", suggests a university support plan or exam-arrangement letter is the kind of document it has in mind; the page does not define it.

### Gaps
- No firm page found says what share of adjustment requests are granted, how long a reply takes, or what a "25% extra time" adjustment does to a 30-second preparation window in practice.
- PwC and Rothschild wording is from search summaries only. Citi's wording is from a search summary. Goldman Sachs has no UK early-careers-specific wording.
- No Morgan Stanley, Citi, Bank of America or Goldman Sachs page names neurodiversity in the context of recruitment adjustments.
- None of the pages read says whether the human reviewer of a recorded video can see that extra time was granted, except Deloitte's statement that the candidate chooses what the assessor is told.

## 4. What is typically granted for dyslexia, and how much extra time

### Takeaway
The default offer across employers and test publishers is 25% extra time; 50% is requested and sometimes granted; untimed is rarer but exists. For video interviews the adjustments documented are extra preparation and answer time, questions or question format in advance, captions, a live or telephone interview instead, and display changes. University guidance stresses that the candidate usually has to propose the adjustment, because employers are familiar with extra time and little else.

### Inferences
- A percentage uplift helps little when the base is short. On a 30-second preparation window, 25% extra is about 8 seconds and 50% is 15 seconds. For someone whose difficulty is decoding the question at all in that window, the adjustments that address the actual barrier are a longer fixed preparation time (BlackRock's standard three minutes shows this is unremarkable), the question text supplied in advance or shortly before, or a live interview where the question is spoken.
- Because employers default to "25% extra time", a candidate who needs something different should name it. The Cambridge and LSE guidance both say to arrive with a specific proposal.
- Requests framed around the barrier ("I read on-screen text slowly and need longer to take in the question before answering") rather than a label fit what L&G, Deutsche Bank and Deloitte say they want to hear, and avoid the need to state a diagnosis.

### Gaps
- No source gives a typical preparation-time adjustment for one-way video interviews in seconds or minutes.
- Aon and Cappfinity publish no candidate-facing adjustment policy that was found; their clients (Schroders, M&G) route requests themselves.
- Nothing found from MyPlus Students' Club, the Business Disability Forum or 10,000 Able Interns that specifies video-interview adjustments. MyPlus material seen (via Bright Network) is general: give notice early, say only what is relevant, and use its "Openness Statement" template. — [Bright Network / MyPlus](https://brightnetwork.co.uk/equal-opportunity/myplus-students-club/what-are-reasonable-adjustments-recruitment)

## 5. Disclosure: does telling the firm affect the outcome?

### Takeaway
No data was found, from any firm, vendor or charity, comparing outcomes for candidates with and without adjustments. What exists is the legal rule that adjustment information should be held separately from the decision, explicit promises from a handful of firms, and survey evidence that most disabled candidates fear disclosure. The honest position is that the effect on outcomes is unmeasured in public sources.

### Cited Findings
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) EHRC Code para 10.29: information obtained for recruitment adjustments "should, as far as possible, be held separately. Also it should not form any part of the decision-making process about an offer of employment". Para 16.39: it is "good practice for this information to be withheld from the people who are short-listing or interviewing". — [EHRC Code](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/sites/default/files/employercode.pdf)
- `[STATUTE]` (fetched) If an applicant claims the employer acted on health information, s.60(5) treats the particulars of the complaint as facts from which a tribunal could find discrimination, shifting the burden to the employer. — [Equality Act 2010 s.60](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/60)
- `[FIRM-OFFICIAL]` (fetched) Deutsche Bank: "Requesting adjustments will never negatively impact your application, and only the relevant team will see your information." — [Deutsche Bank](https://careers.db.com/professionals/hiring-experience)
- `[FIRM-OFFICIAL]` (fetched) Deloitte: information "will not, under any circumstances, negatively impact your application", and "Before your assessment, we will ask what information you are comfortable sharing with the assessor. You may choose to share only the adjustment being provided." — [Deloitte early careers FAQ](https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/careers/early-careers/early-careers-faq.html)
- `[FIRM-OFFICIAL]` (fetched) L&G: "Requesting support or adjustments won't affect your application." — [L&G](https://careers.legalandgeneral.com/reasonable-adjustments)
- `[FIRM-OFFICIAL]` (fetched) KPMG: information shared in the Inclusion and Diversity section "will remain anonymous"; adjustments are requested separately "before each stage". — [KPMG](https://kpmgcareers.co.uk/graduate/applying-to-kpmg/need-support-let-us-know)
- `[FIRM-OFFICIAL]` (fetched via summarising tool) UBS tells candidates not to put health data in the request at all. — [UBS](https://www.ubs.com/global/en/careers/search-jobs/support/request-due-to-adjustment-and-accommodation.html)
- `[VENDOR-OFFICIAL]` (fetched) SHL to employers: "ClearSurvey 2011 confirmed that over 74% of candidates who completed the research stated they would not disclose their disability for fear of not getting the job." This is an overall employment gap, not a measure of the effect of disclosure. — [HEPI](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/2026/05/31/were-collecting-graduate-voice-data-but-are-we-really-listening/)
- `[THIRD-PARTY]` (fetched) A student-journalism feature on HireVue quotes an autistic applicant: "I'm scared that disclosing my disability will mean that I won't be given the job ... But if I don't click on it then I won't be given extra time to understand what the questions are asking me." - `[UNIVERSITY]` (fetched) Cambridge: "Some people choose to wait until they have been offered the job / started the job to mention their disability. However, adjustments won't be available for tests if you haven't made the employer aware beforehand." — [Cambridge guide](https://www.careers.cam.ac.uk/files/psychometric_tests_-_a_guide_for_disabled_and_neurodivergent_applicants_june_2024.pdf)
- `[THIRD-PARTY]` (search summary of one anonymous Glassdoor review; low weight, not read directly) One Barclays applicant described a poor experience with an adjustments request for an online assessment, including a long wait for confirmation. — [Glassdoor](https://www.glassdoor.ca/Interview/Barclays-Interview-E3456-RVW103157036.htm)
- `[VENDOR-OFFICIAL]` (fetched) On HireVue, requesting the in-platform accommodation on an assessment with games means "the interview is not scored" by the algorithm. — [HireVue Score Error Legend](https://hvclassichelp.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/43169163879828-HireVue-Assessment-Score-Error-Legend)

### Inferences
- A candidate can ask for an adjustment without naming a condition at several firms (Deutsche Bank, Deloitte, L&G, UBS). That reduces the disclosure question to "I need X for this stage", which is the lowest-exposure way to ask.
- The choice is asymmetric for a candidate who has already failed repeatedly without adjustments: the downside of asking is an unmeasured risk that the law prohibits and several firms disclaim, while the downside of not asking is a known barrier.
- The absence of outcome data is itself notable. Bath's guide advises employers to track "the success rates of candidates using adjustments", which implies most do not publish it.

### Gaps
- No published data on pass rates with and without adjustments, at any firm or on HireVue.
- No first-person account from a dyslexic UK applicant describing what a bank granted for a HireVue was found. Forums that would hold such accounts (Reddit, The Student Room, Wall Street Oasis, Glassdoor) were not fetched, as instructed.
- No source states how long firms take to answer adjustment requests, other than UBS ("allow a few days") and HireVue's accessibility inbox (5 business days).

## 6. Undiagnosed adults and students: screening, assessment, and what firms accept

### Takeaway
Only a full diagnostic assessment can identify dyslexia; checklists and screeners indicate likelihood only. A current student's cheapest route is the university disability service, which commonly screens for free and may pay for the assessment. A private adult assessment costs roughly £300 to £900. Several firms say they need no diagnosis or medical evidence for recruitment adjustments, so a diagnosis is not a precondition for asking.

- A screening report is not a diagnosis and no firm page says it accepts one as evidence. But most firms that state a position say they do not require evidence at all, so the screening report's practical use is to help the candidate describe what they need, not to prove anything.
- An undiagnosed candidate can truthfully describe the functional difficulty ("I read on-screen text slowly under time pressure and need longer to take in the question") without claiming a condition. That is consistent with ACAS's position and with the firms that ask only what support is needed. It would be wrong to claim a diagnosis that does not exist.
- A candidate who had extra time or other access arrangements at school or university already holds the kind of "supporting documentation" Lazard describes.

## 7. Strategies for taking in a question under time pressure, and the evidence

### Takeaway
The evidence base for self-help reading strategies in a 30-second window is thin. The best-supported points are indirect: adult dyslexia affects word reading and decoding far more than comprehension; text-to-speech gives a small average comprehension benefit; larger text and wider spacing help some readers; coloured overlays lack reliable evidence. Verbal working memory and letter-cued word retrieval tend to be weaker in adults with dyslexia, which supports using a fixed answer framework and written notes to reduce memory load. No study was found that tests any of these in a timed video interview.

### Cited Findings

**How reading difficulty shows up in adults**

- `[ACADEMIC]` (search summary of abstract) Reis et al. (2020), a meta-analysis of 178 studies comparing adults with dyslexia to matched controls: "poor performance on almost all reading and writing tasks expressed by very large effect sizes (range 1.735 ≤ d ≤ 2.034), except for reading comprehension (d = 0.729)." — [Reis et al. 2020, PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/32918699/)
- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) Statutory guidance B10: coping strategies can "cease to work in certain circumstances (for example, where someone who has dyslexia is placed under stress)". — [Equality Act guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/equality-act-guidance/disability-equality-act-2010-guidance-on-matters-to-be-taken-into-account-in-determining-questions-relating-to-the-definition-of-disability-html)

**Enlarging text and zooming**

- `[ACADEMIC]` (search summary; small sample, title and venue not verified by the search tool) A Rello and Baeza-Yates eye-tracking study of 28 participants with dyslexia compared font sizes from 10 to 26 points and found "font size has a significant effect on the readability and the understandability of the text, while line spacing does not", recommending 18-point for web text. **Coloured overlays and tinted screens**

- `[ACADEMIC]` (search summary) Griffiths et al. (2016) systematically reviewed coloured overlays and lenses using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. Related reviews: Albon et al. (2008) found "all studies had serious limitations"; Galuschka et al. (2014) could not show a positive effect; Suttle et al. **Verbal working memory and oral fluency**

- `[ACADEMIC]` (search summary) Smith-Spark and Fisk (2007, *Memory*) tested 22 adult students with dyslexia against 22 age- and IQ-matched controls on verbal and visuospatial working memory. A 2003 study by the same group found "a significant impairment on the dynamic task under high memory updating load". A 2016 follow-up reported adults with dyslexia "were less able to update working memory", on a non-phonological task as well as a phonological one. — [Smith-Spark et al., LSBU Open Research](https://openresearch.lsbu.ac.uk/item/87v11); [Executive functions in adults with dyslexia, City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/13712/3/Executive%20functions%20in%20adults%20with%20dyslexia.pdf)
- `[ACADEMIC]` (search summary) Smith-Spark, Henry, Messer and Zięcik (2017, *Dyslexia*): in adults, "dyslexia predicted lower phonemic fluency, but not semantic or design fluency". — [Verbal and non-verbal fluency in adults with developmental dyslexia, City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/17329/3/Verbal%20and%20non-verbal%20fluency%20in%20adults%20with%20developmental%20dyslexia.pdf)
- `[ACADEMIC]` (search summary) A later review summarising Reis et al.: adults with dyslexia "showed lower performances in several cognitive measures (phonological awareness, working memory, RAN ...), although the most severe DD symptoms emerged for reading and writing skills." — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12238081)

**Chunking, notes and structure**

- `[REGULATOR]` (fetched) ACAS lists "breaking down instructions into clear steps" and "talking through written information" as adjustments for written communication. — [ACAS](https://www.acas.org.uk/reasonable-adjustments/adjustments-for-neurodiversity)
- `[FIRM-OFFICIAL]` (fetched) Deloitte lists "rephrased questions, and the option to take notes to support memory" among in-person adjustments. — [Deloitte early careers FAQ](https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/careers/early-careers/early-careers-faq.html)
- `[CHARITY]` (search summary) Dyslexia Scotland advises employers to ask multi-part questions one part at a time. — [Dyslexia Scotland: hiring and supporting staff](https://dyslexiascotland.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/hiring-and-supporting-staff.pdf)
- `[THIRD-PARTY]` (fetched) In *Bryce*, the adjustments the employer made included agreeing "to remind Mr Bryce of different parts of a question and rephrase any he misunderstood". — [DAC Beachcroft](https://www.dacbeachcroft.com/en/What-we-think/Assumptions-about-how-neurodiverse-candidate-handled-stress-undermined-discrimination-defence)
- `[VENDOR-OFFICIAL]` (fetched) HireVue practice questions are unlimited and private, and the candidate can run the system check, close the browser and return later. — [HireVue: Getting Started](https://hirevuesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028433751-Getting-Started-with-Your-On-Demand-Interview-Computer); [HireVue: Preparing for your Interview](https://hirevuesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028139732-Preparing-for-your-Interview)

### Inferences
- The adult meta-analysis points to decoding, not understanding, as the main bottleneck. Anything that removes decoding from the timed window (hearing the question, having seen it beforehand, or having more time) addresses the cause; tricks that speed up decoding by a few seconds address it only marginally.
- Browser zoom is the one display change that is certainly within the candidate's control and involves no outside tool. Setting it during the practice questions, where the layout is the same, is low risk. Whether HireVue's layout stays usable at high zoom is untested here.
- Text-to-speech is a recognised assistive technology and several firms list it, but in a recorded interview a synthetic voice reading the question aloud would be audible on the recording only if it ran after recording began, and a browser extension running alongside the interview could be mistaken for a prohibited tool. The safe route is to tell the firm in advance and get written agreement, which also turns it into an agreed adjustment.
- Weaker working-memory updating and letter-cued retrieval suggest that holding a multi-part question in mind while composing an answer is the likely second pressure point after reading. That supports three habits that involve no outside help: jotting the two or three key words of the question on paper during preparation, answering to a fixed structure rehearsed in advance so that structure need not be held in memory, and glancing back at the on-screen question during the answer.
- Reading the question aloud during the preparation window is permitted on HireVue (the page says "Not Recording" during preparation), costs nothing, and converts a visual task into an auditory one. No study was found that tests it, so it is offered as a reasonable thing to try in practice mode, not as an evidence-based method.
- Coloured overlays or a tinted screen are harmless to try and some firms offer coloured backgrounds, but the research does not support expecting a benefit.

### Gaps
- No study was found on reading aloud or sub-vocalising as a comprehension aid for adults with dyslexia under time pressure.
- No study was found on finger or cursor tracking for adult readers.
- No study was found on whether answer frameworks (such as STAR) specifically help speakers with dyslexia; the support above is by inference from working-memory findings.
- Working-memory effect sizes for adults were not retrieved; the Reis et al. paper was seen in abstract only.
- No firm page says in terms whether a candidate may run text-to-speech during a HireVue without prior agreement.

## 8. Dyslexia-friendly document formatting

### Takeaway
The British Dyslexia Association's Dyslexia Style Guide (2023) is the standard UK reference: sans serif font at 12 to 14 point, wider letter and word spacing, 1.5 line spacing, bold for emphasis, no italics, underlining or capitals, left-aligned text, a cream or pastel background, headings at least 20% larger, short sentences and bullet points. The research evidence supports spacing and size more than any special font: OpenDyslexic and Dyslexie show no reliable benefit once spacing is matched.

### Cited Findings

**BDA Dyslexia Style Guide 2023** `[CHARITY]` — text below was read from a mirror of the BDA web page hosted by Boston University, which carries the BDA's own URL in its header and the line "The Dyslexia Style Guide 2023 is available as: Dyslexia Style Guide 2023 (PDF)". The BDA's own page and PDF returned "not found" or "access denied" to direct fetch on the research date. — [Mirror (docx)](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx); original address: [BDA Dyslexia friendly style guide](https://www.bdadyslexia.org.uk/advice/employers/creating-a-dyslexia-friendly-workplace/dyslexia-friendly-style-guide)

*Readable fonts*
- "Use sans serif fonts, such as Arial and Comic Sans, as letters can appear less crowded. Alternatives include Verdana, Tahoma, Century Gothic, Trebuchet, Calibri, Open Sans."
- "Font size should be 12-14 point or equivalent (e.g. 1-1.2em / 16-19 px). Some dyslexic readers may request a larger font."
- "Larger inter-letter / character spacing (sometimes called tracking) improves readability, ideally around 35% of the average letter width. If letter spacing is excessive it can reduce readability."
- "Inter-word spacing should be at least 3.5 times the inter-letter spacing."
- "Some dyslexic people find that larger line spacing improves readability. It should be proportional to inter-word spacing; 1.5/150% is preferable."
- "Avoid underlining and italics as this can make the text appear to run together and cause crowding. Use bold for emphasis."
- "Avoid using all capital letters and uppercase letters for continuous text. Lower case letters are easier to read."

*Headings and structure*
- "Use headings and styles to create consistent structure to help people navigate through your content."
- "For headings, use a font size that is at least 20% larger than the normal text. If further emphasis is required, then use bold."
- "Add extra space around headings and between paragraphs."
- "Ensure hyperlinks look different from headings and normal text."

*Colour*
- "Use single colour backgrounds. Avoid background patterns or pictures and distracting surrounds."
- "Use sufficient contrast levels between background and text."
- "Use dark coloured text on a light (not white) background."
- "Avoid green and red/pink, as these colours are difficult for those who have colour vision deficiencies (colour blindness)."
- "Consider alternatives to white backgrounds for paper, computer and visual aids such as whiteboards. White can appear too dazzling. Use cream or a soft pastel colour. Some dyslexic people will have their own colour preference."
- "When printing, use matt paper rather than gloss. Paper should be thick enough to prevent the other side showing through."

*Layout*
- "Left align text, without justification. This makes it easier to find the start and finish of each line and ensures even spacing between words."
- "Avoid multiple columns (as used in newspapers)."
- "Write short simple sentences: 60 to 70 characters is optimal."
- "Use white space to remove clutter near text and group related content."
- "Break up the text with regular section headings in long documents and include a table of contents."

*Writing style*
- "Use active rather than passive voice."
- "Be concise; avoid using long, dense paragraphs."
- "Write in simple clear language using every day words."
- "Use images to support text. Flow charts are ideal for explaining procedures. Pictograms and graphics can help to locate and support information in the text."
- "Consider using bullet points and numbering rather than continuous prose."
- "Give instructions clearly." "Avoid double negatives." "Avoid abbreviations where possible; always provide the expanded form when first used." "Provide a glossary of abbreviations and jargon."

**Evidence on specialist fonts and spacing** `[ACADEMIC]` (all from search summaries of abstracts)
- OpenDyslexic: Wery and Diliberto (2017), single-subject alternating-treatment design with primary-school pupils: "no improvement in reading rate or accuracy for individual students with dyslexia, as well as the group as a whole." — [Wery & Diliberto 2017, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5629233/)
- Dyslexie: Marinus et al. (2016) found Dyslexie beat standard Arial, but "when within-word spacing and between-word spacing of Arial font was matched to that of Dyslexie font, the difference in reading speed was no longer significant." Kuster et al. found the "Dyslexie font does not benefit reading in children with or without dyslexia" (170 children with dyslexia in one study). — [Marinus et al. 2016, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27194598/); [University of Michigan summary of Kuster et al.](https://dyslexiahelp.umich.edu/latest/does-dyslexie-font-help-dyslexic-readers/)
- Spacing: Zorzi et al. (2012) reported that extra-large letter spacing helped reading. Replications are mixed: Hakvoort et al. (2017) found it "decreases the number of errors significantly, but does not increase reading speed in children with dyslexia"; Galliussi et al. - Font size: see section 7 (Rello and Baeza-Yates; 18-point recommendation for web text; small sample). — [Rello & Baeza-Yates](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-W4A-DysWebxia%20More%20Accessible%20Text.pdf)
- Lexend: designed by Bonnie Shaver-Troup and Thomas Jockin "with the goal of improving reading fluency". The supporting evidence located is the designer's own doctoral study and classroom tests with small groups of young children, reported through the font's own site and design blogs; no independent peer-reviewed study was found. — [lexend.com](https://lexend.com/); [Design Observer](https://designobserver.com/the-role-of-letterforms-in-reading-instruction/)
- `[CHARITY]` (search summary) Dyslexia Scotland: letter, word and line spacing combined, not font style, has the larger effect on readability; aim for line spacing of 1.5 times font size. — [Dyslexia Scotland: dyslexia-friendly typed formats](https://dyslexiascotland.org.uk/dyslexia-friendly-typed-formats)

### Inferences
- A document can follow the BDA guide fully with ordinary software: Arial, Verdana or Calibri at 12 to 14 point (larger on request), slightly expanded character spacing, 1.5 line spacing, left-aligned, bold-only emphasis, headings about 20% larger, a cream or pastel page, bullet points and short sentences. No special font needs installing.
- Choosing OpenDyslexic or Dyslexie is a matter of the reader's preference, not evidence. The studies suggest any benefit comes from the wider spacing those fonts carry, which can be reproduced in a standard font.
- The BDA's own first principle is individual preference ("Some dyslexic readers may request a larger font"; "Some dyslexic people will have their own colour preference"), so the reliable approach is to offer the BDA defaults and ask the reader which size and background they prefer.
- Supplying material as an editable Word file as well as PDF lets the reader change font, size and colour and use text-to-speech, which the earlier BDA edition recommends directly.
- The BDA guide tells writers to avoid long dashes and symbols because text readers speak them aloud; that matters if the candidate will listen to coaching material.

### Gaps
- The BDA's own 2023 PDF could not be opened, so any differences between the PDF and the mirrored web text are unchecked.
- No evidence, for or against, was found on Atkinson Hyperlegible for dyslexic readers; the one search run on it returned nothing.
- The Zorzi et al. (2012) paper and the Lexend dissertation were not read directly.
- No research was found testing the BDA's specific figures (35% letter spacing, 3.5 times word spacing, 20% larger headings) as a package.
