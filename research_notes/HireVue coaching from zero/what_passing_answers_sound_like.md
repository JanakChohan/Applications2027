## 1. Annotated example answers: weak versus strong, with assessor or expert commentary

### Takeaway
Genuinely annotated weak-versus-strong pairs are rare in public sources; the best found are a Lancaster University careers before/after pair and a 2012 eFinancialCareers piece where two named ex-investment-bank interviewers mark four real student answers. In every annotated case the weak answer is a one-line summary or a statement that could apply to any firm, and the strong answer adds how and why for each action, or a named specific, and then stops.

### Cited Findings

**A. Lancaster University Careers, interview guide [official]. Question type: "a situation where you had to deal with conflict".**
- Weak answer (15 words), described as what "many candidates would simply say": "I told my colleague to calm down and explained to him what the problem was". — [Lancaster University Careers, Interview Guide (PDF)](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)
- Commentary on the weak answer: "this would not provide a good idea of what drove you to act in this manner. How did you ask him to calm down? How did you explain the nature of the problem? By highlighting the reasons behind your action, you would make a greater impact." — [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)
- Strong answer (67 words): "I could sense that my colleague was irritated and I asked him gently to tell me what he felt the problem was. By allowing him to vent his feelings and his anger, I gave him the opportunity to calm down. I then explained to him my own point of view on the matter, emphasising how important it was that we found a solution that suited us both." — [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)
- Commentary on the strong answer: "This revised answer helps the interviewers understand what drove your actions and reinforces the feeling that you are calculating the consequences of your actions, thus retaining full control of the situation. It provides much more information about you as an individual". — [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)
- Related wording rules from the same guide: "Be personal, talk about you, not the rest of the team. Avoid saying 'we' even in a teamwork example"; "Explain what you did, how you did it, and why you did it"; "Avoid saying 'I had to….' This sounds as if you were acting under duress when you should be demonstrating your initiative"; "Steer clear of technical information, unless it is crucial to your story"; "You do not have to find really complicated examples; in particular the outcome of the story does not have to be extraordinary; what matters most are the skills you demonstrate." — [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)

**B. eFinancialCareers, "Real questions banks ask grads and how to answer them" (November 2012) [practitioner commentary on real student answers]. Four questions students said they were asked that year, the answers they gave, and verdicts from Peter Harrison (former Goldman Sachs investment banker; founder of Harrison Careers, which sells coaching, so flag as vendor too) and Guy Ballantine (RSM Tenon consultant and former investment banking graduate recruiter).**
- Credit Suisse, Investment Banking. Question: "Why our bank?" Student's answer (24 words): "Because it's a multinational organization that operates in more than 50 countries and it has a very multicultural environment. That fits with my values." Verdict (Ballantine): "This is too vague and incomplete as an answer. It is only part of a good answer and it could apply to too many organizations... Banks want you to identify which division you want to work for, so it's good to be specific, say M&A or capital markets. Mention a deal they have done recently, pick out a strength the bank has, like a number one ranking in the league tables. Definitely do not go for stock answers". — [eFinancialCareers, Nov 2012](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2012/11/real-questions-banks-ask-grads-and-how-to-answer-them)
- Morgan Stanley, Investment Banking. Question: "Why should we not give you the job?" Student's answer (23 words): "I don't come from a business administration or finance background. However, I can learn quantitative skills. I believe that I have the aptitude." Verdict (Harrison): "This answer is lightweight and not good enough. I would require an acknowledgement that the candidate understands this is a genuine weakness, along with specific steps they are taking to fix the problem. In my seven years at Goldman Sachs I interviewed around 1,300 candidates and I asked this question in 30% of interviews. I was looking for an honest character evaluation. I often rejected candidates because I either did not believe their answers or they were not talking from the heart, and it made me distrust them." — [eFinancialCareers, Nov 2012](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2012/11/real-questions-banks-ask-grads-and-how-to-answer-them)
- BNP Paribas, Technology. Question: "What do you think of bonuses?" Student's answer (91 words): "The media and therefore the public seem to take a very aggressive dislike to bonuses for bankers only. For example, no one seems to care about the excessive bonuses that some top lawyers, hedge fund managers, footballers, high-profile television presenters and others receive. Saying that, however, there do seem to be cases where the bonus is unjust and not really based on performance, which I think is wrong. In an ideal world, everyone should get rewarded, but they should only get a bonus if they performed exceptionally well during that period". Verdict (Ballantine): "This is a good answer, because it links performance and reward. It can be expanded by making it more personal... I always like it when people say what motivates them". — [eFinancialCareers, Nov 2012](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2012/11/real-questions-banks-ask-grads-and-how-to-answer-them)
- Barclays, Trading and Structuring. Brainteaser (revolver with two adjacent bullets). Student's answer (27 words): "Spin again: the probability of getting a bullet is 2/6=1/3. Do not spin, just shoot: the probability of getting a bullet is 1/4. Hence do not spin." Verdict (Harrison): "This is a correct and succinct answer; the candidate should say just this and nothing more". — [eFinancialCareers, Nov 2012](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2012/11/real-questions-banks-ask-grads-and-how-to-answer-them)

**C. HMRC careers forum, reply by "Philip K., Recruitment Manager" (20 November 2025) [official; practitioner who marks recorded-style Civil Service behaviour interviews at volume]. No sample answers, but the clearest assessor description found of what separates a pass from a top score.**
- "My team mark several thousand interviews per month, the quantity of people scoring 7s is miniscule... There is no cheat sheet to getting a 7. - "The most important thing to do, is clearly explain your personal actions, what your thought process was, why you made the decision to take these actions and why they were important/ led to success." — [HMRC Careers discussion](https://careers.hmrc.gov.uk/discussions/how-are-people-scoring-7-in-the-interview-questions-for-customer-service-advisor)
- "You do not have to make everything sound perfect, you can describe difficulties within your example as long as you explain how you have overcame these." — [HMRC Careers discussion](https://careers.hmrc.gov.uk/discussions/how-are-people-scoring-7-in-the-interview-questions-for-customer-service-advisor)
- "we often find people are trying to make their experience/ examples sound more than what they are. The most important thing is to provide real life, genuine answers with specific focus on what you actually did/ what your involvement was and how you made a difference. Dont use AI to write it for your or generate it as it tends to remove your key actions with buzz words, taking away the depth and context we need". — [HMRC Careers discussion](https://careers.hmrc.gov.uk/discussions/how-are-people-scoring-7-in-the-interview-questions-for-customer-service-advisor)

**D. UK Civil Service Insights (Substack by Grace Nkeshimana, a serving civil servant; 11 November 2024) [practitioner, candidate side]. Shows how the same example scores differently by delivery and level.**
- Scoring matrix: "Good example + poorly written = low score (1-3). Poor example + well written = low score (1-3). Good example + well written = passing score (4*-7)", with the note that "in some cases the recruiting manager may set the pass mark at 5." — [UK Civil Service Insights](https://ukcivilserviceinsights.substack.com/p/choosing-the-right-example)
- The same non-work example (volunteer treasurer forecasting ticket sales against £20k+ event costs) scored 3 for a Grade 7 role in February 2024 and 4 for an SEO role in March 2024; at a June 2024 interview the panel "really liked" the example and gave feedback on how it was communicated verbally, including that she had not brought out the wider (community) impact. ### Inferences
- The weak-to-strong move in every annotated case is the same and can be copied mechanically: take each action verb ("told", "explained", "can learn") and add how you did it and why you chose it (Lancaster, HMRC). A 15-word action becomes about 65 words. Three or four such actions plus a two-sentence setup and a one-sentence result gives roughly 250 to 300 words, which fits a two-minute slot.
- For "why this firm" the test implied by Ballantine is substitution: if the sentence would still be true with another bank's name in it ("multinational", "multicultural"), it scores as vague. The fix he gives is three named specifics: the division, a recent deal, a ranked strength.
- For weakness-type questions, Harrison's stated rejection reason was disbelief ("not talking from the heart"), which is a delivery-plus-content judgment: he wanted the candidate to concede the weakness is real, then list steps already being taken.
- "Say just this and nothing more" (Harrison) and HMRC's "you do not have to make everything sound perfect" both point the same way: assessors reward stopping when the point is made and do not expect polish.
- HireVue's own samples run 30 to 105 words, which at about 150 words a minute is 12 to 42 seconds, well under its own 60 to 90 second guidance. They read as written copy (stacked nouns, round percentages) and should be treated as structure examples, not as models of spoken register.
- Prospects' example uses "we" in the result and its reflection mentions a "delay" never set up in the story, which contradicts Lancaster's "avoid saying we" and shows even official model answers are not carefully marked. The Lancaster and HMRC wording is the safer guide.

### Gaps
- No UK bank, asset manager or Big Four firm was found publishing a scored weak and strong answer side by side. No gov.uk page publishes a Civil Service behaviour answer with its score and marker commentary; the Substack and HMRC forum are the nearest.
- The Sage/Morgan paired answers and the Leicester model answer could not be captured verbatim (paywall-style extraction limits and 403).
- The Rambling Recruiter reaction videos are the only found case of a recruiter marking a real recorded answer line by line; their content is unverified here because transcripts were unreachable.
- No annotated example specific to a one-way video (as opposed to face-to-face) UK finance answer was found.

### Inferences
- The three practitioner voices that could be quoted (HMRC, Harrison, Ballantine) never mention pace, eye line or the first ten seconds. What they describe is a content-and-credibility judgment: do I believe this person did this, and can I see their reasoning. Delivery matters to them mainly as a cue to belief ("talking from the heart").
- The HMRC comment about inflated examples and buzzwords gives a concrete behavioural rule for someone told to "sound like a colleague, not a student": describe an ordinary thing you really did in ordinary words, at its true size, with the reasons.
- Because the reviewer may replay an answer (Lancaster) and is working through dozens at once (HireVue), an answer needs to make sense on its own without the reviewer remembering the previous one.

### Gaps
- No named ex-recruiter, campus recruiter, analyst or associate at a bank, asset manager or Big Four firm was found stating, in a quotable written source, what the top few per cent of recorded answers do in the first ten seconds, at what pace, or how they end. - YouTube transcripts were not reached, so every claim made inside the videos listed is unverified here. The user or report writer could watch the two Rambling Recruiter reaction videos and the Cordingley video directly; these are the three most likely to contain reviewer-side detail.
- Glassdoor, WSO, The Student Room and Reddit were excluded by instruction.

## 3. What "be yourself", "authentic" and "genuine" mean in concrete terms, and what makes speech sound rehearsed

### Takeaway
Where firms and platforms explain the phrase at all, it reduces to three behaviours: do not read, do not recite a script or AI-written text, and describe your own real actions in your own ordinary words. Speech research adds that listeners identify read-aloud speech about four times in five from prosody alone, because reading produces one typical, regular melody, whereas speech composed in the moment varies.

### Cited Findings

**Firm and platform wording**
- Citizens Bank (US) [official]: the video interview "lets you share your skills and experience with our recruiting team in your own words and bring your personality to the interview"; "Be yourself!... While we know you want to be prepared, we want to see your authentic self on camera. It's best to avoid using AI to formulate answers or read scripted responses." — [Citizens, Video Interview Assessment](https://jobs.citizensbank.com/Video-Interview-Assessment)
- HMRC recruitment manager [official]: "provide real life, genuine answers with specific focus on what you actually did/ what your involvement was"; AI text replaces "your key actions with buzz words". — [HMRC Careers discussion](https://careers.hmrc.gov.uk/discussions/how-are-people-scoring-7-in-the-interview-questions-for-customer-service-advisor)
- HireVue's help centre gives "Be yourself" no behavioural content: "Interviewing can be nerve-wracking. Doing an On Demand interview lets you take the interview in your own space at your own time. Get comfortable and be confident in yourself." [official] — [HireVue, Preparing for your Interview](https://hirevuesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028139732-Preparing-for-your-Interview)
- HireVue's blog is more concrete: "Keep a few bullet point notes handy (but don't read them)"; a sample answer on preparing says "I also keep notes nearby to stay focused without sounding rehearsed." [vendor/platform] — [HireVue STAR post](https://www.hirevue.com/blog/candidates/star-method-for-video-interviews); [HireVue common questions post](https://www.hirevue.com/blog/candidates/common-video-interview-questions-and-answers)

**Broadcast trainers on why reading sounds like reading, and how professionals avoid it**
- NPR Training (29 Sept 2025) [expert]: "In everyday conversation, we don't give much — if any — thought to what words we're emphasizing — we just talk... But when we read from scripts, that natural rhythm can disappear. We might stress the wrong word or overdo the emphasis. The same goes for breathing... in the studio we sometimes breathe in unnatural places or simply run out of air. All of this points to one simple reality: It takes skill and technique to sound natural and engaged when reading a script." — [NPR Training, How marking scripts can help you sound more natural](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/29/g-s1-90460/how-marking-scripts-can-help-you-sound-more-natural)
- NPR's *Sound Reporting* (2nd ed., excerpted Dec 2024), Jerome Socolovsky, NPR audio journalism trainer [expert]: "Don't just read your script aloud. It will sound like you're bored, and your audience will hear it." Host Ayesha Rascoe: sounding natural, "and not like you're reading, 'is the hardest thing in the world.'" Rachel Martin: "You just have to own your own voice and talk like you actually talk in real life." Meghan Keane: "It takes a bit of work to sound like yourself". — [Current, excerpt from Sound Reporting](https://current.org/2024/12/new-edition-of-nprs-sound-reporting-shares-tips-on-cultivating-your-unique-voice/)
- Same excerpt, on what "natural" does not mean. Jessica Hansen, NPR voice coach for eight years: "sounding like yourself means not that you're at a bar talking to your buddy over a beer... You have to perform the story." — [Current](https://current.org/2024/12/new-edition-of-nprs-sound-reporting-shares-tips-on-cultivating-your-unique-voice/)
- Same excerpt, the working method. Eyder Peralta: "Live the script"; the trainer's gloss is that hosts who sound natural are "thinking about the meaning of the words coming out of their mouth", and to re-imagine the scene and the people involved while speaking: "If you're in the moment, you'll do that naturally." — [Current](https://current.org/2024/12/new-edition-of-nprs-sound-reporting-shares-tips-on-cultivating-your-unique-voice/)
- NPR Training's checklist for a delivery that sounds "authentic, conversational and engaged" starts with the words: "Is the language conversational and natural? (Delivery starts with the writing. If the script isn't written the way someone talks naturally, they won't sound natural.)" — [NPR Training, The art of coaching vocal performance](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/05/g-s1-86003/the-art-of-coaching-vocal-performance-how-to-help-hosts-and-reporters-own-the-mic)
- NPR intern Abē Levine on wedding toasts, a close analogue of a memorised interview answer: they "were often stuffy, over-rehearsed and thirsting for personality." — [NPR, 7 tips to let your voice shine through](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/17/1156597697/how-to-write-script-speech-podcast-voice)

**Research on read versus spontaneous speech**
- Blaauw (1995, ICPhS, Utrecht) [expert]: 48 fluent spontaneous utterances and the same speaker's read versions were each judged by 10 listeners; "The average classification score was 79% correct (81% for the spontaneous utterances and 77% for the read utterances)." Conclusion: "read speech is characterized by a typical prosodic make-up. Spontaneous speech seems to be characterized by the absence of that typical prosodic make-up; digressions from the 'read' prosody, in any direction, appear to induce listeners to classify the speech as spontaneous." Also: "Read speech is typically clear and careful, whereas spontaneous speech can easily digress in any direction." One male speaker (a Utrecht study, so presumably Dutch speech); small study. — [Blaauw 1995, ICPhS proceedings (PDF)](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/Phonetics/icphs/ICPhS1995/13_ICPhS_1995_Vol_3/p13.3_254.pdf)
- Laan (1997, *Speech Communication* 22:43-65), as summarised in search results (paper not read directly): two speakers spoke spontaneously then read their own transcripts; swapping pitch, durations and spectral features each shifted listeners' read/spontaneous judgments, but "none of these acoustic features by itself can clearly discriminate between the two speaking styles." — [Laan 1997 record](https://researchr.org/publication/Laan97)
- Wagner and Windmann, as summarised in search results (paper returned 403): listeners "may at times be unable to differentiate between read and spontaneous utterances, if speakers are able to truthfully re-enact spontaneous dialogues." — [Wagner & Windmann (CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/211853700.pdf)
- Kirkland, Gustafson and Székely (Interspeech 2023) [expert]; already in the earlier notes, one point added here: the paper notes disfluencies "can convey friendliness or spontaneity", yet in its own experiment "disfluent speech was also rated as less sincere" as well as less competent and confident; the competence penalty shrank when listeners attributed the disfluency to anxiety. Synthesised speech, 30 to 40 second clips. — [Kirkland et al. 2023 (PDF)](https://www.speech.kth.se/tts-demos/kirkland23_interspeech.pdf)

### Inferences
- A workable definition of "genuine" for a beginner, assembled from the firm wording: (1) the words are ones you would use out loud to a tutor or manager; (2) the events are real and at their real size; (3) you are thinking about what happened while you say it, not about the next line. Each of the three has a named source above (NPR writing rule; HMRC; Peralta/Socolovsky).
- Blaauw's result implies a memorised answer is detectable not because it is fluent but because it is regular: same melody, same pace, breaths in the wrong places. The countermeasure used by broadcasters is not to add errors but to re-attach emphasis and pauses to meaning (see NPR marking technique in section 4).
- Wagner and Windmann's finding, if it holds, means rehearsed material can pass as spontaneous when the speaker re-enacts the original thinking, which fits the earlier notes' TED finding that full memorisation works only past the "valley of awkwardness".
- Adding filler words to sound natural is not supported: the evidence in Kirkland et al. is that disfluency lowers sincerity ratings too.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that directly tests whether interview answers judged "rehearsed" or "read" are rated less sincere or less hireable than the same content delivered spontaneously. The read/spontaneous literature measures detectability, not preference.
- No UK finance employer was found defining "authentic" in behavioural terms beyond warnings against AI and scripts; Citizens Bank is a US retail bank.
- The Ask a Manager column on a candidate who read every answer from a script (August 2021) was located but returned 403 and is not quoted. — [Ask a Manager, Aug 2021](https://www.askamanager.org/2021/08/candidate-read-all-their-answers-from-a-script-i-dont-want-to-talk-to-people-while-theyre-driving-and-more.html)

## 4. Speaking mechanics for a beginner: technique and source for each

### Takeaway
Broadcast training, a speech and language therapy leaflet and university media guides give a concrete technique for almost every mechanic asked about; the common thread is to write and rehearse for the ear (short sentences, one idea each, said aloud while drafting), aim for roughly 150 to 180 words a minute, pause where the meaning changes, and plan the exact last sentence.

### Cited Findings

**Sentence length and writing for the ear**
- University of Westminster School of Media and Communications (Charles Carroll) [expert]: on radio "The listener only gets one chance to understand"; "the normal rules of grammar and punctuation don't apply. You're writing 'stored speech'"; "Sentences for the printed page can be long and contain lots of sub-clauses. On radio, the newsreader has to have somewhere to breathe. That's why radio script-writers speak the words out loud as they are written. It does not matter what it looks like on the page, the key question is – does it sound right?"; "Vocabulary is kept simple"; wasted words "are passengers". — [University of Westminster, Radio news: the art of the written spoken word (PDF)](https://www.westminster.ac.uk/sites/default/public-files/general-documents/EPQ_radio-news.pdf)
- NPR (Life Kit host Marielle Segarra) [expert]: "Run-on sentences going in many directions are your enemy... Keep your sentences focused by saying what you mean"; "Say your ideas out loud – once before you write them, and again after you've written them down. Ask yourself: Do I sound like me?"; "Apostrophes are your friend. To sound less formal, use 'won't' instead of 'will not' and 'don't' instead of 'do not.'" — [NPR, 7 tips](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/17/1156597697/how-to-write-script-speech-podcast-voice)
- A UBC workshop page, per search snippet only: "Just write one thought per sentence. The best sentence structure when writing for the ear is subject (someone) / verb (doing) / object (something)." — [UBC Wiki, Writing for the Ear](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:LFS400/Workshops/Writing_For_the_Ear)

**Speaking rate and how to measure your own**
- Westminster: "A typical radio news bulletin lasts about three minutes... three minutes only allows space for about 540 words", which is 180 words a minute. — [University of Westminster (PDF)](https://www.westminster.ac.uk/sites/default/public-files/general-documents/EPQ_radio-news.pdf)
- A study of radio bulletins cited by Wikipedia found "the average rate of bulletins varied from 168 (English, BBC) to 210 words per minutes (Spanish, RNE)." — [Wikipedia, Speech tempo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_tempo)
- Per search results (textbook page returned 403), Stewart and Alexander's *Broadcast Journalism* says British radio usually favours three words per second, or 180 wpm, and that station styles range from 140 to 220 wpm. Snippet-level. — [Broadcast Journalism, ch. 17 Newsreading mechanics](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/broadcast-journalism-6th/9780240810249/xhtml/ch17.xhtml)
- VirtualSpeech [vendor] attributes to the National Center for Voice and Speech that "the average conversation rate for English speakers in the United States is about 150 wpm", and gives the self-measurement method: record yourself for a few minutes, get a word count (by hand or speech-to-text), convert the time to minutes (4 min 30 s = 4.5), then "Speaking rate (wpm) = total words / number of minutes." — [VirtualSpeech, Average speaking rate](https://virtualspeech.com/blog/average-speaking-rate-words-per-minute)
- NPR Training: "Slow down when introducing new or important information. It's fine to pick up the pace during background or familiar content." — [NPR Training, marking scripts](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/29/g-s1-90460/how-marking-scripts-can-help-you-sound-more-natural)
- UC San Diego's instructor guide: "don't worry if you think you're speaking too quickly—studies suggest that fast, energetic delivery often correlates with higher engagement." (Their cited basis is Guo et al. 2014 on lecture videos.) — [UC San Diego Multimedia Services, On-Camera Performance Tips](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/peformance-tips.html)
- Lancaster Careers, by contrast: "Relax and speak slowly." — [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)

**Pausing and emphasis (the NPR script-marking method)**
- Mark a slash where a short pause belongs: "She moved to New York City five years ago to look for steady work / and raise her son. A brief pause gives the listener a chance to register two distinct motivations." — [NPR Training, marking scripts](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/29/g-s1-90460/how-marking-scripts-can-help-you-sound-more-natural)
- "Underline or bold the words that carry the meaning — the ones you'd naturally lean into if you were in a conversation... Let the underlined words guide your voice — not dominate it." — [NPR Training, marking scripts](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/29/g-s1-90460/how-marking-scripts-can-help-you-sound-more-natural)
- "If the copy is dense or visually overwhelming, add a line break between thoughts." And: "Read your script out loud. If it flows, great. If it doesn't, mark what helps you sound more natural and engaged." — [NPR Training, marking scripts](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/29/g-s1-90460/how-marking-scripts-can-help-you-sound-more-natural)
- Matt Abrahams (Stanford GSB lecturer): "Most audiences are very forgiving, and some may actually be thankful for the pause because it allows them time to process what you've presented." — [Stanford GSB, The Art of the Graceful Recovery, 4 March 2014](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/matt-abrahams-presentations-art-graceful-recovery)

**Ending sentences on a falling tone; upward inflection; trailing off**
- Vaughan-Johnston, Guyer, Fabrigar, Lamprinakos and Briñol (*Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, online 2024, vol. 52 pp. 3-21) [expert]: "In three experiments, we show that falling (vs. rising) vocal intonation at the ends of sentences can signal speaker confidence." The persuasion benefit is conditional: "falling intonation only benefits persuasion under certain circumstances"; under moderate attention it "increased message processing, bolstering the benefit of strong over weak messages". — [University of Birmingham research portal, abstract](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/falling-vocal-intonation-signals-speaker-confidence-and-condition/)
- Roche et al. (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 14 Dec 2023) [expert]: "rising intonation is a common cue that both women and men produce to communicate lack of confidence, but listeners are more sensitive to this cue when it is produced by women." — [Roche et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10753021/)
- Levon and Yang (2019), per search snippet only: uptalk carries "strong stereotypical associations that link it to the speech of young women and a lack of authority or credibility more generally." — [Levon & Yang 2019 (PDF)](https://qmul.ac.uk/sllf/media/sllf-new/department-of-linguistics/documents/41%29-Levon-%26-Yang-2019.pdf)
- NPR Training's listening checklist treats trailing off as a fault to coach out: "Are breaths smooth, without gasps, awkward pauses or trailing off?"; "Is there enough variation in pitch to avoid sounding monotonous?" — [NPR Training, coaching vocal performance](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/05/g-s1-86003/the-art-of-coaching-vocal-performance-how-to-help-hosts-and-reporters-own-the-mic)

**Starting the answer**
- HireVue's mechanics define the start: after prep time, or after you click "Start Recording", "you will see a 3-second countdown, and then your recording will begin." During prep the screen shows "Not Recording". — [HireVue, Getting Started with Your On Demand Interview (Computer)](https://hirevuesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028433751-Getting-Started-with-Your-On-Demand-Interview-Computer)
- A presenter's trick for a natural first sentence: Marielle Segarra "sometimes starts her sentences with, 'Hey mom ...' to get herself into a comfortable space", then cuts it in the edit. — [NPR, 7 tips](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/17/1156597697/how-to-write-script-speech-podcast-voice)

**Ending the answer**
- Lynn Meade, *Advanced Public Speaking* (University of Arkansas) [expert]: "Your ending will leave a lasting impression so do not leave it to chance. Plan out the exact last words and then resist the temptation to tack on any last comments. Speech endings are always challenging, they are even more difficult online." — [Meade, Speaking to a Camera](https://uark.pressbooks.pub/speaking/chapter/speaking-to-a-camera/)
- HireVue: "You are not required to use the full response time; you can click Done Answering when you have completed your answer. Otherwise, your recording will automatically submit after the allotted response time has run out." — [HireVue, Getting Started (Computer)](https://hirevuesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028433751-Getting-Started-with-Your-On-Demand-Interview-Computer)
- Harrison on a complete answer: "the candidate should say just this and nothing more". — [eFinancialCareers, Nov 2012](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2012/11/real-questions-banks-ask-grads-and-how-to-answer-them)

**A mistake or blank mid-answer**
- Abrahams: "First, try not to be too hard on yourself. Often, speakers blurt out comments that reduce their credibility: 'Sheesh, how could I forget?' 'I'm so nervous'... If you must overtly acknowledge your forgetfulness, simply apologize and collect your thoughts." — [Stanford GSB, 2014](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/matt-abrahams-presentations-art-graceful-recovery)
- Abrahams' recovery method: "focus on what you've just said. Too often, people who blank out try to figure out what they need to say next. But you are more likely to continue smoothly if you reorient yourself by looking to what you said previously." Scripted bridges he offers: "So just to step back for a moment, I've already covered how X and Y are relevant…" and "So we can see that [insert your core message] is really important." — [Stanford GSB, 2014](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/matt-abrahams-presentations-art-graceful-recovery)
- HMRC assessor: "You do not have to make everything sound perfect". — [HMRC Careers discussion](https://careers.hmrc.gov.uk/discussions/how-are-people-scoring-7-in-the-interview-questions-for-customer-service-advisor)
- Kirkland et al.: the competence penalty for disfluency was "attenuated when participants attributed disfluency to anxiety." — [Kirkland et al. 2023 (PDF)](https://www.speech.kth.se/tts-demos/kirkland23_interspeech.pdf)

**Where to look; notes**
- HireVue: "Look at the camera, not at the screen: Looking at your webcam while speaking gives the feeling of eye contact to the person on the other end." — [HireVue, Preparing for your Interview](https://hirevuesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028139732-Preparing-for-your-Interview)
- Meade: "If it helps, draw a smiley face and put it on your camera to remind you to look in the 'eyes' of your audience"; "Tape your note cards to the top of your computer screen or hang them on something behind your computer. Place your cards so you never have to look down to see your notes." — [Meade, Speaking to a Camera](https://uark.pressbooks.pub/speaking/chapter/speaking-to-a-camera/)
- UC San Diego: "try to position your camera as close as possible to your speaker notes"; eye contact "is not about locking eyes constantly"; "If you're comfortable with your material and setup, you're more likely to maintain natural, confident eye contact without appearing stiff or distracted." — [UC San Diego, On-Camera Performance Tips](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/peformance-tips.html)
- Lancaster: "Ensure any prompts (post-its or notes) are within the eyesight of your computer so you do not lose eye contact by looking down at notes." — [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)

**Sitting, standing and framing**
- Meade: "make sure the camera is level or a little higher than your eyes. The most common mistake that I see is when someone leans the laptop lid back making it look like the camera is looking up the nose of the speaker" (fix: "set your laptop on a stack of books"); "Stand Don't Sit (If possible)... if in doubt stand. You will have better air support if you are standing. You will also gesture more freely"; frame "a 3/4 shot" so "the audience can see your gestures". — [Meade, Speaking to a Camera](https://uark.pressbooks.pub/speaking/chapter/speaking-to-a-camera/)
- Lancaster: "Sit at a table (avoid an office/ computer style chair if you might be prone to 'swinging' if nervous!)"; "Align the webcam with your face and shoulders; ensure you are looking straight ahead at the camera not down". — [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)

**Hands**
- NPR Training: "One of the simplest ways to help a host or reporter sound more natural is to get them using their hands and facial expressions as they speak." (This is advice for audio, where nobody sees the hands; the effect is on the voice.) — [NPR Training, coaching vocal performance](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/05/g-s1-86003/the-art-of-coaching-vocal-performance-how-to-help-hosts-and-reporters-own-the-mic)
- Meade: "You should have open gestures. If you do a tight headshot, you will not see your gestures, so you need to pull the camera back and gesture higher." — [Meade, Speaking to a Camera](https://uark.pressbooks.pub/speaking/chapter/speaking-to-a-camera/)
- HireVue's blog: "Sit straight, smile naturally, and use hand gestures sparingly to make your delivery engaging without distraction." [vendor/platform] — [HireVue STAR post](https://www.hirevue.com/blog/candidates/star-method-for-video-interviews)

**Warming up the voice and loosening delivery before recording**
- Royal Berkshire NHS Foundation Trust, Speech and Language Therapy leaflet (Caroline Parry, April 2024) [expert/clinical]: "Just as you would warm up before doing a physical activity, such as running, it is important to warm up the muscles involved in voice production, particularly before prolonged or demanding voice use." Sequence: relax shoulders and jaw; breathe in through the nose and out through the mouth silently; "Rapidly, but silently, do an 'ooo-eee' movement to move the lips"; stick out the tongue and hold 5 seconds, three times; "Take a deep breath in and release on a yawn-sigh... Repeat 3 times"; "Breathe in and then let the breath out on a lip trill ('brrrrr'). Repeat several times"; glide the trill up and down in pitch; sustain "ssss", "zzzz", "vvvv" three to five times each; sip water. — [Royal Berkshire NHS, Voice warm up and warm down (PDF)](https://royalberkshire.nhs.uk/media/5ehceiw2/voice-warm-up-and-warm-down_apr24.pdf)
- NPR Training's fault-and-fix drills, each done once in exaggerated form and then "again, naturally": if stiff, "Try a belly laugh... The laugh releases physical tension"; if too fast with words running together, "Try a dramatic drawl... finishing each word before moving to the next"; if stilted, "Go fast — as fast as possible... It loosens stiffness"; if flat, "Try sing-song exaggeration... It helps add vocal range and energy". — [NPR Training, coaching vocal performance](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/09/05/g-s1-86003/the-art-of-coaching-vocal-performance-how-to-help-hosts-and-reporters-own-the-mic)
- NPR's Meghan Keane, from Jessica Hansen's training: "take her script and read it with different emotions: proud, loving, angry, bored, jealous and so on. 'It's really interesting to see which emotions actually make you sound the best.'" — [Current, Sound Reporting excerpt](https://current.org/2024/12/new-edition-of-nprs-sound-reporting-shares-tips-on-cultivating-your-unique-voice/)
- NPR (Segarra): "After doing your first reading, do it again – but outrageously, dramatically... Even if you don't ultimately go with that version of yourself, you'll have stretched your range beyond your classroom reading voice." — [NPR, 7 tips](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/17/1156597697/how-to-write-script-speech-podcast-voice)
- UC San Diego: "Some instructors find it helpful to do light physical warmups or vocal exercises before filming. Others revisit a favorite example or remind themselves why the topic matters". — [UC San Diego, On-Camera Performance Tips](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/peformance-tips.html)

**Energy**
- Ayesha Rascoe (NPR host): "Whatever I'm reading, even if it's sad, you still have to bring energy to it"; when she lowered her energy she was told her voice "sounded flat". Eyder Peralta: "People can hear you talking through a smile." — [Current, Sound Reporting excerpt](https://current.org/2024/12/new-edition-of-nprs-sound-reporting-shares-tips-on-cultivating-your-unique-voice/)
- UC San Diego: enthusiasm "doesn't mean you need to turn into a game show host. It simply means letting your interest in the material shine through. If there's a concept you find elegant, powerful, or surprising, say so." — [UC San Diego, On-Camera Performance Tips](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/peformance-tips.html)

### Inferences
- A target of roughly 150 to 180 words a minute is defensible for a HireVue answer: conversational American English averages about 150 and British bulletin delivery about 168 to 180. A two-minute slot therefore holds about 300 to 360 words at most, and a 90-second answer about 225 to 270. The earlier notes already cover timing arithmetic; the new point is the broadcast benchmark and the self-measurement formula.
- The sources disagree on speed (UCSD: fast is fine; Lancaster: speak slowly). They are reconcilable through NPR's rule: slow on new or important information, quicker on background.
- Sources disagree on sitting versus standing and on gestures. For a webcam HireVue the practical reading is: either posture works; if seated, use a fixed chair at a table; let the hands move because it changes the voice (NPR), keeping them low or within frame rather than waving at the lens.
- A beginner can apply NPR's marking method directly to bullet-point notes: put a slash where the idea changes, underline the one or two words per sentence that carry the meaning, and leave the rest unscripted.
- "Carry on, correct briefly" is supported by Abrahams (no self-criticism; step back to the last point) and by the HMRC assessor's statement that perfection is not expected. A usable recovery line for a HireVue is his "So just to step back for a moment…".

### Gaps
- BBC Academy guidance could not be reached (bbc.co.uk is blocked to the search tool and the public News style guide is about written style). The "three words a second" BBC rule is supported here only by the Westminster 540-words-in-three-minutes figure, the Wikipedia-cited 168 wpm BBC measurement and a textbook snippet.
- No source was found giving a rule for the first second of an answer beyond the platform countdown; first-sentence technique is covered in the earlier notes.
- No controlled evidence was found on hand gestures, standing versus sitting or vocal warm-ups in recorded interviews specifically; the guidance is craft knowledge from adjacent fields.
- The falling-intonation and uptalk studies use short persuasive messages or isolated sentences, mostly with North American listeners; none tests interview ratings.

## 5. Conversational versus formal register, plain words and jargon

### Takeaway
Broadcast trainers and multimedia-learning research both favour plain spoken English with contractions and "I"/"you"; the best-known experiment (Oppenheimer 2006) found that swapping in needlessly long words lowered readers' ratings of the writer's intelligence, and a 650-person study found jargon made readers disengage even when it was defined. The limit is that these are studies of written text and lay audiences, not of interviewers who share the candidate's technical vocabulary.

### Cited Findings
- Oppenheimer, D. M. (2006), "Consequences of erudite vernacular utilized irrespective of necessity: problems with using long words needlessly", *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 20:139-156 [expert]. As reported by the Harvard Crimson: the paper "found that contrary to prevailing wisdom among undergraduates, readers are more likely to think that clear, concise writers are more intelligent", and "almost two thirds of the Princeton students polled admitted to using a thesaurus when writing an essay 'to give the impression that the content is more valid or intelligent.'" Oppenheimer: "by no means should you go out of your way to make your writing less clear, as many students tend to do". — [Harvard Crimson, 18 Oct 2006](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2006/10/18/bigger-isnt-always-better-big-words/); [paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1178)
- Per search-result summaries of the paper (full text returned 403): the experiments replaced words in graduate-school application essays with longer thesaurus alternatives, and also used dissertation abstracts, translations of Descartes and a hard-to-read font; lower fluency of any kind lowered ratings; when raters were told the source of the difficulty they over-corrected. The finding concerns needless complexity, not long words as such. Snippet-level. — [paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1178)
- Shulman and colleagues (2020), *Journal of Language and Social Psychology*, N = 650 [expert]. Ohio State's summary: people who read jargon versions "later said they were less interested in science", and "it made no difference if the jargon terms... were defined in the text". Lead author Hillary Shulman: "The use of difficult, specialized words are a signal that tells people that they don't belong... You can tell them what the terms mean, but it doesn't matter." — [Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/the-use-of-jargon-kills-peoples-interest-in-science-politics/)
- UC San Diego, citing Mayer's personalization principle [expert]: "people learn better when instructors adopt a conversational, informal style rather than a formal or academic one (Mayer, 2009)." Concrete changes: "Use contractions ('you're' instead of 'you are')"; "Include personal pronouns like 'you' and 'I'"; "this isn't about dumbing things down. It's about sounding like a real human." Test: "try reading it out loud to check how it actually sounds. If it feels stiff or overly academic", loosen it. — [UC San Diego, On-Camera Performance Tips](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/peformance-tips.html)
- NPR (Segarra) [expert]: "Fancier isn't better in radio. 'So it'd be like saying "the vehicle" instead of "the car." Why would you say that? No one talks like that'"; "you're not here to write an essay or give a presidential address. You're writing for the ear. This means writing in a casual, conversational tone – like you're telling a story to a friend". — [NPR, 7 tips](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/17/1156597697/how-to-write-script-speech-podcast-voice)
- The limit on casualness, from NPR's voice coach: natural does not mean "at a bar talking to your buddy over a beer... You have to perform the story." — [Current, Sound Reporting excerpt](https://current.org/2024/12/new-edition-of-nprs-sound-reporting-shares-tips-on-cultivating-your-unique-voice/)
- Lancaster Careers on technical language in competency answers: "Steer clear of technical information, unless it is crucial to your story." — [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)
- Harvard writing preceptor Lydia Fillingham on a specific student habit that makes prose stiff: "One thing people do a lot of is use the noun form instead of a verb. Instead of using 'spent more,' they write 'spending increased'". — [Harvard Crimson, 18 Oct 2006](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2006/10/18/bigger-isnt-always-better-big-words/)
- HMRC assessor on buzzwords: AI-written answers "remove your key actions with buzz words, taking away the depth and context we need". — [HMRC Careers discussion](https://careers.hmrc.gov.uk/discussions/how-are-people-scoring-7-in-the-interview-questions-for-customer-service-advisor)

### Inferences
- The register to copy is "explaining to a tutor or a manager you get on with": contractions, first person, verbs instead of abstract nouns ("I checked the numbers" not "a verification of the figures was undertaken"), everyday words where an everyday word exists, and technical terms only where they are the real name of the thing (a deal type, a product, a ratio).
- In a finance interview, correct technical vocabulary is part of the evidence of knowledge, so the Oppenheimer and Shulman findings should be applied to padding and corporate buzzwords ("leverage synergies", "passionate about driving value"), not to accurate terms such as "leveraged buyout" or "duration".
- A quick self-check that follows from the sources: read the answer aloud; any sentence you would never say to a friend who works in finance gets rewritten.

### Gaps
- Oppenheimer 2006 could not be read in full, so sample sizes and effect sizes are not reported here; the study concerns written text judged by student readers.
- No study was found testing plain versus formal spoken register on interview ratings.
- The Brown, Anicich and Galinsky work on low status and jargon use was searched for but not retrieved; not cited.

## 6. Talking to a camera as a learnable skill

### Takeaway
Presenters and lecturers treat the lens as one person and use physical props and rehearsal to make that feel real: a face or note on the camera, a spoken address to a named person, hiding the self-view, and talking the answer through aloud several times first. HireVue itself offers a "Hide Video" control so candidates do not have to watch themselves.

### Cited Findings
- UC San Diego Multimedia Services [expert]: "teaching to a camera is different: without audience feedback or shared energy, it can be harder to find the right cadence or sense of connection"; rehearsal "is not just about knowing what to say—it's about building the confidence and clarity to say it well, even in the silence of a studio or home office"; "Perform your material a few times, even if it's just to your laptop"; "rehearsing helps free up mental space. When you're confident in your content, it becomes much easier to focus on how you're delivering it". — [UC San Diego, On-Camera Performance Tips](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/peformance-tips.html)
- Same guide: an informal tone "enhances the perception that your video is a conversation between you and the student viewer". — [UC San Diego](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/peformance-tips.html)
- Meade (University of Arkansas) [expert]: "draw a smiley face and put it on your camera to remind you to look in the 'eyes' of your audience"; "record a test segment and then make sure it works before you record too much"; "Record yourself speaking for a minute and then play it back." — [Meade, Speaking to a Camera](https://uark.pressbooks.pub/speaking/chapter/speaking-to-a-camera/)
- NPR (Segarra) [expert]: begin with "Hey mom ..." to put yourself in a comfortable, one-listener frame; write "like you're telling a story to a friend, or even your pet iguana". — [NPR, 7 tips](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/17/1156597697/how-to-write-script-speech-podcast-voice)
- NPR's Eyder Peralta and Jerome Socolovsky [expert]: while speaking, picture the scene and the people in the story; "imagine you're still on the scene... It's being in the moment, being in that world when you're actually reading." — [Current, Sound Reporting excerpt](https://current.org/2024/12/new-edition-of-nprs-sound-reporting-shares-tips-on-cultivating-your-unique-voice/)
- HireVue help centre, "Nervous about being on camera?" [official]: "we recommend trying a few practice questions before your interview starts so you can feel more comfortable. In addition to practicing before your interview, you can utilize a feature to 'hide' your camera. This will make it so you can't see yourself as easily while talking during your interview, allowing you to focus more on your responses." The control is labelled "Hide Video". — [HireVue, Nervous about being on camera?](https://hirevuesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360042042452-Nervous-about-being-on-camera)
- Careers Wales [official]: "Practice answering questions aloud. If you can, practice with someone that can give you honest feedback." Lancaster Careers: "Practise your answers with a critical friend". — [Careers Wales](https://careerswales.gov.wales/node/1459); [Lancaster University Careers](https://portal.lancaster.ac.uk/ask/download?document=%2Fmedia%2Flancaster-university%2Fcontent-assets%2Fdocuments%2Fcareers%2Fguides%2FInterviewGuide%28Accessible%29.pdf)
- Search-snippet level only (pages not retrievable): Loughborough University's "Tips for filming yourself" says "Imagine your audience as one person, sitting just behind the camera of your recording device" and that video "is a less formal medium than a lecture room presentation so adapt your style accordingly"; University of Michigan's online teaching page says "Speak directly into the camera as if it were a person". — [Loughborough PDF (404 on fetch)](https://www.lboro.ac.uk/media/wwwlboroacuk/external/content/services/cap/review/documentsandfiles/Tips%20for%20filming%20Yourself%20(low%20res.%20version)%5B6%5D%5B1%5D.pdf); [University of Michigan, Getting Comfortable On Screen](https://onlineteaching.umich.edu/getting-comfortable-on-screen)
- YouTube presenter-trainers located by title only, not verified: "How to Talk to the Camera the Way TV Presenters Do" (Chris Goor, 8:21, about 66,000 views; sells a "Couch to ..." course) and "How to talk to the camera: Naturally" (Presenter Academy, 5:36). [vendor/coach] — [YouTube rAIhsokIdJY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAIhsokIdJY); [YouTube djbpR4QffWM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=djbpR4QffWM)

### Inferences
- A beginner's sequence that follows from the sources: (1) tell the story aloud to a real person once; (2) tell it again to the laptop with a face stuck beside the lens, starting "Hey [name]," in your head or aloud in practice; (3) in HireVue practice mode, record, hide the self-view, play back; (4) repeat until the content no longer needs attention and attention can go to the listener.
- Hiding the self-view removes one known distraction (watching yourself) and makes it easier to hold the lens; it is a platform feature, so using it carries no risk.
- The "imagine the scene" method from NPR gives a concrete meaning to "be in the moment": while describing an action, picture the room and the person you were dealing with.

### Gaps
- No trial was found comparing these techniques; they are practitioner methods. The earlier notes cover the anxiety and no-audience research.
- Loughborough and Michigan guidance is quoted from search snippets, not from the pages.
- Whether the "Hide Video" control appears in every employer's HireVue configuration is not stated by HireVue.

### Takeaway

### Inferences
- A first-timer can open the invitation link days before the deadline, run the equipment test and unlimited practice questions, and close the browser without starting the real interview, provided they stay on the route marked by the blue practice ribbon and never click "Begin" or "Start Now". This lets them rehearse the exact timing loop (prep countdown, 3-second countdown, response countdown, "Done Answering") on the real interface.
- Since playback exists only in practice, the practice recordings are the only chance to check framing, eye line and sound on the actual device; they vanish once you leave the playback screen, so watch them fully each time.
- The three practice questions are generic and cycle; they are for learning the software, not for predicting the real questions.
- Because a dropped connection mid-question can submit a partial answer, a wired or strong connection and closing other camera-using apps (Teams, Zoom, FaceTime) before starting are the two failures worth preventing.
