Part 0

Your two claims, and the verdict

You said two things. Both are broadly right. Both need a correction before you say them to a practitioner, and both become much stronger once the correction is in.

0.1 What you said

Claim one, as you put it

"I like it because there's no house view. I'm not biased on one strategy or pod, because all the pods are separate, and that's how they make money."

Claim two, as you put it

"I like the pass-through model, as it'll allow us to really get the funding we need to build out our own proprietary systems, utilise AI, all of these things."

0.2 The verdict on claim one

Right: Millennium's centre does not pick securities or hold a market view; each of the 350-plus teams runs its own strategy under its own limits, and the firm's return is the sum of many independent bets rather than one person's judgement [S352][S351]. That is why the return is smooth, and it is the single biggest structural difference from a fund like Bridgewater or Pershing Square, where one view drives everything. "That's how they make money" is also right, in a precise sense: the money is made from diversification, from the fact that hundreds of small, uncorrelated bets produce a return with far less risk than any one of them [S340][S347].

The correction: "no house view" is true about alpha and false about risk. The centre has a very strong house view about what a pod may look like: how much it may lose before its capital is halved, how much market and factor exposure it may carry, how big it may be, how it is financed [S104][S113][S350]. Say "no house view on what to buy; a very strong house view on how much to risk". A practitioner will hear the difference at once, and it is the difference between a candidate who has read the homepage and one who understands the machine. Two further nuances: independence has costs (no idea flow, duplicated work, teams that crowd into the same trade anyway, as July 2026 showed [S223]), and it is not unique to Millennium, though Millennium sits at the far end of the spectrum [S343].

0.3 The verdict on claim two

Right: under pass-through, investors pay the firm's operating costs directly, on top of a performance fee, instead of a fixed management fee [S104][S87]. That is what lets the platform pay winning teams in a year when the firm as a whole is flat, hire aggressively, and fund a centre that is more than half the firm: 54% of staff are outside investment functions, and the firm reports over 1,800 technologists [S392][S382]. Englander's own framing is that the firm competes for talent "with technology companies as much as we do with financial services firms" [S376]. So yes: pass-through is the funding mechanism for the platform, including its systems and its AI work.

The correction: most of the pass-through dollar is people, not systems. Where filings have been read, at one peer 87% of pass-through was compensation [S372]. Technology is real and growing, but it is the minority. And the more important point is who pays: the investor, not the firm. So "pass-through funds our systems" is only half the sentence; the other half is "which is why every central function, including the desk I am interviewing for, has to prove it earns its place, because its cost lands on the investor's statement" [S366][S370]. Say that, and you have turned a claim about money into a claim about accountability, which is what a head of desk cares about. Three further nuances: pass-through is now the industry norm at platforms (about 80% of multi-strategy firms use it [S372]), so it does not by itself make Millennium stand out; investors are pushing back on it, and Millennium's answer has been a fee floor and longer lock-ups rather than a retreat [S87][S370][S400]; and the model only works while net returns after all those costs beat what the investor could get elsewhere [S366].

0.4 The two claims, upgraded

Claim one, as you should say it

"Millennium has no house view on what to buy, and a very strong house view on how much to risk. Each of the 350-plus teams runs its own strategy behind information barriers; the centre allocates capital, sets loss and exposure limits, and picks no stocks. The return is the average of hundreds of independent bets, which is why it barely moves with the market and has had one losing year since 1989. The cost is that the teams do not share ideas and can still crowd into the same trade, which is exactly why a central desk that sees all of them has to be neutral and discreet."

Claim two, as you should say it

"Millennium passes its operating costs through to investors on top of the performance fee, with a floor of one per cent or twenty per cent of gains since 2023. That is what funds a centre that is more than half the firm, the eighteen hundred technologists and the AI work, and lets the firm pay a winning team in a year the fund is flat. But the investor pays for it, and press estimates say they keep about half of gross returns. So the model only holds while net returns justify it, and every central cost, including a corporate access desk, has to be able to show what it delivered. I find that a healthy place to work: the accountability is built into the fee."

The rest of this document is the evidence behind those two paragraphs, built from nothing.

Part 1

Foundations from zero: the assumptions you need before either mechanism makes sense

Six ideas, each in plain English, each with the one number or picture that makes it stick. If you already have them, skim the figures and move to Part 2.

1.1 A fund, and how its manager is paid

A fund is a pool of money that investors hand to a manager, who invests it and charges for the service. A hedge fund is a private fund, open only to institutions and wealthy individuals, that may bet on prices falling as well as rising and may borrow to make its bets bigger [S151]. The classic charge is "two and twenty": a management fee of about 2% of assets every year regardless of results, and a performance fee of 20% of any profit. The management fee pays the firm's costs: salaries, offices, data, technology. The performance fee is the reward. Over time the average has drifted below two and twenty, and the typical management fee at a large hedge fund is about 1.35% [S373].

1.2 A portfolio manager, a strategy, and a view

A portfolio manager (PM) is the person who decides what a book buys and sells. A strategy is the repeatable way they try to make money: buying European industrial companies whose margins will expand and shorting those whose margins will shrink; trading the gap between a bond and the future on it; trading how a stock moves around its results. A view is a belief about the world that shapes the book: rates will fall, AI capital spending will disappoint, the euro will weaken. A house view is one such belief held at the top of a firm and imposed on every book beneath it. At Bridgewater, the world's largest hedge fund for most of the last two decades, the flagship fund is built on the founder's economic framework; at Pershing Square, one investor's judgement on a dozen companies is the whole product. Neither is wrong; both are bets on one mind being right.

1.4 A pod, and what "350 of them" means

A pod is one PM and a few analysts running one strategy on a slice of the firm's capital, under limits the centre sets, paid a share of their own profit after their own costs [S351][S352]. Millennium says it has more than 350 [S71]. They do not share ideas or positions: a former head of enterprise risk describes pods as "a siloed group" that "don't communicate with other pods" [S351]. The centre, everyone not in a pod, is about 54% of roughly 6,700 staff [S392]: risk, technology, treasury, compliance, operations, and the service desks, including corporate access.

1.5 Why an investor pays for any of this

Pension funds and endowments have liabilities they must meet whatever markets do. What they want from a hedge fund is not the highest return but a return that does not fall when everything else falls, so that it protects the rest of their portfolio. A fund that made 14% a year with one small losing year in thirty-six is worth more to them than a fund that made 20% a year with three years of minus 30% [S111][S379]. That preference is the whole demand side of this document: the platform sells consistency, and both mechanisms exist to produce it.

1.6 Two mechanisms, one sentence each

No house view

The centre does not decide what to buy. Hundreds of independent teams each decide for their own book, behind information barriers, and the firm's return is the sum of their independent bets.

Pass-through

The fund charges its operating costs (mostly the teams' pay, plus technology, data and infrastructure) directly to investors, on top of a performance fee, instead of covering them from a fixed management fee.

They answer different questions: no house view is about how the return is made; pass-through is about who pays for it. Part 4 shows why choosing the first almost forces the second.

Part 2

No house view: what it is, why, and what it does to everyone

The claim is simple: the centre picks no securities and holds no market view. The consequences are not. This part takes the idea from its exact meaning, through the maths that makes it pay, to what it does to a PM, an investor, the firm, its rivals, and the trust between the centre and the teams.

2.1 Precisely what "no house view" means

Millennium's own words are "Independent decisions, collaborative culture, united mission", and it tells prospective PMs they will "pursue independent investment strategies, managing the process and team while leveraging Millennium's platform and expertise" [S352] Confirmed. Three things follow, and each is a testable statement rather than a slogan.

  • The centre holds no positions of its own. There is no central book expressing the firm's opinion about rates, sectors or the market. The firm's exposure is the sum of what the pods hold, hedged at the centre where the sum drifts from neutral [S316][S351].
  • Each pod chooses its own strategy inside its mandate. A European industrials long-short team decides which industrials; a rates team decides which curve trades. Nobody above them says "we are bullish on Europe this quarter".
  • The pods do not talk to each other about positions. A former head of enterprise risk: "In a pod, you typically have a siloed group... they don't communicate with other pods... Their payout is a percentage of their P&L after costs" [S351] Confirmed. An outside commentator calls Millennium "the most siloed and least internally collaborative of multimanager platforms" [S343] Reported. The firm's own word "collaborative" on its site describes the environment and the support functions, never PMs sharing trade ideas [S352] Inferred.

"No house view" is about alpha. About risk, the centre has the strongest view in the industry. 2.3 Why: the maths that makes independence pay

Here is the whole argument in one formula, then in one picture. Take N pods with the same skill (the same Sharpe ratio, S) and the same average correlation with each other (ρ). The firm's Sharpe ratio is S multiplied by the square root of N divided by (1 + (N-1)ρ). If ρ is zero, the firm's Sharpe grows with the square root of N: ten independent pods give three times the Sharpe of one; a hundred give ten times [S347][S340]. If ρ is not zero, the growth stops: the multiple can never exceed one over the square root of ρ, however many pods you add. At ρ of 0.1 the ceiling is about 3.2 times; at 0.5 it is about 1.4 times.

@@f5@@
Figure 5. The diversification chart. The same formula that makes a platform want many pods makes it want them uncorrelated far more. Computed by the author from the standard result; the practitioner sources give the logic and the "uncorrelated bets" framing [S347][S340][S351].

Allocator research says the same thing with a sharper number: "Even a 10% correlation among PMs cuts overall Sharpe ratios by half once costs are considered", and "reducing correlation across risk takers is far more impactful than simply adding more PMs" [S520] Reported, allocator model.

Why a house view raises correlation

If the centre says "we like European banks this quarter", every pod that listens tilts the same way, and their returns start to move together. That is a rise in ρ, and Figure 5 shows what a rise in ρ does: it caps the benefit of every other pod in the firm. The barrier between pods is not a cultural preference; it is the mechanism that keeps ρ low so that 350 teams are worth more than one. Griffin's "same elevator" worry is the same maths seen from the other side [S340].

The maths also says something less comfortable. A former Millennium risk head puts it plainly: with "a large number of independent bets or quasi independent bets, you need to be able to scale your method to a large number of independent bets, which means that you are in some way a quant" [S351]. The centre of a no-house-view firm is a statistical machine for measuring, limiting and combining bets, not a room of investors. That is why more than half the firm is the centre, and why so much of it is technology and risk [S392][S382].

2.5 What it means for the PM: a franchise, not a job

A PM at a no-house-view platform is closer to a franchisee than an employee. They bring a strategy; the firm brings capital, financing, risk, technology, data, execution and services, and takes most of the profit. They are paid a share of their own P&L after their own costs, reported at 15 to 22% depending on the deal [S312][S351] Reported. They are sized up when they win and cut when they lose, by rule rather than by conversation. And they must earn back any loss before they are paid again: Millennium's own filing language, as quoted in the press, explains why "zombie" PMs exist, teams that are still on the platform but under water and unpaid until they recover [S305] Confirmed as quoted. Turnover is high by design: press estimates put PM turnover at 15 to 20% a year, with 130 hires and 50 departures in one year and three-year retention of about 55% [S340] Reported.

2.6 What it means for the investor

The investor is buying a return built from hundreds of small, hedged, independent bets, cut early when they go wrong. The record that results is the product: about 14% a year since 1989 with one losing year [S111][S119]; on third-party data to 2022, a Sharpe ratio of about 3.2 and a worst drawdown of about 7% [S378]. Allocator research describes the trade: investors "assume the netting risk of underperforming PMs, resulting in typically higher total expense ratios", in exchange for "alpha via diversification" [S379]. What they give up is any chance of a spectacular year; in 2025 three rivals beat Millennium, and in 2026 to August it was up 8.2% against a market up more [S126][S402]. What they get is that the losing years almost never come, which for a pension fund is worth the fee.

Say this

"The product is not the return; it is the shape of the return. No house view is what gives it that shape: hundreds of uncorrelated bets, none big enough to hurt, cut by rule when they go wrong. A pension fund buys that because it cannot afford the years a single-manager fund gives you."

2.7 What it means for the firm

For the firm, no house view turns talent into raw material. Because no strategy is central, any strategy that produces uncorrelated alpha can be added, and any that stops producing it can be removed, without changing the firm's identity [S104]. The number of teams grew from 265 in 2020 to over 350, with 160 PMs hired in a single year and 32 external managers running Millennium capital [S92][S71][S168][S355]. It also means the firm's revenue depends on a population, not a person: a founder can step back, a co-CIO can retire, an Office of the CIO can be created, and the return does not change, because the return never came from the top [S397][S96]. The cost is that the firm must be very good at the unglamorous things: measuring, limiting, hiring, cutting, and running the services that 350 teams cannot run for themselves. A former risk head's phrase for what the centre must be is "in some way a quant" [S351].

Part 3

Pass-through: what it is, why, and what it does to everyone

Pass-through is the money side of the same machine. It replaces the fixed management fee with the firm's actual costs, billed to the investor. This part gives the exact mechanics, the worked numbers, where the dollar goes, how Millennium's terms have moved, why the model exists, what it does to the PM, the investor, the firm and the rivals, and why it makes every central function accountable.

3.1 Precisely what pass-through means

Under the classic model (Figure 1), the manager charges a fixed percentage of assets and pays the firm's costs out of it. Under pass-through, the fund itself pays the firm's operating expenses, and those expenses are charged to investors on top of the performance fee [S104]. The expenses include the pods' compensation (the PM payouts in Figure 7), analysts' pay, data, technology, offices, and an eligible list that at the big platforms grew by nearly 40% over seven years [S370][S371] Confirmed; a Goldman Sachs prime-brokerage report described the charged costs as "bonuses, deferred compensation, travel, client entertainment, office rents" [S494]. Millennium's own regulatory filing carries the fingerprint: its Form ADV lists the adviser's compensation as "performance-based fees" and "other: reimbursement of expenses" [S529] Confirmed, primary; its brochure, as summarised by a third party, says the funds pay asset-based fees quarterly in advance and performance fees annually, without publishing rates [S528]. The total is usually expressed as a percentage of the fund's net asset value, and the reported range has drifted up: Barclays in 2023 said the structure looked "more like 7-and-20" than 2-and-20 [S505]; Hedgeweek in 2025 put top platforms at "up to 8% of assets annually" [S509]; Goldman Sachs data put multi-manager fees "as high as 10% of assets" against a typical hedge fund management fee of about 1.35% [S373]; and one analyst puts Millennium and Citadel "in the 10% range" with the industry average nearer 5% [S527] All reported or estimated. About 80% of multi-strategy firms use pass-through [S372].

Millennium adds a floor. Since summer 2023 investors pay whichever is greater of about 1% of assets a year or 20% of gains, so a fee is paid even in a flat or losing year; if the 1% is charged, it must be recovered before performance fees are charged the following year. The firm's letter described the change as reflecting "current industry-standard approaches" [S87] Reported from a Bloomberg-reported letter. Millennium's own fund-level expense ratio has not been disclosed; the best public estimate is Net Interest's, that after pass-through and performance charges "the investor gets about half of the return" [S104] Estimate.

Key terms

Net asset value (NAV): what the fund is worth after everything is paid. Expense ratio: the year's pass-through costs as a share of NAV. Gross return: what the trading made before any charge. Net return: what the investor actually receives. Fee floor: a minimum charge that applies even if performance is poor. Hurdle: a return the fund must beat before a performance fee is charged; a "cash hurdle" means beating the interest rate on cash [S166].

3.5 Why pass-through exists: the talent war, and the firm's own economics

Put Part 2 and Figure 7 together and the reason is almost forced. A no-house-view firm makes its money from hundreds of independent PMs. Each PM is paid a share of their own P&L, in the year they earn it, whether or not the firm as a whole made money. Under a fixed management fee, the firm would have to fund those payouts from a pool that does not grow when the fund is flat: in a mixed year, it could not pay the winners without losing money itself, and the winners would leave. Pass-through solves this by making the fund, and therefore the investor, pay each payout as it is earned [S104][S346]. The payout formula, as a careers site states it: "Pod P&L, minus pod costs, times PM's negotiated payout % = bonus pool", with the reminder that "the costs of running a team are not a firm subsidy" [S491]. A rival's own note gives the manager-side rationale for pass-through and locks together: "a more stable fee base, enabling firms to retain top talent and invest in infrastructure" [S522]. It also lets the platform hire without a budget ceiling: 160 PMs in one year, 32 external managers, a centre of more than 3,600 people [S168][S355][S392]. Englander's 2022 letter names the competitor: technology companies, for talent [S376].

The firm's own income is then two things: the performance fee (reported 20%) and its ownership of a fifth of the fund itself, since the adviser and related persons own about 18% of the master fund [S360]. That ownership matters for your argument: the firm eats a fifth of every pass-through dollar, which is the strongest alignment claim available, and a stronger one than "pass-through aligns incentives", which a sceptical allocator will not accept on its own [S366].

Say this

"Pass-through follows from no house view. If your return is the sum of three hundred independent teams paid on their own books, you have to be able to pay a winning team in a year the firm is flat, or it leaves. A fixed management fee can't do that; billing the costs to the fund can. The check on it is that the firm owns about a fifth of the fund, so it pays a fifth of its own bill."

3.6 What it means for the PM

For the PM, pass-through is the reason the franchise deal can exist at all. Their payout is a contractual share of their own net P&L, not a bonus from a firm-wide pool decided at year end [S351][S312]; the reported range is 10 to 20%, with top-quartile traders at about 24.5% of profits in 2025 by Goldman data [S491]. Millennium's own brochure, as quoted in the press, says a PM's salary "is generally treated as an advance against their profits interest" and that losses "are carried forward and past losses must be made up before performance-based compensation becomes payable" [S508] Confirmed as quoted. Their costs, the analysts, the data, the technology and the services they draw from the centre, are charged to their book before the payout is calculated, so a PM is in effect running a small business whose expenses are visible to them line by line [S351]. Two consequences follow for the desk. A PM who is charged for services will ask what they got; and a PM who is paid only on their own book has no reason to help another team get a meeting. Both are why the consumption report exists and why the desk's discretion between teams is absolute.

3.7 What it means for the investor: the bar, and the pushback

The investor's position is simple to state and hard to judge. They pay the pods' payouts and the centre's costs, plus a performance fee, plus a floor; they lock money for years; and press estimates say they keep about half of gross returns [S104][S87][S400]. In exchange they get the shape of return described in Part 2. The fair test, from the allocator side, is whether net outcomes "meaningfully exceed" a classic 2-and-20 fund "in mediocre and down years" [S366]. On the public record, they have: one losing year of about 3% since 1989, against an industry whose bad years run to double digits [S111][S119].

The cost of diversification has a name and a number. Albourne's Ronan Cosgrave: "When there are performance fees involved, diversification is no longer a free lunch. The cost to diversify is called netting risk"; Albourne's simulations put it at about 1% a year [S519][S495] Reported. And the down-year case is not theoretical: Eisler Capital wound down in 2025 with most of its 14.3% loss attributed to pass-through expenses [S510].

The pushback is also real and worth stating before an interviewer does. Investors kept 41 cents of each gross dollar at multi-strats in 2023, down from 54 in 2021 [S370]; a coalition of pensions demanded better terms in 2024 [S371]; ExodusPoint moved to a cash hurdle, with no performance fee unless returns beat three-month Treasury bills, made retroactive to the start of 2024 [S166][S493]; Citadel returned capital rather than grow [S165]; Brevan Howard cut its fees in 2025 after its all-in load rose [S522]; multi-strategy funds saw more than $30bn of net outflows in the year to June 2024 [S494]. A fund-services firm's 2026 summary: allocators "are not revolting against paying for performance" but "are objecting to writing a blank cheque", demanding expense caps and disclosure schedules [S518] Opinion piece. Millennium's answers were a floor rather than a cut, longer locks rather than shorter, and a raise that closed at $22bn with a five-year withdrawal restriction longer than peers' three to four [S87][S400]. Wealthy individuals are reported at 30 to 40% of assets [S400], a client base that has historically been less fee-sensitive than pensions Inferred.

Don't say

"Pass-through aligns the firm with investors" as a bare claim; an allocator hears the netting drag and the 41 cents. Say the alignment comes from ownership (18%) and from the record, and that the investor is right to watch the expense list. Do not quote a Millennium expense ratio; none is public.

3.10 What it means for relationships and trust

Pass-through changes who the centre answers to. Under a fixed fee, a service desk is a cost the firm chooses to bear and can hide inside its margin. Under pass-through, the desk is a line on the investor's bill, and the investor's consultants read the eligible-expense list [S370][S366]. That does three things to a corporate access desk. It must be able to show what it delivered, which is why the consumption report is a strategic document and not an admin one. It must be cheaper and better than 350 pods doing the work themselves, or its existence is a fee without a service. And it must treat the pods as clients who are, in the end, paying for it through their own book's charges, which is why the New York posting talks about monitoring "access quality, utilization, costs, and team feedback" [S25]. Your Round 1 observation that the admin is the substance is, in fee terms, the observation that the record is what the investor is paying for.

Say this

"Pass-through means the desk's cost lands on the investor's statement, not in the firm's margin. So the desk has to prove itself twice: to the PMs, who are charged for it and owe it nothing, and to the investors, whose consultants read the expense list. The consumption report is how it does both. I like that; it means the service has to be real."

4.3 Why the firm is structured like this, in one paragraph

Because the founder's insight in 1989 was that a firm could sell consistency rather than genius, and consistency at scale requires two things no single-manager fund can offer: many independent bets, and a way to pay for them that does not depend on the firm having a good year. The first is no house view; the second is pass-through. The pods exist because independent bets need independent owners. The centre exists because 350 independent owners need one measurer, one financier, one compliance function, and one desk that can see all of their demand for company meetings and turn it into one voice to the market.

5.1 Claim one in three lengths

Ten seconds

"No house view on what to buy; a strong house view on how much to risk. That's why the return is smooth."

Thirty seconds

"The centre doesn't pick stocks or hold a market view. Three hundred and fifty teams each run their own strategy behind information barriers, and the centre allocates capital, sets loss and exposure limits, and hedges the aggregate. So the firm's return is the average of hundreds of independent bets rather than one person's call, and that's what gives it one losing year since 1989. The cost is that teams don't share ideas and can still crowd into the same trade, which is what July showed."

Ninety seconds, if they ask why it matters for the desk

"Two things follow for a corporate access desk. First, the desk has no view either: it can't favour a sector or a PM, so every allocation has to be by a rule the losing team could accept. Second, because each team owns its own outcomes and is paid on its own book, teams guard their positions and their meeting requests; the desk is the only place that sees all of them, so its discretion is the condition of its existence. The maths behind independence is that the firm's Sharpe rises with the number of teams only if they stay uncorrelated; a house view would raise the correlation for everyone. So the barrier between pods isn't a cultural preference, it's the mechanism, and the desk works inside it."

5.2 Claim two in three lengths

Ten seconds

"Pass-through funds the platform: most of it talent, some of it systems, all of it paid by the investor, which is why every central function has to earn its place."

Thirty seconds

"Instead of a fixed management fee, the fund pays the firm's operating costs directly, on top of a performance fee, with a floor of about one per cent or twenty per cent of gains since 2023. That's what lets the firm pay a winning team in a flat year and run a centre that's more than half the firm, including eighteen hundred technologists and the AI work. The investor pays for it and keeps about half of gross by press estimates, so it only holds while the down years stay shallow."

Ninety seconds, if they ask whether investors are right to push back

"They're right to watch it. Across the industry investors kept 41 cents of each gross dollar in 2023, down from 54 two years earlier, and the eligible-expense list has grown. Pensions have organised, one rival added a cash hurdle, another returned capital. The alignment I'd point to isn't the fee itself but that the firm owns about a fifth of the fund, so it pays a fifth of its own bill. ProbeAnswer
"If there's no house view, what does the Office of the CIO do?""Allocation, risk oversight and strategy across the pods: how much capital goes where, and which strategies the firm wants more of. Part 6

Glossary

Every term used above, in plain English, in the order you are likely to need it.

Hedge fund
A private fund for institutions and wealthy individuals that can bet on prices falling as well as rising and can borrow to enlarge its bets.
Management fee
A fixed annual charge, a percentage of assets, paid whatever the result; the classic way a manager covers its costs.
Performance fee
A share of profits, classically 20%.
Two and twenty
The classic fee pair: 2% management, 20% performance. Averages are now lower.
Pass-through
The fund pays the firm's operating costs directly and bills investors, on top of the performance fee.
Expense ratio, NAV
A year's pass-through costs as a share of the fund's net asset value, what it is worth after everything is paid.
Fee floor
A minimum charge paid even in a poor year; at Millennium, the greater of about 1% of assets or 20% of gains since 2023.
Hurdle, cash hurdle
A return the fund must beat before the performance fee applies; a cash hurdle means beating the interest rate on cash.
Gross return, net return
What the trading made before any charge; what the investor actually receives.
Lock-up
A period during which an investor cannot take money out; five years on the 2026 raise. A callable commitment is money promised and drawn down over time.
Portfolio manager (PM)
The person who decides what a book buys and sells.
Pod, team, franchise
One PM and a few analysts running their own strategy on a slice of capital under the centre's limits, paid on their own results.
House view
A belief about markets held at the top of a firm and imposed on the books beneath it. Millennium has none on securities; it has a strong one on risk.
The centre
Everyone who is not in a pod: risk, technology, treasury, compliance, operations, service desks. About 54% of Millennium's staff.
Office of the CIO
Millennium's body, since 2022, overseeing allocation, risk and strategy across pods; not a source of market views.
Alpha, beta
Return from skill; return from the market moving. Pods are paid for alpha and required to carry little beta.
Correlation (ρ)
How much two return streams move together, from -1 to +1. The value of many pods depends on it being near zero.
Sharpe ratio
Return above cash divided by its volatility; a third-party estimate for the flagship to 2022 was about 3.2.
Drawdown
A fall from peak to trough. Reported pod limits: capital halved at 5%, closed at about 7.5%.
Factor cage, beta-neutral
The centre's rules that stop a pod's return being a bet on the market or on a style such as value or momentum.
Netting risk
Investors pay winning pods' payouts while losing pods offset the gains; a flat year still costs money.
Loss carry-forward
A PM who loses must earn the loss back before being paid again; the reason "zombie" PMs exist.
Payout
The PM's contractual share of their own net P&L, reported at 15 to 22%.
Information barrier
A rule that stops information moving between pods. Crowding is what still happens when independent teams see the same evidence.
