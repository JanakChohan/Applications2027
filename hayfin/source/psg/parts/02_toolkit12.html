Fund maths, the way an LP reads it

Cash flows, IRR, MOIC, DPI, TVPI, the J-curve, subscription lines, fees, the waterfall and currency, all worked through

Everything here uses one illustrative fund so the numbers connect. It is not a Hayfin fund. An LP commits €100m. Hayfin calls €90m over three years and pays back €116m over six.

Cash flows, IRR and MOIC

Year: 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 Capital called: -30 -40 -20 0 0 0 0 Distributions: 0 +3 +8 +25 +30 +30 +20 Net cash flow: -30 -37 -12 +25 +30 +30 +20 MOIC = total returned / total paid in = 116 / 90 = 1.29x IRR = the rate r where -30 - 37/(1+r) - 12/(1+r)^2 + 25/(1+r)^3 + 30/(1+r)^4 + 30/(1+r)^5 + 20/(1+r)^6 = 0 → r ≈ 8.1% In Excel: =IRR(range) for annual flows, or =XIRR(values, dates) for real dates.
Why both numbersIRR rewards speed: getting money back sooner lifts it. MOIC ignores time: it only counts how many euros came back per euro in. A fund can have a high IRR and a low multiple (quick but small gains) or the reverse. LPs look at both, which is why ILPA's new Performance Template asks for IRR and multiples together [S45].

DPI, TVPI and the J-curve

Why this matters in 2026. When exits slow, LPs focus on DPI, the cash actually returned. A credit fund's steady interest and repayments are a DPI story. Hayfin's DLF V was over 50% deployed across 35+ companies at close [S71], so its DPI clock is already running.

The subscription line trick (and why ILPA cares)

Same fund, but it borrows from a bank for one year before calling capital (a "subscription line"), paying 5% interest, so each call arrives a year later and 5% larger: Y0 Y1 Y2 Y3 Y4 Y5 Y6 Net cash flow: 0 -28.5 -34.0 +4.0 +30 +30 +20 IRR ≈ 9.6% (was 8.1%) → looks better, because the LP's money is out for less time MOIC ≈ 1.23x (was 1.29x) → is actually worse, because the fund paid interest

ILPA's own notional example shows the same effect: IRR 6.62% and TVPI 1.45x with no line; 7.14% and 1.40x with a one-year line; 7.98% and 1.35x with a two-year line [S437]. ILPA's 2017 guidance suggested lines of 15-25% of uncalled capital outstanding for no more than 180 days [S437].

This is why the ILPA Performance Template asks managers to show performance with and without the effect of subscription facilities [S45]. When you proofread a performance page, check which basis each IRR uses. An intern who can explain this example will stand out.

Fees and the waterfall

Management fee. Hayfin's terms are not public. European direct lending norm (Macfarlanes, Hayfin's fund counsel): "a management fee on net invested capital, a performance fee subject to a preferred return with catch-up", headline about 1%, slightly less after discounts; "A 2% management fee would be seen as commercially unviable within direct lending" [S104]. Charging on invested rather than committed capital means LPs do not pay fees on money sitting idle [Inferred]. In Macfarlanes' model, cutting the fee from 1% to 0.75% lifts net IRR to 9.05%, and "all of the performance fee" arrives in the last two years [S440]. A survey of mostly US lenders (Cliffwater 2025, relayed by a blog, so treat with care) put unlevered direct lending at about 1.04% fees, an average hurdle of 5.12% and average carry of 11.2% [S441]: far below private equity's typical 2% and 20% [Inferred].

Illustrative "whole-fund" waterfall (NOT Hayfin's terms). Fund returns €120m on €90m paid in. Assume a preferred return worth €15m, a 100% catch-up and a 15% performance fee (within the 10-15% range seen in direct lending [S441]). Step 1 Return of capital LP gets 90.0 (remaining profit 30.0) Step 2 Preferred return (hurdle) LP gets 15.0 (remaining 15.0) Step 3 Catch-up to GP GP gets c, where c = 15% × (15 + c) → c = 2.65 Step 4 Split the rest 85/15 remaining 12.35 → LP 10.50, GP 1.85 Totals: LP profit 25.5, GP profit 4.5 → GP has exactly 15% of the 30.0 profit.
Gross-to-net in one lineGross return (what the loans earned) minus management fee, minus fund expenses, minus the performance fee, minus the cost of any fund borrowing, equals net return to the LP. ILPA's 2025 Reporting Template adds a standard gross-to-net fee reconciliation, including rebates, waivers, offsets, placement fees and internal chargebacks [S170][S258].

The loan underneath

How a direct loan is priced, how its health is measured, and what PIK really does

You cannot explain a credit fund without understanding a single loan. Hayfin's quarterly Portfolio Breakdown shows each company's "LTM EBITDA, enterprise value and leverage" [S305]. Here is how to read those.

Pricing a unitranche loan

Illustrative €100m unitranche, 3-year expected life (NOT a Hayfin deal): Base rate: 3-month Euribor, June 2026 [S443] 2.32% Margin over Euribor (market range E+500-625bps) + 5.25% Cash coupon = 7.57% a year Issue price 98 (a 2-point OID; market 97.5-98.5), spread over a 3-year expected life + 0.67% a year (approx.) Approximate all-in yield to the lender ≈ 8.2% a year (gross) Floating rate: if Euribor rises 1 point, the coupon rises 1 point. Good for the lender's income; harder for the borrower to pay.

Where the market is (2025-2026). European direct lending median spreads fell from 613bps (2023) to 550bps (2024) to 500bps (2025 YTD), while sponsored leverage averaged 6.12x EBITDA against 4.83x in syndicated loans (Rothschild) [S445]. Q1 2026 unitranche priced at about E+525-625bps in the traditional mid-market and E+500-600bps upper mid-market (Baird) [S442]; KBRA said euro loans were "flirting with sub-8% yields for the first time" [S444]. The ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.25% in June 2026, its first hike in almost three years [S443]. Call protection is thin: over 80% of European deals had none from year three (2022 data) [S446].

This is why Hayfin was described by its LP as "an 8%+ lender" in direct lending and "15%+" in special opportunities [S307]: the label is best read as the rough all-in yield range of each product [Inferred].

Measuring a borrower's health

Illustrative borrower: LTM EBITDA €50m, total debt €250m, enterprise value (EV) €550m (5.0x is below the 6.12x 2025 average for sponsored European direct lending [S445]) Leverage = debt / EBITDA = 250 / 50 = 5.0x Loan-to-value (LTV)= debt / EV = 250 / 550 = 45% Interest cost = 250 × 7.57% = €18.9m Interest cover = EBITDA / interest = 50 / 18.9 = 2.6x (syndicated loans averaged 2.71x in Q2 2026, vs 4.0x before 2022 [S443]) Equity cushion = EV − debt = €300m (what the PE owner loses first)
Read the Portfolio Breakdown like a credit analystRising leverage or falling interest cover across several quarters is a warning. A big equity cushion below the loan is protection: the private equity owner loses first. A covenant (for example "leverage must stay under 6.0x") gives the lender a seat at the table before things get bad. Hayfin says European deals offer "lower leverage, tighter documentation and smaller lender groups" than US ones [S95] (firm claim).

PIK: the topic of 2026

PIK (payment in kind) = interest added to the loan instead of paid in cash. €100m loan, 4% PIK for 3 years: 100 × 1.04^3 = €112.5m owed Interest earned on paper: €12.5m. Cash received: €0. If the company's value falls, the extra €12.5m may never be collected.
Hayfin's three PIKs [S229]What it meansHow you would report it
Good PIKAgreed at the start, capped, time-limited, priced at a premium, used for growth that pays it back.Show it as planned, with the cap and the coverage.
Bad PIKIntroduced later because the borrower cannot pay cash: a stress signal. Lincoln calls loans that switched to PIK a "shadow default" (6.2% of loans, Q2 2026) [S162].Flag as an amendment; track on the watchlist.
Fake good PIKAgreed at entry, but the business never really covers it, so value slowly moves from lender to borrower.Hayfin's point: origin "says nothing about whether the position stays covered" [S229]. Track coverage, not just origin.

Why it is in your job. Fitch counted interest deferrals and PIK switches as 47% of US private credit default events in August 2026 [S245], and Hayfin's LPs probed PIK harder in DLF V than ever before [S222]. Expect ad hoc requests like "PIK income as a share of total income, split by planned and amended", and DDQ questions on PIK policy.

From one loan to the whole fund

Weighted average, the most used formula in IRWeighted average leverage = Σ (loan size × leverage) ÷ Σ loan size. Two loans: €60m at 5.0x and €40m at 4.0x → (60×5 + 40×4) ÷ 100 = 4.6x. A simple average would wrongly say 4.5x. Always ask "weighted by what?" (commitment, drawn amount, fair value).
