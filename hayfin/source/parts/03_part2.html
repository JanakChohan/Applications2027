Part 2

Hayfin as a business, from the inside

Founding insight, how the money flows, the contracts, the risk architecture, ownership, and the last 24 months
Key terms for this partPrivate credit: loans made by investment funds rather than banks, negotiated directly with the borrower and usually held until repaid. Asset manager: a firm that invests other people's money for a fee. LP (limited partner): an investor in a fund. GP (general partner): the manager that runs the fund, here Hayfin. AUM (assets under management): how much money the manager looks after. Fund vintage: a fund raised in a given period; DLF V is the fifth vintage of the Direct Lending fund.

A. The founding insight

Who. Hayfin Capital Management LLP was registered in England on 11 June 2009 (LLP number OC346320) [S86][S87]. It began as Haymarket Financial LLP and took the name Hayfin in 2013 by joining the first three letters of each word [S79][S82]. The founders are Tim Flynn and Mark Tognolini [S79]. [Confirmed]

What they did before. Flynn was a partner at Goldman Sachs and co-head of European leveraged finance and acquisition finance. Before that he was a corporate lawyer at Sullivan & Cromwell in New York [S282]. In a 2024 podcast he said his first job was as a beekeeper on a CARE Canada project in Nicaragua [S105]. Tognolini was a vice president in Goldman's leveraged finance, UK M&A and private finance teams, after legal roles at Allen & Overy and Gadens in Australia [S283]. [Confirmed]

The thesis. Flynn's own account is precise. Bank capital rules (he cites Basel II risk weighting) would force banks to reprice lending to mid-sized and sub-investment-grade companies, and that "could create a window to build" a European market leader [S105]. The crisis came while he was on garden leave, "which made capital formation certainly harder" [S105]. Hayfin's history page puts it as a funding gap left when banks pulled back from mid-market European companies after the financial crisis [S79]. [Reported]

The design choice that followed. Hayfin did not start as a fund manager. A 2016 Pennsylvania public schools pension (PSERS) memo says the original backers, TowerBrook, OMERS, PSP Investments and Australia's Future Fund, "capitalized Hayfin's management company by providing cash to form a balance sheet to invest in private credit" [S307]. So Hayfin began as a lender using its own balance sheet. It moved to managing third-party funds later, after a shareholder transaction made the team the largest shareholder [S307]. [Confirmed, primary LP document]

One reading [Inferred]: the firm's habits were formed by lending its own owners' money, which rewards caution and workout skill more than asset gathering. You can test that reading against the 2026 language: "Scale should be an outcome of repeatability, rather than an objective in itself" [S222]. Decide whether you believe it, and why.

Why breadth. Flynn explains that Europe is "very, very shallow and inefficient", so "We have to run extremely broad mandates compared to what we would do in the U.S." In the US "we might run a very focused, cash flow only, sponsor-only business" [S324]. That one sentence explains most of Hayfin's shape. [Confirmed]

Mission in its own words. The homepage calls Hayfin "a leading European alternative asset management platform, providing critical debt, equity and hybrid capital" [S127]. The JD pack says: "Our mission is to deliver consistent, superior, risk-adjusted returns to our investors" through "integrity, discipline, and creativity" [JD].

B. How Hayfin makes money

An asset manager like Hayfin makes money in two ways. First, a management fee: a percentage of the money it manages, paid every year whatever happens. Second, a performance fee or carried interest: a share of the profit above an agreed minimum return (the hurdle or preferred return), paid only if the fund does well. Hayfin does not publish its fund terms [Gap]. The best public guide is a November 2024 note by Macfarlanes, Hayfin's own fund counsel, on European direct lending norms [S104]:

  • A typical unlevered direct lending fund charges "a management fee on net invested capital, a performance fee subject to a preferred return with catch-up". The headline management fee is about 1%, with the effective average slightly lower after discounts for early, large and loyal investors [S104]. [Reported, industry norm, not Hayfin's terms]
  • "A 2% management fee would be seen as commercially unviable within direct lending" [S104].
  • Over a fund's life, manager income splits roughly 50/50 between management and performance fees [S104].
  • The one listed peer that publishes a figure, Tikehau, reported a credit fee rate of 75 basis points (0.75%) for the twelve months to June 2026 [S141]. [Confirmed]

D. The risk and control architecture

A credit manager's product is the absence of losses. So the controls are part of what investors buy. Hayfin's Stewardship Code report (October 2025) describes them in unusual detail [S305].

Named owners of risk. Andrew McCullagh is Chief Risk Officer and a member of the LLP since March 2010; Lara Dressler is Chief Compliance Officer; David Rushford is General Counsel; Kevin Sarafilovic is CFO (from 2025) [S303]. Carlos Pla, with Hayfin since 2009, is Head of Portfolio Management [S76][S277]. Michaela Campbell joined in April 2024 as MD to oversee private credit portfolio monitoring and risk [S70]. [Confirmed]

Why this produces the characteristic result. A direct lender wins by not losing. Three mechanisms do the work [Inferred, from Confirmed pieces]: (1) a wide funnel, so the firm can say no often (in 2012 Hayfin saw over 1,400 opportunities and made 60 investments, about 4% [S306]); (2) senior secured positions with covenants, so problems surface early; (3) an in-house legal and workout team that has run restructurings in each European creditor regime [S307]. Flynn says workout capability is "the number one theme in the LP community" [S281].

Source uncertainHayfin publishes no current loss rate. The only public statement found is dated: "Since inception through September 30, 2015, Hayfin has not incurred any realized losses as a result of an event of default by a borrower" across Special Opportunities and Direct Lending [S307]. Do not repeat this as current. If asked, say what is public and what is not.

E. Strategy mix and growth

What grew [Confirmed figures, Inferred reading]:

  • Direct lending is the engine. Fund sizes went about €2bn, then over €3.5bn, €5bn, €6bn and now €15bn [S79][S90][S88][S71]. Hayfin invested a record €7.1bn in direct lending in the twelve months before DLF V closed, and €38bn across 350+ investments since inception [S71]. A Finance job post already refers to "DLF VI to follow" [S333].
  • Opportunistic credit is steady at about €5bn (Special Opportunities plus Tactical Solutions) [S305]. Hayfin hired a London product strategy MD for it in September 2025 [S272].
  • Liquid credit is being refocused on Europe. In May 2026 Hayfin raised new equity for future European CLOs (now €5.8bn across 14 deals) and hired Greensledge to "explore options" for its €1.4bn US CLO business [S225].
  • Specialist verticals (healthcare, maritime, real estate, PE secondaries) are small in AUM but give capability and story. Maritime has deployed over $4bn across 100+ vessels [S227].
  • Distribution geography widened. Dubai (2024), Chicago (2024), a Dallas MD (2025), Melbourne coverage (2025), Seoul (2026) [S275][S97][S99][S272][S223].

What "private" means for research. Hayfin has no listed shares, so there are no earnings calls or annual reports with group numbers. I. Skeletons in the cupboard, told straight

Nothing serious turned up. Know these so nothing surprises you, and do not raise them unprompted.

  • Regulatory record. All three SEC-filing Hayfin advisers show no disciplinary disclosures [S82]. The FCA register page would not load for this research, so the UK record could not be checked directly [Gap]. The UK LLP's FCA reference is 503730, authorised 23 June 2010 [S87].
  • Kingsland litigation. The founder of Kingsland, the US CLO manager Hayfin bought in 2017, sued Hayfin in New York over the purchase agreement. The case was dismissed with prejudice on 7 May 2021 [S110]. That same US CLO business is now under strategic review [S225]. [Confirmed]
  • A sale that did not happen. In September 2023 Bloomberg reported Hayfin was considering options including a sale; DWS and Janus Henderson were named [S100]. No outside sale happened; the management buyout followed. [Reported]
  • Portfolio stress. Hayfin was among the lenders (with Pemberton and Blue Owl) that took ownership of Cinven-backed Netceed through a debt-for-equity swap in October 2025 [S109]. Write-down size not disclosed. [Reported]
  • Softer UK entity numbers. FY2025 revenue at the UK LLP fell 10%, profit before members' remuneration fell 25%, and performance fees fell from £8.6m to £3.2m [S87]. Entity-level only. [Confirmed]
  • Old returns below target. The 2012 Special Opportunities fund showed 8.1% net IRR against an 11-15% target at September 2015; for a US dollar investor that was minus 2.6% because the euro fell [S307]. Early-life IRRs are often depressed by fees. [Confirmed]
  • People moves. The former Global Head of Partner Solutions, Alex Wolfman, no longer appears on the site and is labelled "former" by a third-party directory [S296]. No departure announcement was found [Inferred].
Don't say thisDo not ask "Why did the head of Partner Solutions leave?" or "Why did revenue fall?" in an intern interview. If the interviewer raises a weakness, acknowledge it calmly and move to what you would want to understand. That is the investor relations skill they are testing.
