Part 5

Private credit from zero

What it is, who borrows, who invests, how big it is, what is changing, and three views of the next five years

What private credit is

A company that wants to borrow has three broad options. It can borrow from a bank. It can sell bonds or a syndicated loan to many investors through a bank, which are then traded. Or it can borrow directly from a private credit fund, which negotiates the loan one to one and usually holds it until it is repaid. Private credit means the third option [Inferred, standard definition].

Key termsDirect lending: a fund lends directly to a mid-sized company, usually PE-owned, often to pay for a buyout; floating rate, senior secured, 5 to 7 years. Senior secured: first claim on the company's assets. Unitranche: one loan blending senior and junior debt. Mezzanine: junior debt between senior and equity. Special situations / opportunities: flexible capital for complex or stressed cases. Distressed: buying debt of companies near default at a discount. Asset-based finance (ABF): loans secured on pools of assets, not company cash flow. NAV lending: loans to a PE fund secured on its portfolio. CLO: a vehicle that buys syndicated loans and funds itself with rated slices of debt. High yield: traded bonds rated below investment grade. Floating rate: interest that moves with a base rate such as Euribor. Covenant: a promise by the borrower, such as keeping leverage below a level. PIK (payment in kind): interest added to the loan instead of paid in cash.

Who borrows, who invests, and how it differs from neighbours

Borrowers. Mostly PE-owned mid-market companies. The IMF said private credit borrowers "are often smaller and more indebted" than bond or syndicated loan issuers [S150]. The FSB said in May 2026 that borrowers "typically have lower credit quality and higher leverage", concentrated in technology, healthcare and services [S178]. [Confirmed]

Investors. Pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, endowments, family offices, funds of funds and, increasingly, wealthy individuals through wealth platforms. The Alternative Credit Council's 2025 survey found institutions supplied 76% of commitments and retail about 24% [S182]. In the euro area, insurers hold about €211bn of private credit exposure, pension funds about €52bn, banks €62.5bn (ECB, May 2026) [S177]. [Confirmed for ECB]

Compared withThe key difference
BanksBanks fund loans with deposits and must hold regulatory capital; private credit funds use investors' money locked up for years, so there is less mismatch between short-term funding and long-term loans. AIFMD II now bans EU loan-origination funds whose strategy is "originate-to-distribute" [S163]. [Confirmed for AIFMD II; rest Inferred]
Private equityPE buys equity for capital gain. Private credit lends for contractual interest with seniority and covenants. PE firms are private credit's main customers [Inferred].
Hedge fundsUsually trade liquid securities with frequent redemption; private credit is illiquid, closed-end, hold to maturity. They overlap in distressed and special situations [Inferred].
Traditional asset managersLong-only bond managers buy public bonds. Private credit managers originate, structure, monitor and work out loans, which needs deal and restructuring teams [Inferred].

Banks and funds are increasingly linked, not just rivals. The FSB counts about $220bn of bank credit lines to private credit funds; commercial estimates run $270bn to $500bn [S178]. In May 2026 Citi and HPS launched a €15bn European programme in which Citi sources deals and HPS provides capital [S255].

How big it is, and why estimates differ

Say this in the interview"It depends on the definition. The IMF said about $2.1 trillion at end-2023; the industry body says $3.5 trillion including more vehicles; Apollo argues the addressable market is $40 trillion because it counts most bank lending. Hayfin plays in the classic slice: sponsor-backed European mid-market loans and special situations."

Defaults: three measures that tell three stories

Returns. PitchBook: rolling one-year private debt IRRs "fell to 3.9%" through H1 2025, the weakest since 2020 [S152]. By Q2 2026 "headline returns have compressed into the mid-single digits", and "the metric most relevant to LPs is not absolute return, but the excess spread that managers can generate over public fixed-income products" [S151]. [Confirmed]

The forces reshaping the sector now

  • The 2026 retail redemption wave. In Q1 2026 investors asked to redeem 21.9% of Blue Owl Credit Income Corp and 40.7% of Blue Owl Technology Income Corp; both capped payouts at 5%. Apollo Debt Solutions saw 11.2%, Ares Strategic Income about 11.6%, HPS Corporate Lending 9.3%, Blackstone's BCRED 7.9% (it raised its cap to 7%) [S184]. In Q2 about 10% of BCRED asked to leave and 5% was paid [S243] (Bloomberg headline, not read in full). Mohamed El-Erian asked whether it was a "canary-in-the-coalmine moment, similar to August 2007?" [S184]. [Reported]
  • Fundraising: more money, fewer funds, longer waits. Preqin data via Paul Weiss: $132.5bn raised in H1 2026 from only 80 funds, the lowest count in five years; top 10 funds took 64%; average time to close 39 months, up from 26 in 2024 [S153]. PitchBook: in 2025, 93% of private debt capital went to managers raising fund four or later; "Private debt fundraising has fully entered its era of manager concentration" [S151][S152]. [Reported / Confirmed]
  • Europe in favour. Europe took 35.9% of global private debt capital in 2025, helped by US investors diversifying after the April 2025 tariffs [S152]. European direct lending hit a half-year record of €63.16bn in H1 2026 (Debtwire) [S186]. LPs in the 2026 Intralinks survey prefer UK and Europe (54%) over North America (50%) [S189].
  • AI. Private credit loans to software firms rose from about $8bn in 2015 to over $500bn by end-2025, about 19% of direct loans; software stocks fell almost 30% between October 2025 and February 2026 [S242]. Hayfin says software is under 6% of its DL book [S227].
  • Valuations. The FCA's March 2025 review of 36 firms found valuation conflicts only partly identified and praised "value bridge" reporting [S166]. The SEC issued a staff statement on private credit valuations on 28 September 2026 [S250]. The Bank of England's private markets stress scenario reports early 2027 [S175].
  • Banks compete and partner. The US Fed, FDIC and OCC re-proposed Basel III capital rules on 19 March 2026, estimating up to $441bn of extra lending capacity [S180]. Final status not confirmed [Gap].

Three competing views of the next three to five years

View 1 · Structural bullWho: Marc Rowan (Apollo), Jon Gray (Blackstone). Claim: private credit is a $40tn opportunity; "The vast, vast majority of private credit is investment grade" [S179]; Gray cites "tremendous structural tailwinds" [S199]. Logic: banks keep shrinking risk, insurers need long assets, retail wants yield.
View 2 · Cyclical bearWho: Jamie Dimon ("when you see one cockroach, there are probably more", 14 Oct 2025) [S157]; El-Erian [S184]; Fitch's record default series [S245]; IMF and FSB warnings [S150][S178]. Logic: weaker borrowers, defaults hidden as PIK, model-based marks, and evergreen funds promising liquidity on illiquid loans. 2026 gating is the evidence.
View 3 · Watched, not systemicWho: ECB (May 2026): "unlikely that private credit in isolation could be a source of systemic financial instability at present" [S177]; Cambridge Associates on First Brands and Tricolor: "idiosyncratic, driven by fraud" [S158]; PitchBook: performance will "depend more on manager selectivity than on macro tailwinds" [S152]. Logic: losses will be manager-specific; good underwriters and workout teams win.

The LP middle ground. Coller Capital's Summer 2026 barometer: only 29% of LPs plan to grow private credit target allocations, down from 42% six months earlier, but 87% will maintain or grow exposure; 53% see "isolated pockets of elevated risk", 18% a systemic problem [S183]. [Reported]
