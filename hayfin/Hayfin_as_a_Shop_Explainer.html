Section 0

Before you start: ten things to assume

The background facts that make everything else make sense

Most confusion about firms like Hayfin comes from carrying assumptions over from ordinary shops. Read these ten first.

#AssumptionWhat it means in plain English
1Hayfin does not mainly lend its own money.It lends other people's money and charges a fee for doing it well. The "other people" are big institutions such as pension funds. Hayfin is the shopkeeper, not the owner of the stock. (It started in 2009 by lending its backers' money from its own balance sheet, then switched to running funds [S307].)
2There are two kinds of customer.Investors give Hayfin money to lend. Borrowers (mostly mid-sized European companies owned by private equity firms) take the loans. You are applying to the team that serves the investors, so in this document "customer" means investor unless it says otherwise.
3The customer is an institution, not a person."All of our investors are institutional" [S305]. Hayfin's private credit takes "no retail capital" [S227]. Nobody walks in off the street with £1,000; the usual minimum is about $11.8m (roughly €10m) [S303].
4The "product" is a stream of loan repayments.When an investor buys into a Hayfin fund, it is buying a share of hundreds of millions of euros of loans. The return is mostly interest the borrowers pay, not a share price going up.
5You cannot return the product.Money is locked in for years. Funds invest for three to four years, then collect repayments for another three to four, with two possible one-year extensions [S305]. Investors "are not entitled to have their interests in the respective Fund redeemed" [S301]. Think of it as a ten-year subscription you cannot cancel.
6Customers pay over time, not at the till.An investor commits an amount, and Hayfin calls it in chunks as loans are made. So "raising €15bn" means €15bn of promises, drawn over years.
7The shop opens a new "edition" every few years.Each fund is raised once and then closed to new money. The next one is a new edition: Direct Lending Fund I, II, III, IV, V [S79][S90][S88][S71]. Customers decide each time whether to buy the new edition. That decision is called a re-up.
8Buying is slow and heavily checked.Before committing, an investor sends long questionnaires (DDQs), often hires a consultant to vet the manager, and gets committee approval. One vendor estimates six to eighteen months for the process [S202] (low-weight source). Your future team does much of this paperwork.
9Some numbers are private.Hayfin is privately owned, so fees, recent fund returns and loss rates are not public. Where this document says "not public", that is the honest answer, and it is the right one to give in an interview too.
10Dates matter.Figures change fast. Assets under management (AUM) were about €33bn in March 2025 [S305] and about €40bn by mid-2026 [S224]. Always say the date with the number.
Five words you needFund: a pot of investors' money run by a manager for a fixed purpose and period. LP (limited partner): an investor in a fund (the customer). GP (general partner): the manager (Hayfin). Private credit: loans made by funds rather than banks, agreed one to one and held until repaid. Partner Solutions Group (PSG): Hayfin's investor-facing team, the shop front.

The shop at a glance

What kind of shop Hayfin is, and how the analogy maps onto the real firm
The analogy in one paragraphImagine a specialist wholesale food importer based in London that supplies hospitals, schools and airlines across the world. It does not run a supermarket for the public. Its buyers are professional procurement teams who need steady, reliable supply for years and who inspect everything. The importer's edge is that it has local buyers in every European market who know which farms are sound, it has a range from everyday staples to premium specialist goods, it packs the same produce in different ways for different customers, and when a farm has a bad harvest it sends its own team to fix the problem. That is Hayfin.

The target audience

Who the customers are, what each type needs, and what each one specifically wants from Hayfin
Shop analogyA wholesale importer does not market to "everyone". It markets to hospital caterers, airline kitchens and school canteens, and it knows each one's menu, budget, rules and calendar. Hayfin is the same. It markets to a short list of very large professional buyers, and each type buys for a different reason.

Below, each customer type gets the same five questions: who they are, what is on their "shopping list", what hurts, what they buy at Hayfin, and what they specifically like about Hayfin. Where a reason is the author's reading rather than a stated fact, it is tagged.

Customer 1 · Pension funds (51.1% of AUM)the anchor customer

Who they are. Funds that pay retirement income to workers, for decades. Public ones (state and city employees, teachers) and corporate ones. Public examples that have committed to Hayfin: Pennsylvania's public school pension, PSERS (€200m in 2012, up to $200m in 2016) [S306][S307]; Nashville's employee pension ($60m in 2013) [S309]; Vermont ($50m to the Tactical Solutions Evergreen Fund, reported August 2026) [S322].

Shop analogyThe hospital caterer: it must feed patients every day for the next 30 years, so it values steady, predictable supply over a one-off bargain.
Shopping listSteady income to meet pension payments; returns above what government bonds pay; diversification away from shares; a long time horizon that can live with money locked up.
Pain pointsPromises to retirees are fixed, but markets move. Running dozens of managers is a big workload. Boards and trustees need simple, defensible reasons. Non-euro pensions face currency swings: PSERS's first Hayfin fund made 8.0% a year in euros but -2.6% in dollars by 2015 because the euro fell [S307].
What they buyMostly the core Direct Lending fund; some take Special Opportunities or Tactical Solutions [S306][S307][S322]. Some hedge the currency: Nashville voted to "execute a Euro currency hedge" alongside its commitment [S309].
What they like about Hayfin(1) Senior, secured loans mostly paid in cash: the "staple" product [S71]. (2) The PSERS memo's point that Hayfin's dual role as an "8%+ and 15%+ lender" widens its deal flow [S307]. (3) Line-by-line quarterly reporting that a trustee board can read [S305]. (4) A seat on the fund's Advisory Board for selected investors [S305]. [Inferred: which of these each pension values most is not public.]
Customer 2 · Insurers (19.5% of AUM)the regulated customer

Who they are. Life insurers and other insurers that hold large investment portfolios to pay future claims. Two insurer-linked groups own minority stakes in Hayfin: Samsung Life (Korea) and AXA IM Prime [S75][S74]. Hayfin's Fund Controller job supports "Solvency II reporting" [S333], the EU insurance capital rules.

Shop analogyThe airline kitchen: strict regulations on what it may serve and how it must be packed. It will only buy produce that comes in approved, certified packaging.
Shopping listLong-dated assets that match long-dated promises; extra yield over bonds; but in a form that does not eat up too much regulatory capital.
Pain pointsCapital rules make some investments "expensive" to hold even if they are safe. A plain fund stake can be capital-heavy Inferred. Insurers need detailed data for their regulators.
What they buyDirect Lending exposure through a rated note feeder: a vehicle that issues rated bonds backed by the fund, which about $600m of DLF V came through [S237]. The PSG co-heads describe it as "giving insurers a more capital-efficient route into the strategy without changing the underlying investment" [S222].
What they like about HayfinThe same loans, re-packaged for their rulebook. Hayfin claims its feeder was "one of the first priced by a European direct lending manager" [S222] (Hayfin's own claim). Ownership links (Samsung Life, AXA IM Prime) and a Seoul office [S223] show Hayfin courting insurers directly Inferred.
Customer 3 · Sovereign wealth funds (12.1% of AUM)the very large, very patient customer

Who they are. State-owned investment funds that invest a country's savings, often for generations. Australia's Future Fund was one of Hayfin's founding backers [S307]. Mubadala (Abu Dhabi) is a minority shareholder [S74]; whether it is also a fund investor is not public. DLF V drew 10% of its capital from the Middle East [S222].

Shop analogyThe national catering contractor: buys in bulk, wants its own custom mix, and visits the warehouse in person.
Shopping listVery large tickets, often through their own account rather than a shared fund; diversification across regions; access to specialist teams; sometimes co-investment.
Pain pointsHard to deploy billions without moving markets; need to diversify away from their home region; want control over terms.
What they buyOften SMAs (separately managed accounts): a custom account just for them that follows a Hayfin strategy. Hayfin's SEC filings list several single-investor vehicles, such as one $568m vehicle with one owner [S304] (identity not public; SMA reading is inferred).
What they like about HayfinEuropean diversification ("adding European exposure is a genuine diversification of underlying risk", Carlos Pla [S277]); custom packaging; a local presence: Hayfin opened a Dubai office in January 2024 "to enhance its coverage in the UAE and Middle Eastern markets" [S9].
Customers 4-7 · Banks, funds of funds, family offices and endowments, consultants (17.3% together)the smaller segments
Banks (6.9%)Banks with investment arms. Example: Sumitomo Mitsui Trust Bank committed to Hayfin's Maritime Yield strategy in April 2025 [S114]. Like: specialist assets they cannot easily build themselves.
Funds of funds (4.3%)Funds that pick other managers for smaller clients. Like: a manager they can defend to their own clients; standard reporting.
Family offices, endowments (4.0%)Very wealthy families and university or charity endowments. Like: long-horizon income; often smaller tickets, close to the €10m minimum [S303].
Consultants (2.1%)Investment consultants that also run money for clients. Like: a manager that passes their research process.

QQa2cQQWho is not a customer, and why that is a choice

Many rivals now sell private credit to wealthy individuals through "evergreen" funds that let investors leave each quarter. Hayfin does not: its private credit uses "fully locked-up institutional drawdown funds, with no retail capital" [S227]. In 2026 that choice became a selling point, because several large US retail funds received withdrawal requests well above their 5% quarterly limit and had to cap payouts [S184][S243]. Hayfin said those strains "accelerated LP demand for conservative fund structures" [S221]. Note the nuance: Hayfin does offer "institutional evergreen vehicles" [S221], such as the Tactical Solutions Evergreen Fund [S322], but only to institutions.

Shop analogyA wholesaler that refuses to open a retail counter. It gives up some sales, but it never has a queue of members of the public demanding refunds on the same day.

Section 5

Product differentiation

The street is full of credit shops. Eight things that make Hayfin's different, each with evidence, and where rivals beat it
Shop analogyEvery importer sells rice. Customers pick one because of how it sources, checks, packs and stands behind the rice. "Our rice is good" is not differentiation. "We have a buyer on every farm, and if a batch fails we replace it ourselves" is.

QQa5aQQThe street: who else is selling

So Hayfin is one of the three biggest "shops" on the street for this product, next to two much larger, stock-market-listed groups (Ares and ICG). Size alone does not differentiate it. The eight points below do.

QQa5bQQEight differentiators, in depth

1 · One sourcing team, several doorsthe core idea

What it is. The same local teams can offer a company a cheap senior loan (Direct Lending), something in between (Tactical Solutions) or expensive rescue capital (Special Opportunities). A 2016 Pennsylvania pension memo: Direct Lending "has served to benefit the Firm's Special Opportunities strategy... the market now not only viewing Hayfin as a 15%+ lender but an 8%+ lender. Once engaged, Hayfin has a greater ability to obtain favorable pricing" [S307]. [Confirmed, primary investor document]

Shop analogyA farm that sells you everyday wheat also tells you first when it has a rare harvest. Because you buy both, you hear about every crop, and you get first pick.

Why a customer cares. More deals seen means more can be rejected; in 2012 Hayfin saw over 1,400 opportunities and made 60 investments [S306]. And the firm can keep lending when the economy turns, because stressed companies become Special Opportunities customers rather than "no thanks" [S328].

Versus rivals. Arcmont, Pemberton and Permira Credit are more focused on direct lending; Ares and ICG are broader but are huge multi-asset groups [S130][S138][S133]. Hayfin is unusual in being credit-only but across the whole risk range [Inferred from S127].

2 · Its own repair service: the workout teamthe warranty

What it is. When a borrower struggles, Hayfin's in-house legal and restructuring people step in. "Our edge lying in our origination networks and proven expertise in managing complex restructurings, workouts and liquidations" [S331]; internal legal staff experienced "in each of the relevant creditor regimes in Europe" [S307].

Shop analogyTwo shops sell the same washing machine. One sends its own engineer within a day if it breaks; the other gives you a phone number. Customers pay for the engineer.

Why a customer cares. In credit, the upside is capped (you get your interest), so avoiding losses is everything. Flynn: workout capability is "the number one theme in the LP community" [S281]; at a Hayfin Investor Day it was the top-voted factor in a poll [S105]. Europe has many different insolvency regimes, so local legal skill is hard to copy Inferred.

3 · The local "home team" in a fragmented marketbuyers on every farm

What it is. 13 offices with local origination [S71]. Tognolini: "We consider ourselves the European home team, with 17 years of track record and a platform built to operate across fragmented jurisdictions, languages and legal regimes" [S227]. 40% of the Direct Lending book is sole-lender deals [S95], and about half of recent lending went to existing borrowers, which Tognolini calls "a competitive advantage" [S117].

Shop analogyA buyer who lives in the village knows which farmer is reliable before the harvest is weighed. A buyer who flies in once a year does not.

Why a customer cares. Sole-lender deals mean Hayfin controls the terms and the restructuring if things go wrong Inferred. Repeat borrowers mean Hayfin already knows the business. Hayfin also argues Europe carries "lower leverage, tighter documentation and smaller lender groups" than the US [S95] (firm claim).

4 · Specialist counters few rivals havethe fishmonger and the pharmacy

What it is. Dedicated healthcare (life sciences), maritime and real estate teams [S327]. Maritime has 15 specialists, over $4bn deployed and over 100 vessels, with its own ship manager, Greenheart [S227][S102].

Why a customer cares. These are small (Healthcare €0.6bn, Maritime €0.5bn in March 2025 [S305]) but give investors returns that move differently from ordinary company loans, and they make the generalist teams smarter about those sectors Inferred. No peer of similar size in the comparison set showed maritime lending [Inferred from S127 and peer sites].

5 · No run on the shop: locked-up institutional money onlythe 2026 differentiator

What it is. "Hayfin's private credit strategies rely exclusively on fully locked-up institutional drawdown funds, with no retail capital" [S227].

Shop analogyA shop with an unlimited same-day refund policy can be forced to dump stock cheaply when everyone returns things at once. A shop with no refunds never has to.

Why a customer cares. In 2026 several large US retail credit funds got withdrawal requests far above 5% a quarter and had to cap payouts [S184]. A fund that never faces withdrawals never has to sell loans at a bad price Inferred. Hayfin linked this directly to DLF V's success [S221]. Honest note: ICG says almost exactly the same ("no open-ended or so-called semi-liquid structures in direct lending") [S134], so this is a shared stance, not unique.

6 · Packaging built for each customerthe certified box

What it is. Five wrappers for DLF V, including a rated note feeder of about $600m for insurers [S222][S237] (Section 4).

Why a customer cares. Without the right wrapper, some customers simply cannot buy, whatever the quality. The rated feeder turns a capital-heavy stake into something an insurer's rulebook can accept [S222]. Ares also serves insurers through its insurance platform [S136], so the idea is not unique; Hayfin's point is doing it inside a European mid-sized manager's flagship close Inferred.

7 · Written fairness rules between its own fundsthe honest scales

What it is. Because Hayfin runs several funds, two could end up lending to the same company in different layers. Its rules: one must be a "non-blocking minority stake", under a third of the tranche; trades between Hayfin funds need prior sign-off from the Chief Compliance Officer and Chief Operating Officer; exceptions go to an Investments Conflicts Committee of the CEO, CRO, COO, CCO and General Counsel [S305]. Staff are "rewarded to collaborate, rather than outperform peers" [S305].

Shop analogyA shop that serves two rival restaurants publishes exactly how it splits the best fish between them, so neither suspects favouritism.

Why a customer cares. An investor in one Hayfin fund needs to know another Hayfin fund will not benefit at its expense. These rules are written, specific and auditable, and they answer the first question in a multi-fund investor's DDQ Inferred.

8 · Owned by the people running itthe owner behind the counter

What it is. After a 2025 management buyout funded by Arctos, the Hayfin team holds the majority of the common equity; Mubadala, AXA IM Prime and Samsung Life hold minority stakes [S73][S74][S75][S301].

Shop analogyAn owner-run shop versus a branch of a chain: the owner lives with every decision and is not following head-office targets.

Why a customer cares. Alignment: the decision-makers own the business. Contrast: Arcmont is majority-owned by Nuveen/TIAA, HPS by BlackRock, and Ares, ICG and Tikehau are listed [S131][S146][S133]. Some customers prefer the resources of a big parent, so this cuts both ways Inferred.

Why the customer sees the value

The value equation, and three customers walking through the shop
Shop analogyA caterer does not buy the cheapest rice. It buys the rice with the best mix of price, reliability, quality checks, packaging that fits its kitchen, and a supplier it trusts to fix problems. "Value" is the whole bundle, not the price tag.

Investors themselves rank what matters. In the 2026 SS&C Intralinks survey of 280+ LPs, the top reasons for choosing a manager were investment strategy (51%), sector-specific knowledge (30%), risk appetite (27%), co-investment opportunities (22%), reputation and track record (21% each) and fees (14%) [S189]. Fees came last. That supports the point: customers buy the bundle.

Section 8

Retention: why customers come back

The re-up loop, the switching costs, the evidence, and what could break it
Shop analogyA caterer stays with an importer for decades if every delivery arrives as described, every invoice is clear, problems get fixed, and switching would mean months of new supplier checks. Loyalty is earned on every delivery, not at the sale.

QQa8aQQWhy customers stay: six reasons

ReasonShop equivalentEvidence and explanation
1. They must keep buying anywayThe caterer needs supplies every yearPensions and insurers set a target share for private credit and must recommit as old funds repay [Inferred, standard allocation practice; see S202]. The question is not "whether" but "with whom". An existing, trusted manager starts ahead.
2. Switching is expensiveRe-vetting a new supplier takes monthsA DDQ can take 40-60 hours (vendor estimate) [S190]; the whole selection process can take 6-18 months (vendor, low weight) [S202]. Re-upping with a known manager skips much of that.
3. The market rewards incumbentsCustomers stick to the shops they know93% of 2025 private debt capital went to managers raising their fourth fund or later [S152]. PitchBook reads 2026's big closes as "LPs re-upping with proven managers rather than underwriting new relationships" [S151].
4. Every receipt builds trustClear invoices, no surprisesLine-by-line quarterly reports, CEO letters, audited accounts in 90 days [S305]. Accuracy here is what the intern role protects.
5. One relationship, many shelvesBuying more lines from one trusted supplierAn investor in Direct Lending can add Special Opps, Tactical or a specialist strategy without a new relationship [S327]. Vermont's 2026 commitment was to Tactical Solutions Evergreen [S322]; PSERS invested in two Special Opportunities funds [S306][S307].
6. Customers become partners, even ownersThe caterer that buys a stake in the importerBCI sold its majority stake but stayed "a significant and strategic limited partner in certain key Hayfin fund strategies" [S315]. Samsung Life became a shareholder and said it "will support Hayfin's investment strategies" [S75].

QQa8cQQWhat could break loyalty (be ready to discuss)

  • Bad deliveries. Losses or weak returns. Hayfin publishes no current loss rate; one investor (PSERS) booked a small negative carried-interest accrual on its Hayfin funds in 2024 [S308], suggesting weaker marks on older funds Inferred.
  • Too big to stay picky. A €15bn fund must be deployed while European deal volumes were down 29% in 2026 (PitchBook via ACI) [S240]. Hayfin's own answer: "Scale should be an outcome of repeatability, rather than an objective in itself" [S222].
