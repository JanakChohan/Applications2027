Section 1

What you need to assume

Eight building blocks. If any feels shaky, read it twice; every later section depends on them
#Building blockPlain-English explanation
1A fund is a contract, not a product on a shelfInvestors (limited partners, LPs) sign a limited partnership agreement (LPA) promising money to a fund run by Hayfin (the general partner, GP). The LPA sets the fees, the rules, the life of the fund and the reports Hayfin must send. Much of Partner Solutions' work is honouring that contract in writing.
2Commitments are drawn over timeAn LP commits, say, €100m. Hayfin "calls" it in pieces when it makes loans, then "distributes" interest and repayments back. Hayfin's private credit funds invest for three to four years and harvest for three to four more, with two possible one-year extensions [S305].
3Returns are measured several waysIRR (an annual rate that accounts for timing), MOIC (how many times money came back), DPI (cash returned so far), TVPI (cash returned plus what is still held). Section 4 calculates each.
4Gross is before fees; net is afterInvestors care about net. Regulators insist net is shown next to gross in marketing [S53].
5Private loans have no market priceSo the manager estimates their value every quarter (a "mark"). That is why valuation policy is one of the most scrutinised sections of any due diligence questionnaire [S166][S250].
6Investors have their own rulebooksAn insurer's capital rules, a pension's currency limits, a sovereign fund's custom terms. The same loans may need different packaging (a "wrapper") for each [S222].
7Everything external is regulatedEvery slide, case study and DDQ answer is a communication that must be "fair, clear and not misleading" (FCA COBS 4.2.1R) [S49].
8Hayfin is privateFees, recent fund returns and loss rates are not public. This guide teaches the mechanics with illustrative numbers and only uses real Hayfin figures where they are published.

Section 4 · Toolkit 1

Fund maths, the way an LP reads it

Cash flows, IRR, MOIC, DPI, TVPI, the J-curve, subscription lines, fees, the waterfall and currency, all worked through

Everything here uses one illustrative fund so the numbers connect. It is not a Hayfin fund. An LP commits €100m. Hayfin calls €90m over three years and pays back €116m over six.

QQt4aQQCash flows, IRR and MOIC

Year: 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 Capital called: -30 -40 -20 0 0 0 0 Distributions: 0 +3 +8 +25 +30 +30 +20 Net cash flow: -30 -37 -12 +25 +30 +30 +20 MOIC = total returned / total paid in = 116 / 90 = 1.29x IRR = the rate r where -30 - 37/(1+r) - 12/(1+r)^2 + 25/(1+r)^3 + 30/(1+r)^4 + 30/(1+r)^5 + 20/(1+r)^6 = 0 → r ≈ 8.1% In Excel: =IRR(range) for annual flows, or =XIRR(values, dates) for real dates.
Why both numbersIRR rewards speed: getting money back sooner lifts it. MOIC ignores time: it only counts how many euros came back per euro in. A fund can have a high IRR and a low multiple (quick but small gains) or the reverse. LPs look at both, which is why ILPA's new Performance Template asks for IRR and multiples together [S45].

QQt4bQQDPI, TVPI and the J-curve

Why this matters in 2026. When exits slow, LPs focus on DPI, the cash actually returned. A credit fund's steady interest and repayments are a DPI story. Hayfin's DLF V was over 50% deployed across 35+ companies at close [S71], so its DPI clock is already running.

QQt4cQQThe subscription line trick (and why ILPA cares)

Same fund, but it borrows from a bank for one year before calling capital (a "subscription line"), paying 5% interest, so each call arrives a year later and 5% larger: Y0 Y1 Y2 Y3 Y4 Y5 Y6 Net cash flow: 0 -28.5 -34.0 +4.0 +30 +30 +20 IRR ≈ 9.6% (was 8.1%) → looks better, because the LP's money is out for less time MOIC ≈ 1.23x (was 1.29x) → is actually worse, because the fund paid interest

ILPA's own notional example shows the same effect: IRR 6.62% and TVPI 1.45x with no line; 7.14% and 1.40x with a one-year line; 7.98% and 1.35x with a two-year line [S437]. ILPA's 2017 guidance suggested lines of 15-25% of uncalled capital outstanding for no more than 180 days [S437].

This is why the ILPA Performance Template asks managers to show performance with and without the effect of subscription facilities [S45]. When you proofread a performance page, check which basis each IRR uses. An intern who can explain this example will stand out.

QQt4dQQFees and the waterfall

Management fee. Hayfin's terms are not public. European direct lending norm (Macfarlanes, Hayfin's fund counsel): "a management fee on net invested capital, a performance fee subject to a preferred return with catch-up", headline about 1%, slightly less after discounts; "A 2% management fee would be seen as commercially unviable within direct lending" [S104]. Charging on invested rather than committed capital means LPs do not pay fees on money sitting idle Inferred. In Macfarlanes' model, cutting the fee from 1% to 0.75% lifts net IRR to 9.05%, and "all of the performance fee" arrives in the last two years [S440]. A survey of mostly US lenders (Cliffwater 2025, relayed by a blog, so treat with care) put unlevered direct lending at about 1.04% fees, an average hurdle of 5.12% and average carry of 11.2% [S441]: far below private equity's typical 2% and 20% Inferred.

Illustrative "whole-fund" waterfall (NOT Hayfin's terms). Fund returns €120m on €90m paid in. Assume a preferred return worth €15m, a 100% catch-up and a 15% performance fee (within the 10-15% range seen in direct lending [S441]). Step 1 Return of capital LP gets 90.0 (remaining profit 30.0) Step 2 Preferred return (hurdle) LP gets 15.0 (remaining 15.0) Step 3 Catch-up to GP GP gets c, where c = 15% × (15 + c) → c = 2.65 Step 4 Split the rest 85/15 remaining 12.35 → LP 10.50, GP 1.85 Totals: LP profit 25.5, GP profit 4.5 → GP has exactly 15% of the 30.0 profit.
Gross-to-net in one lineGross return (what the loans earned) minus management fee, minus fund expenses, minus the performance fee, minus the cost of any fund borrowing, equals net return to the LP. ILPA's 2025 Reporting Template adds a standard gross-to-net fee reconciliation, including rebates, waivers, offsets, placement fees and internal chargebacks [S170][S258].

Section 5 · Toolkit 2

The loan underneath

How a direct loan is priced, how its health is measured, and what PIK really does

You cannot explain a credit fund without understanding a single loan. Hayfin's quarterly Portfolio Breakdown shows each company's "LTM EBITDA, enterprise value and leverage" [S305]. Here is how to read those.

QQt5aQQPricing a unitranche loan

Illustrative €100m unitranche, 3-year expected life (NOT a Hayfin deal): Base rate: 3-month Euribor, June 2026 [S443] 2.32% Margin over Euribor (market range E+500-625bps) + 5.25% Cash coupon = 7.57% a year Issue price 98 (a 2-point OID; market 97.5-98.5), spread over a 3-year expected life + 0.67% a year (approx.) Approximate all-in yield to the lender ≈ 8.2% a year (gross) Floating rate: if Euribor rises 1 point, the coupon rises 1 point. Good for the lender's income; harder for the borrower to pay.

Where the market is (2025-2026). European direct lending median spreads fell from 613bps (2023) to 550bps (2024) to 500bps (2025 YTD), while sponsored leverage averaged 6.12x EBITDA against 4.83x in syndicated loans (Rothschild) [S445]. Q1 2026 unitranche priced at about E+525-625bps in the traditional mid-market and E+500-600bps upper mid-market (Baird) [S442]; KBRA said euro loans were "flirting with sub-8% yields for the first time" [S444]. The ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.25% in June 2026, its first hike in almost three years [S443]. Call protection is thin: over 80% of European deals had none from year three (2022 data) [S446].

This is why Hayfin was described by its LP as "an 8%+ lender" in direct lending and "15%+" in special opportunities [S307]: the label is best read as the rough all-in yield range of each product Inferred.

QQt5bQQMeasuring a borrower's health

Illustrative borrower: LTM EBITDA €50m, total debt €250m, enterprise value (EV) €550m (5.0x is below the 6.12x 2025 average for sponsored European direct lending [S445]) Leverage = debt / EBITDA = 250 / 50 = 5.0x Loan-to-value (LTV)= debt / EV = 250 / 550 = 45% Interest cost = 250 × 7.57% = €18.9m Interest cover = EBITDA / interest = 50 / 18.9 = 2.6x (syndicated loans averaged 2.71x in Q2 2026, vs 4.0x before 2022 [S443]) Equity cushion = EV − debt = €300m (what the PE owner loses first)
Read the Portfolio Breakdown like a credit analystRising leverage or falling interest cover across several quarters is a warning. A big equity cushion below the loan is protection: the private equity owner loses first. A covenant (for example "leverage must stay under 6.0x") gives the lender a seat at the table before things get bad. Hayfin says European deals offer "lower leverage, tighter documentation and smaller lender groups" than US ones [S95] (firm claim).

QQt5cQQPIK: the topic of 2026

PIK (payment in kind) = interest added to the loan instead of paid in cash. €100m loan, 4% PIK for 3 years: 100 × 1.04^3 = €112.5m owed Interest earned on paper: €12.5m. Cash received: €0. If the company's value falls, the extra €12.5m may never be collected.
Hayfin's three PIKs [S229]What it meansHow you would report it
Good PIKAgreed at the start, capped, time-limited, priced at a premium, used for growth that pays it back.Show it as planned, with the cap and the coverage.
Bad PIKIntroduced later because the borrower cannot pay cash: a stress signal. Lincoln calls loans that switched to PIK a "shadow default" (6.2% of loans, Q2 2026) [S162].Flag as an amendment; track on the watchlist.
Fake good PIKAgreed at entry, but the business never really covers it, so value slowly moves from lender to borrower.Hayfin's point: origin "says nothing about whether the position stays covered" [S229]. Track coverage, not just origin.

Why it is in your job. Fitch counted interest deferrals and PIK switches as 47% of US private credit default events in August 2026 [S245], and Hayfin's LPs probed PIK harder in DLF V than ever before [S222]. Expect ad hoc requests like "PIK income as a share of total income, split by planned and amended", and DDQ questions on PIK policy.

QQt5dQQFrom one loan to the whole fund

Weighted average, the most used formula in IRWeighted average leverage = Σ (loan size × leverage) ÷ Σ loan size. Two loans: €60m at 5.0x and €40m at 4.0x → (60×5 + 40×4) ÷ 100 = 4.6x. A simple average would wrongly say 4.5x. Always ask "weighted by what?" (commitment, drawn amount, fair value).

Packaging: why the same loans need different wrappers

The rated note feeder, insurer capital rules in the EU, US and Korea, and the look-through data insurers need. The most Hayfin-specific technical topic in this guide

The PSG co-heads put the problem in one sentence: "A European pension fund, a US institution and an Asian insurer may all want exposure to the same underlying senior-secured loans, but they may not want to access that exposure in the same form" [S403]. This section explains why, with the actual rule numbers.

QQt6cQQWhy it saves capital: three insurer rulebooks

Key ideaInsurers must hold capital against each investment, in proportion to how risky the regulator thinks it is. An opaque fund stake is treated like equity (high charge). A rated bond is treated like a bond (low charge). Same loans underneath; very different capital cost.

The three rulebooks, simply

  • EU insurers (Solvency II). Without look-through, fund units are "type 2" equity at 49% plus a symmetric adjustment of -10% to +10% (widening to ±13% from 30 January 2027) [S408][S409][S414]. Unleveraged closed-ended EU funds can be "type 1" at 39% [S407]. With look-through, each loan is charged as spread risk; unrated loans cost 3% × duration [S410], which is why insurers demand line-by-line data (below). A rated note gets bond factors, unless it counts as a securitisation, in which case much higher factors apply [S412]. Macfarlanes says a feeder that works for Solvency II insurers, as opposed to US ones, "remains a work in progress" [S448]. The Solvency II review applies from 30 January 2027 [S414][S415].
  • US insurers (NAIC). A private fund LP interest carries a 30% risk-based capital charge; investment-grade debt is "generally under 2%" [S419]. Rated feeders started here ("first designed for US insurers") [S418]. Regulators push back: the NAIC worried feeders could "repackage equity-like exposure", one driver of the principles-based bond definition effective 1 January 2025 [S419][S423], and the NAIC's securities office gained power from 1 January 2026 to challenge private ratings [S422].
  • Korean insurers (K-ICS). Since 2023 Korea uses K-ICS, a market-consistent regime at a 99.5% confidence level [S429]. Regulators cut the recommended ratio from 150% to 130-140% in 2025 and add a core capital rule from 2027 [S426][S427]. Samsung Life, a Hayfin shareholder, reported 208.2% at end-June 2026 [S428]. Exact fund and credit factors were not found [Gap], but one industry comment says some Korean insurers restructure alternatives into "rated" notes to get better treatment [S430] (snippet). This is the context for Hayfin's new Seoul office [S404].
Why this is the most distinctive thing you could learn at HayfinMost students can define IRR. Very few can explain why an insurer would buy the same European loans through a rated note rather than a fund stake, what the capital difference is, and what can go wrong (the securitisation classification, the NAIC's scrutiny). Hayfin did this in its flagship raise in 2026 [S402]. That is a conversation you can have in an interview.

QQt6dQQLook-through data: the TPT and EET

If an insurer wants the lower look-through charge, it needs data on every loan. The industry template is the Tripartite Template (TPT), maintained by FinDatEx: "a widely used European data exchange template designed to support the provision of asset data from asset managers to insurers for Solvency II reporting purposes" [S431]. Version 7 (December 2024) added "Improved handling of bonds or loans with payment-in-kind features" [S432]. Version 8 was consulted on from July to September 2026 and is expected to be mandatory in Q1 2027 [S431]. Whether V8 was finalised is not confirmed [Gap].

The European ESG Template (EET) does the same job for sustainability data under SFDR and the EU's suitability rules; version 1.1.3 has applied since 1 January 2025 [S434]. Hayfin runs six SFDR Article 8 funds [S25].

SentenceWhy it is there
"...where Hayfin's lending becomes something an investor can underwrite."Defines PSG's job in one line, from the investor's side. Shows you understand the role is translation, not sales.

QQt12cQQAn alternative version, naming two teams

Use this only if you genuinely want a second team considered. Portfolio Management is the natural partner: it supplies the data PSG reports.

Alternative answer · 172 words + your own sentence (up to ~25 words)

My first choice is Partner Solutions, with Portfolio Management second, because together they connect a loan's performance to the investor who owns it.

Partner Solutions appeals to me because Hayfin sells one platform through many doors. DLF V raised over €15bn through five wrappers, including a rated note feeder built for insurers, and investors pressed hardest on PIK, software exposure and loss rates. Answering them well needs both a clear story and exact data, which is the work I want to learn: DDQs, case studies and quarterly reports where every number ties back to its source.

Portfolio Management interests me as the source of that data. Understanding how leverage, interest cover and PIK are tracked loan by loan would make me a better investor relations professional, because I could explain a workout as well as a win.

[One sentence from your own experience.] My goal is a career on the client side of private credit. Hayfin's mix of strategies, its workout capability and its global investor base, from Seoul to Melbourne, make it the best place to start.

QQt12dQQTraps to avoid in a 200-word answer

  • Stale facts. Do not write "€30bn" or "ten offices". Use "about €40bn" (Q2 2026) [S224] or avoid AUM entirely.
  • Adjectives. "Leading", "prestigious", "dynamic" add nothing. Replace each with a fact.
  • Overclaiming. Do not say Hayfin has the best returns or the biggest fund; neither is supported.
  • The JD's typo. "Special Memorandum Accounts" should be "separately managed accounts" [S25]. If you mention SMAs, use the correct term.
  • No "you". Without your own evidence sentence, the answer could be written by anyone who read this guide.
  • Word count. The limit is 200. Count after you add your own sentence.
