1. The role, explained from zero

Assumptions made in this section
The job posting is deliberately non-technical, so this section fills gaps using (a) J.P. Morgan's own descriptions of its Private Bank team model, (b) the Private Bank "Advisor" analyst programme it runs for graduates, and (c) how comparable private banks run summer programmes. Where I infer rather than quote, I say so. Anything marked assumption should be verified with a recruiter or an alumnus before you repeat it in an interview.

1.1 One paragraph, no jargon

J.P. Morgan's Private Bank looks after very wealthy families: people who have typically built or inherited tens of millions of pounds, dollars or euros. Those families need someone to invest their money, lend them money against what they own, keep their cash safe, move it across borders, and plan how it passes to the next generation without being wasted or fought over. The Advisor (J.P. Morgan's name for the private banker) is the person the family actually talks to. The Advisor finds the family in the first place, earns their trust, understands what they want, and then brings in J.P. Morgan's specialists (investors, lenders, trust lawyers) to deliver it. The summer internship is a nine-week trial of whether you can learn to be that person.

1.2 What the words in the job title mean

Global Private Bank (GPB)
The part of J.P. Morgan that serves ultra-wealthy individuals, families and their family offices worldwide. It sits inside the firm's Asset & Wealth Management (AWM) division. "Private" is historical: it means banking done privately for individuals, as opposed to for corporations or the public.
Advisor
At J.P. Morgan this is the client-facing relationship role. At other banks the same seat is called Private Banker, Relationship Manager or Client Advisor. J.P. Morgan itself has used "Banker" for the same seat and still does internally; the careers pages now say "Advisor". It is a sales-and-relationship job with technical content, not a research or trading job.
Summer Internship Program
Nine weeks in London, starting with a one-week induction. Interns are placed with a team of advisors and specialists. Strong performers are offered a place on the full-time Advisor programme after graduation.
London, 60 Victoria Embankment
J.P. Morgan's EMEA headquarters on the Thames, opposite Blackfriars. The Private Bank's international (non-US) business is run largely from here; EMEA private banking has offices in roughly 17 locations across 11 countries per the posting.

1.3 What the Advisor is, and is not

The Advisor IS
  • The owner of the relationship: one name the family can call at 7am when a deal is closing or a child is starting university abroad.
  • A business developer: finding new families and persuading them to move assets to J.P. Morgan. This is sales, and the firm says so ("relationship building and sales capacity" is in the posting).
  • A diagnostician: listening well enough to work out what the family actually needs (often not what they first ask for).
  • A coordinator or "quarterback": pulling in the Investor, Capital Advisor, Wealth Advisor and product specialists, and owning the answer that goes back.
  • A generalist who must be conversant in markets, credit, tax and estate structuring, without being the deepest expert in any of them.
The Advisor IS NOT
  • A portfolio manager. The Investor (investment specialist) builds and manages the portfolio.
  • A trader or research analyst. Execution and research sit in other teams.
  • A retail bank adviser selling products from a menu. Every client's solution is bespoke and the firm uses both in-house and external products.
  • A lawyer or accountant. Advisors work with the family's own professionals, and with J.P. Morgan's in-house trust and planning specialists.
  • A desk job. Much of the week is meetings, calls, travel, events and prospecting.

1.4 The basics of the business, for someone who has never studied finance

Who the clients are

The Private Bank's clients are described as high-net-worth (HNW) and ultra-high-net-worth (UHNW). There is no legal definition, but the industry convention is HNW = more than $1 million of investable assets, UHNW = more than $30 million. J.P. Morgan's Private Bank pitches well above the HNW floor: in the US it has historically focused on clients with $10 million or more, and in international markets the effective floor is typically around $5 million with a bias to much larger relationships. In practice, London clients include:

  • Entrepreneurs and founders, before and after selling a business (the sale itself is often the moment they first need a private bank).
  • Senior executives of listed companies, with stock, options and concentrated positions.
  • Multi-generational families and their family offices (a family's own in-house investment team).
  • Financial professionals: private equity and hedge fund partners with carried interest and lumpy cash flows.
  • International families from the Middle East, Africa, continental Europe and Asia who use London as a hub.
  • Sports, entertainment and media figures, and foundations and endowments.

How the bank makes money from all of this

The Private Bank earns in four ways, and an Advisor is judged on growing all four for each family. First, fees on assets: an annual percentage of the money the client has invested with the bank, quoted in basis points (one basis point is 0.01%; 100 basis points is 1%). A £50 million portfolio at, say, 60 basis points earns £300,000 a year, whether or not any trade happens. Second, net interest income on deposits and loans: the bank pays the client one rate on cash and charges a higher rate on loans, and keeps the spread. Third, transaction and placement fees: commissions on trades, fees for placing clients into private equity or hedge funds. Fourth, trust and planning fees. The mix matters strategically: fee income is stable and scales with markets, interest income rose sharply when rates rose in 2022 and 2023, and lending makes a client far less likely to leave. Section 5 gives the actual numbers from the 10-K.

Why they come to a bank like J.P. Morgan rather than a boutique

  • Balance sheet: a boutique cannot lend £100 million against a portfolio of private company shares; J.P. Morgan can, and can do it in several currencies.
  • Access to the rest of the firm: the investment bank that might sell the client's company, the trading desks, the asset manager's private funds, and the research.
  • Safety: in the 2023 US regional bank crisis wealthy clients moved deposits to the largest banks. Being "too big to fail" is, for a client with £200 million in cash, a feature.
  • Global reach: a family with members in London, Dubai, Geneva and New York can be served by one bank with a consistent view.
  • Brand and longevity: the name signals seriousness to the client's own advisers, counterparties and children.

The honest counter-arguments (which a good interviewer will probe) are that a large bank can be slower, more bureaucratic, more compliance-heavy, and that clients sometimes fear being sold the house product. J.P. Morgan's answer to that last point is its open architecture: the Private Bank uses external fund managers as well as its own, chosen by a separate Manager Selection team, and the Advisor is held to a fiduciary-style standard. Whether that is fully lived is a fair interview question to ask them.

2. How the work actually flows: client, Advisor, and the firm behind them

2.1 The team around one client

The single most important structural fact about J.P. Morgan's Private Bank is that no client "belongs" to one banker. Every relationship is served by an integrated team, and the Advisor is its captain. Pablo Garnica, who runs the EMEA Private Bank from Madrid, describes the pod as a banker (captain of the relationship), an investor (portfolio decisions), a capital advisor (cash, deposits and lending), a wealth advisor (trusts, estates and structuring) and a client service specialist (transactions). The job adverts use the same language: interns "work with a team of bankers, investment specialists, wealth advisors, and lending specialists".

3. What JPMorgan Chase is

3.1 One paragraph

JPMorgan Chase & Co. is the largest bank in the United States and, by market value, the largest in the world. It does three things at scale: it banks ordinary Americans and small businesses under the Chase brand; it advises, lends to, trades for and moves money for companies, governments and institutions worldwide under the J.P. Morgan brand; and it manages money and advises wealthy people through Asset & Wealth Management. In 2025 it earned $57 billion of profit on $186 billion of revenue, has around 320,000 employees in 66 countries, and is worth roughly $945 billion on the stock market, which is more than Bank of America, Citi, Wells Fargo and Goldman Sachs combined. Its chief executive since 2005, Jamie Dimon, is the most influential banker of his generation and is now in the last few years of his tenure.

3.2 Where the firm came from (the short version that matters)

The name carries two histories. J.P. Morgan descends from the merchant bank of Junius and J. Pierpont Morgan (Drexel, Morgan & Co., 1871; J.P. Morgan & Co. from 1895), which financed US railroads, US Steel and General Electric, and effectively acted as the American central bank in the Panic of 1907. Its London arm, Morgan Grenfell, and the J.S. Morgan business gave the firm UK roots that the Private Bank dates to 1838. Chase descends from Chase National Bank (1877) and Chemical Bank, retail and commercial lenders that merged repeatedly through the 20th century. The two combined in 2000 (Chase Manhattan bought J.P. Morgan & Co.), and the modern firm was shaped by the 2004 merger with Bank One, which brought Jamie Dimon and a management culture of cost discipline, a "fortress balance sheet" and detailed operating reviews. In 2008 the firm absorbed Bear Stearns and Washington Mutual during the financial crisis; in 2023 it absorbed First Republic. Each crisis acquisition was possible because the firm had more capital than its peers when it mattered, which is the practical meaning of "fortress balance sheet" and a story the firm tells about itself constantly.

The phrase every employee learns comes from J.P. "Jack" Morgan Jr., testifying to the US Senate in May 1933 during the Pecora hearings into Wall Street: "the idea of doing only first-class business, and that in a first-class way, has been before our minds." It is quoted in the Private Bank's letters to this day, and 23 Wall Street, the old headquarters opposite the New York Stock Exchange, gives its name to the unit that serves the very largest families.

3.4 Each division in plain English, and why sectors sit where they do

Consumer & Community Banking: the deposit and data machine

Chase is the largest US retail bank by deposits (11.1% of national retail deposits) and the largest US credit card issuer (23.6% of card spending). Its economics are scale economics: 87 million customers generate cheap deposits that fund the firm, and card spending generates fees and interest. Its strategic logic is the "funnel": a customer opens a checking account, gets a card, buys a house, and eventually invests. Marianne Lake, who ran it until June 2026, said the "secret sauce" is that "90% of new advised clients were referred by bankers" in the branches. That is why J.P. Morgan Wealth Management sits in Chase rather than in AWM: its clients are acquired through the branch network, the model is scaled and digital, and the product shelf is supplied by AWM. Chase has 5.5 million affluent banking households but only about a fifth of them invest with the firm, which the firm sees as its biggest untapped wealth opportunity. The same logic explains why Chase UK sits here: it is a digital consumer bank built to be "a multi-country digital bank at the intersection of banking and investing", now in the UK (2.8 million customers) and Germany (launched May 2026).

Commercial & Investment Bank: the corporate lifecycle machine

The CIB serves companies and institutions at every stage. Commercial Banking lends to and banks mid-sized firms and start-ups ("innovation economy"); Global Corporate Banking covers the largest multinationals; Global Investment Banking advises on mergers and raises equity and debt; Markets makes prices in every asset class for institutional investors; Payments moves money and holds $760 billion of corporate operating deposits (a third of the firm's funding); Securities Services safeguards $41 trillion of assets for fund managers. In January 2024 the firm merged Commercial Banking into the CIB. Doug Petno's explanation: "Our clients are growing, scaling and expanding globally at an accelerated pace... a single wholesale operating entity, four business units tightly knit... to be our clients' most important financial partner." The logic is client-for-life coverage: the company that Commercial Banking lends to at $50 million of revenue is the company Investment Banking takes public at $5 billion. Payments sits in the CIB because it is a wholesale product (treasury services, merchant acquiring) whose deposits fund the Markets business and whose rails Chase and Securities Services also use.

Asset & Wealth Management: the fiduciary machine

AWM is where the firm acts as a fiduciary, managing other people's money for a fee rather than lending its own. It has two halves that share one engine. Asset Management ("J.P. Morgan Asset Management", run by George Gatch) manufactures products: it is the world's largest active ETF manager ($250 billion), the second-largest separately-managed-account provider ($434 billion), and runs $560 billion of alternatives and roughly $1 trillion of money-market funds. The Global Private Bank (run by David Frame) distributes advice, investments, lending, banking and trust services to wealthy families. Erdoes insists they be seen as "two different businesses", but they sit together because the Private Bank is Asset Management's most important distribution channel and Asset Management's research and products are the Private Bank's raw material. A shared unit, Wealth Management Solutions (Martin Marron), builds the advice platform used by both the Private Bank and Chase's advisors so that "no matter where you come into this institution, you get the best advice." Section 6 goes deep on this division.

Corporate: the balance sheet's balance sheet

Treasury and the Chief Investment Office manage the firm's liquidity ($1.5 trillion of cash and marketable securities), capital and interest-rate risk. It is where the "fortress" is engineered. It also carries the firm's central costs and one-off items (the 2024 Visa share gain landed here).

3.5 How the pieces link: the "one firm" engine

Dimon's phrasing in every annual letter is that the firm is "not a conglomerate": "we strive to build enduring businesses, which rely on and benefit from one another." CFO Jeremy Barnum in February 2026: "Our completeness, global presence, diversification and scale are not just attributes. They're our competitive advantages... our operating model enables us to support our clients and customers through their entire lifecycle and through multiple generations." The disclosed evidence for the Private Bank specifically:

  • "Over 95% of our top 50 AWM clients also work with our other lines of business." (Erdoes, 2025 letter)
  • "There are 2,000 private banking clients that walk into those [Chase] branches every day." (Erdoes, Investor Day 2025)

4.5 The honest verdict for the UK and EMEA private bank

Where J.P. Morgan wins in London
  • One global model. "We operate the only private bank in the industry with a uniform business model globally" (Erdoes). A family in London, Dubai and New York gets the same team structure, platform and credit process.
  • Balance sheet. Lending capacity, in size and across currencies, that Swiss partnerships and boutiques cannot match; the largest sports-finance and jumbo-mortgage franchise in private banking.
  • Alternatives. Euromoney's world's best private bank for alternatives in 2025 and 2026; over $200bn of alternatives, evergreen structures, an 18-person in-house structuring team.
  • Growth and margin. Advisor headcount up over 60% since 2020, "outpacing major peers", funded by a 36 to 38% margin. Barclays' private bank profit fell and Julius Baer's fell 25% in the same year.
  • Founder connectivity. The investment bank that sells the company is the bank that meets the founder first.
  • Safety. Credit Suisse collapsed in 2023 and Julius Baer took heavy losses on Signa; J.P. Morgan's "fortress" is a sales argument in Europe.
Where J.P. Morgan loses in London
  • Scale in Europe. International Private Bank client assets are about $750bn across all non-US regions, against UBS's $4.8tn. UBS is the default for Swiss booking, Gulf family offices and cross-border European families.
  • UK heritage. Coutts (330 years, royal warrant), Barclays and HSBC own domestic UK private banking: sterling current accounts, mortgages, the "family bank" role. Chase UK and the Private Bank are not yet joined up the way Coutts and NatWest are.
  • Thresholds. At roughly $10m and up, J.P. Morgan leaves the £1 to 5m market to Coutts, Evelyn, Rathbones and St. James's Place.
  • Sub-scale local coverage. Erdoes' own words: market share "below 1%" in several international markets. A runway, but also a gap.
  • Non-dom outflows. The April 2025 tax changes push internationally mobile UHNW families out of London, which hits J.P. Morgan's core international segment harder than a domestic-heavy Coutts.

The strategic response to the "loses" column is exactly what Adam Tejpaul, CEO of the International Private Bank, described in 2026: "in terms of where the biggest opportunity is to grow market share, it's very much within the European franchise", with Europe split into three regions under "almost CEO-like" heads (UK; Continental Europe; Switzerland and the Middle East), 1,000 international relationship managers today with a stated ambition of around 1,900 "over time", and new offices in Manchester, Edinburgh, Glasgow, Munich, Hamburg and Berlin. That is the growth plan your internship class is being hired into.

6.4 From sales to relationship: what the journey looks like in revenue terms

AWM's income statement has four lines that map onto the client relationship: asset management fees ($15.5bn in 2025), commissions and other fees ($1.2bn), net interest income ($6.8bn) and other income ($0.6bn). Net interest income was about a quarter of AWM revenue in 2022, rose to 40% in 2024 as rates went up, and fell back to 28% in 2025 as loan spreads narrowed. In 2022 and 2023 clients moved deposits into money-market funds ("cash sorting"); Private Bank deposits fell from $282bn to $233bn while AWM's money-market assets rose to nearly $1tn. The "sales" phase (steps 1 to 3 of the journey) is measured in new clients and net new assets. The "relationship" phase (steps 4 to 7) is measured in share of wallet, revenue per client and retention. J.P. Morgan's disclosed facts: more than 95% of Private Bank clients stay; when a US Private Bank client also actively banks with the firm, assets and revenue are on average 65% higher; only 2% of clients are credit-only; revenue per producer runs around $3 million; and revenue per average banker rose 15% between 2020 and 2025 even as the number of bankers grew 64%. A junior banker's book is built by "seeding", not by buying: "This is where we don't buy books of business. We seed books of business" (Erdoes, 2022).

7. Turning all of this into your own reasons

The posting says the firm wants to hear from you in a "complete and thoughtful application". The HireVue opener reported by 2026 candidates was: "What attracts you to J.P. 7.1 Three questions and the honest logic behind each

Why private banking, not investment banking or asset management?

The true answer, if it applies to you: the work is relationship-led and generalist. You are paid to understand a whole family and to orchestrate specialists, not to be the deepest specialist. It combines sales (finding and winning clients) with markets, credit and planning. It compounds: a banker's network and book grow over decades, and the great wealth transfer ($124tn in the US through 2048; £5.5tn in the UK by 2047) means the pool grows too. The honest downside is that it is a sales culture with a slow start; say so, and say why that suits you.

Why J.P. Morgan's private bank rather than UBS, Goldman or Coutts?

Pick the two or three reasons that you actually believe and can defend: the integrated team model (you learn every discipline before specialising); the balance sheet and investment bank behind the advice (the client conversations are bigger); the apprenticeship model ("we seed books, we don't buy them", half of advisors home-grown); the growth (advisors up 64% in five years, Europe named as the biggest market-share opportunity, new offices across the UK and Germany); and the fiduciary, open-shelf stance. Avoid "it's the biggest bank" as a reason; the firm itself says size is often a disadvantage.

Why the Advisor track, not Investment Solutions?

Because you want to be the person the client calls, and you accept that this means prospecting, rejection and responsibility for the whole relationship. Evidence from your life: anything where you won trust, sold something, built a network, or kept going through a long run of "no". The posting's own words are your checklist: "relationship building and sales capacity", "genuine interest in financial markets and macro-level economic trends", "good judgment and discretion".

What the firm's thinking tells you it wants to hear

Read the letters and Investor Days together and the firm is telling you what it values in an advisor: a fiduciary who can say no, a team player who does not count credit, someone who prepares for the worst ("prepare for the worst and hope for the best"), someone who shows up and grinds early, and someone curious about markets beyond the headlines. Reflect those in your examples, not in adjectives.

Appendix A. Glossary (the words you will hear in week one)

AUM / AUS / client assets
Assets under management are money the bank actively invests for clients. Assets under supervision (J.P. Morgan's older term) and "client assets" add money the bank holds or advises on but does not manage, plus deposits and custody. Client assets is the broadest number and the one J.P. Morgan now headlines.
Net new money / net flows
Money coming in minus money going out, excluding market movements. The cleanest measure of whether clients are choosing you. Mary Erdoes' phrase: "clients vote with their feet".
Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of one percent. Fees, spreads and returns are all quoted in bps.
Discretionary / advisory / brokerage
Three ways to run a client's money. Discretionary: the bank decides and executes within an agreed mandate. Advisory: the bank recommends, the client approves each move. Brokerage (execution-only): the client instructs, the bank executes. Fees are highest for discretionary.
Strategic vs tactical asset allocation
Strategic: the long-run mix of equities, bonds, alternatives and cash designed for the client's goals (J.P. Morgan bases it on its annual Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions). Tactical: short-term tilts around that mix based on the house view.
Alternatives
Anything not listed equities, bonds or cash: private equity, private credit, hedge funds, real estate, infrastructure, timber. Illiquid, higher fee, and a strategic priority for every private bank because they lock in assets and differentiate.
Open architecture
Offering external managers' funds, not only the bank's own. J.P. Morgan's Manager Selection team researches third-party managers.
Securities-based lending (SBL) / Lombard loan
A loan secured on the client's investment portfolio. Lets a client raise cash without selling. The bank sets a loan-to-value and can demand more collateral (a "margin call") if the portfolio falls.
Net interest income (NII)
Interest earned on loans and investments minus interest paid on deposits. Rose sharply for all banks in 2022 and 2023 as central banks raised rates.
KYC / AML / source of wealth
Know Your Customer and Anti-Money-Laundering checks. Before a client is onboarded the bank must document who they are and how they made their money. In private banking this is slow, intrusive and non-negotiable; Advisors spend real time on it.
Suitability / Consumer Duty
UK regulatory requirements that products recommended are appropriate for the client and that firms deliver good outcomes. The FCA's Consumer Duty (2023) applies to private banks' retail clients.
Non-dom
A UK tax status for residents domiciled elsewhere, abolished from April 2025 and replaced by a residence-based regime. A live topic for every London private banker because it changed where some wealthy clients choose to live.
Family office
A private company set up by a very wealthy family to manage its investments and affairs. Single-family offices serve one family; multi-family offices serve several. J.P. Morgan's "23 Wall" team covers the largest ones.
Liquidity event
The moment a founder's paper wealth becomes cash: a sale, an IPO, a secondary. The single most important moment for a private banker to be in the room.
Share of wallet
The proportion of a client's total wealth that sits with you. Most UHNW families use two to four banks; the game is to become the primary one.
CET1 / fortress balance sheet
Common Equity Tier 1 capital as a percentage of risk-weighted assets, the core regulatory measure of a bank's safety. "Fortress balance sheet" is Jamie Dimon's phrase for holding more capital and liquidity than required so that the bank is a safe haven in a crisis.
ROTCE
Return on tangible common equity, JPMorgan's headline profitability measure. The firm's stated through-the-cycle target has been 17%; it has run well above that recently.
10-K
The annual report every US-listed company files with the SEC. JPMorgan's runs to several hundred pages and is the primary source for all segment numbers in Guide 2.
