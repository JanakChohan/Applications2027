5. Drill-down: Asset & Wealth Management through Mary Erdoes' letters, 2021 to 2025

Mary Callahan Erdoes has run AWM since September 2009 (she joined J.P. Morgan in 1996 aged 29 to run fixed income, and ran the Private Bank from 2005 to 2009). Her annual letter is short, numerical and repetitive in a useful way: the same handful of measures every year, so the trend is legible. The job posting told you to read the 2021 letter. This section reads all five.

5.1 The 2021 letter: the four ingredients

The 2021 letter set out "four ingredients for future growth": (1) investment performance, (2) talent ("we retained nearly 95% of top senior talent and more than doubled the number of front office joiners to over 2,500... adding nearly 300 net new client advisors"), (3) new clients ("over the past five years, the number of net new GPB clients tripled"), (4) flows ("clients vote with their feet"; a record $389bn). It closed on "first-class business in a first-class way" and "our fiduciary duty to our clients above everything else".

{{FIG:flywheel}}

The four ingredients are not a list; they are a loop. Performance attracts talent and clients; talent wins clients; clients bring flows; flows fund investment in performance and talent. The Advisor sits in ingredients 2 and 3 and is measured on ingredient 4. Every subsequent letter can be read as a report on the loop.

Reading the shift. 2021 and 2022 are letters about inputs: the four ingredients and the acquisitions. 2023 is about money in motion after the bank crisis. 2024 is a league-table letter (ROE against peers). 2025 is a rankings letter (number one in active flows, active ETFs, private bank) with, for the first time, a detailed account of the economics of training advisors and a signal that hiring will "normalize later this decade". "First-class business in a first-class way" fades after 2022; "fiduciary" and "north star" replace it. The one constant is the flows sentence: "clients vote with their feet" (2021), "vote daily... with new assets" (2024), "vote with their flows" (2025).

5.3 The targets, and why they never move

At Investor Day 2020 AWM set four through-the-cycle targets: revenue growth of 5%, pretax margin of at least 25%, ROE of at least 25%, and long-term flows of 4% of the asset base. They have been reiterated unchanged at every Investor Day since and beaten every year.

5.4 The business model, past the marketing

Two halves, one engine

The 10-K defines them. Asset Management "offers multi-asset investment management solutions across equities, fixed income, alternatives and money market funds to institutional and retail investors." The Global Private Bank "provides retirement products and services, brokerage, custody, estate planning, lending, deposits and investment management to high net worth clients." Client assets are split roughly equally between them. Asset Management is a manufacturer: it employs about 1,300 investment professionals and 500 career research analysts (Erdoes: "treasured athletes, not want-to-be portfolio managers"), spends over $500m a year on research, covers 5,000 companies, and sells the output to pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurers, fund platforms and, crucially, the Private Bank. The Private Bank is a distributor and adviser: it has the client relationships and the balance sheet.

The tension in every bank-owned wealth manager is between selling the house product and advising impartially. J.P. Morgan handles it by making the fiduciary standard the stated rule ("we do not believe that any fiduciary manager should dictate choice or preferences") and by running a large third-party platform (850+ external alternatives managers; Morgan Money as an "open architecture" cash platform), while candidly noting on its EMEA site that it "prefers J.P. Morgan managed strategies" and earns more when they are used. Whether the balance is right is a legitimate question to ask in an interview; the firm's answer is that performance is the arbiter: 83% of its ten-year active fund assets beat the peer median.

Three mechanics worth understanding

  • Net interest income and rates. NII was "about 25% of our business" in 2022, rose to 40% in 2024 as rates went up, and fell back to 28% in 2025 as loan spreads narrowed. A private bank is partly a bank: when rates rise, its deposit franchise earns more.
  • Cash sorting. In 2022 and 2023 clients moved deposits into higher-yielding money-market funds; Private Bank deposits fell from $282bn to $233bn while AWM's money-market assets rose to nearly $1tn. Because J.P. Morgan owns both the bank and the funds, "nearly all net deposit migration stays within the firm". A pure asset manager or a pure bank would have lost that money.
  • Lending as strategy. Only 2% of Private Bank clients are credit-only. When a US Private Bank client also actively banks with the firm, assets and revenue are on average 65% higher. The firm is the number one mortgage lender to US households with over $10m of net worth and the largest sports-team lender. Losses are tiny (one basis point over ten years; a single client charge-off pushed 2025 to four basis points) because "we want two ways out". Lending is how a client becomes hard to lose.

5.6 The economics of an advisor, and why the firm keeps hiring

{{FIG:jcurve}}

Erdoes has disclosed more about advisor economics than any peer. In 2022: "This isn't the brokerage business model... This is really an apprenticeship model business. This is where we don't buy books of business. We seed books of business... nearly half of our new advisors are homegrown... their productivity doubles after they've been here for a year... our breakevens are still in year three or earlier and our paybacks are in year five." In 2023: revenue per producer around $3m, and "47,000 resumes for those 400 slots for the analysts and associates... It's harder than college." In 2024: revenue per US client advisor up 28% and average client size up 23% since 2019; training hours per new hire up 5.6 times; 493,000 applications for 4,000 summer places firmwide. In 2025: "these 25-year-olds that are coming online... are just as productive as our 30- to 35-year-old mid-career hires"; productivity "up 3.4 times"; the home-grown share of new advisors rose from 33% to 48%. In the 2025 letter: advisors 2,500 to 4,100 in five years, revenue per banker up 15%, training cost per advisor down 27%, and the signal that hiring "normalizes later this decade".

The reason this matters to an intern: the firm has decided that growing its own advisors is cheaper and more loyal than poaching them, that AI shortens the J-curve, and that the years in which it will hire most are the next two or three. Every analyst is trained in Python (from 2023) and prompt engineering (from 2024); front-line advisors teach the classes; the aim is to get hires "JPMorganized... that's our secret sauce".

5.7 The AI thread inside AWM

  • SpectrumIQ has "automated nearly 75% of equity trading (saving clients about $4 billion in trading costs since inception) and almost 85% of foreign exchange trading"; from April 2026 it also runs proxy voting, making J.P. Morgan "the first major asset manager to fully disengage from external proxy advisors".
  • Connect Coach, the advisor "next best action" tool: 12,000 users across the Private Bank and US wealth management, "25 specialized AI agents", "1 million personalized AI-driven insights"; advisers find information "up to 95% faster"; the firm expects it to let advisors "expand client rosters by approximately 50%". Erdoes' framing: "a practical step toward having every advisor operate like our very best and every client feel like our only one."
  • Her view on advisors versus AI (February 2026): "The more you invest, the more the ecosystem creates a moat... if a client says, 'I don't like fixed income'... that's not the right answer, but that's where AI will go. And so, you need the combination of really smart AI and then really smart advisors." The advisor's edge is judgment and the willingness to disagree with the client.
