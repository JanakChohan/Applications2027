2. How the work actually flows: client, Advisor, and the firm behind them

2.1 The team around one client

The single most important structural fact about J.P. Morgan's Private Bank is that no client "belongs" to one banker. Every relationship is served by an integrated team, and the Advisor is its captain. Pablo Garnica, who runs the EMEA Private Bank from Madrid, describes the pod as a banker (captain of the relationship), an investor (portfolio decisions), a capital advisor (cash, deposits and lending), a wealth advisor (trusts, estates and structuring) and a client service specialist (transactions). The job adverts use the same language: interns "work with a team of bankers, investment specialists, wealth advisors, and lending specialists".

Why the team model matters for your application
It tells you what the Advisor is being selected for. Not portfolio maths (the Investor has that), not legal knowledge (the Wealth Advisor has that), but the ability to hold the whole picture of a family, win their trust, and orchestrate experts. The posting's phrase "desire to ultimately work with external clients in a relationship building and sales capacity" is a literal description of the seat.

2.2 How we work with the client: the journey

{{FIG:client_journey}}
1
Source
Where clients come from, in order of quality: referrals from existing clients; referrals from inside J.P. Morgan (the investment bank that just sold a founder's company, the commercial bank that lends to their firm, a Chase branch whose customer has outgrown it); "centres of influence" such as lawyers and accountants; events and thought-leadership; and cold prospecting. Mary Erdoes at the 2025 Investor Day: "We are every day investing in the companies that are taken public by the investment bank... helping the CEOs on their personal balance sheets, taking their 10b5-1 programs... or we're taking all the great things in the commercial bank and the leads that they have." Referrals convert at a far higher rate than anything else, which is why a banker's own network compounds over a career.
2
Discover
One or several meetings mapping the family's balance sheet (liquid assets, business stakes, property, existing banks), their tax residence, their family structure, and what they are actually worried about. J.P. Morgan's planning framework uses four "lenses": Liquidity (cash for the near term), Lifestyle (spending), Legacy (heirs and philanthropy) and Growth (long-term appreciation). Most of what is said in these meetings is confidential; discretion is a hiring criterion for that reason.
3
Propose
The Advisor and Investor produce a written proposal: a strategic asset allocation, a critique of the client's existing portfolio (concentration, fees, tax drag), lending capacity, a cash and currency plan, and planning ideas. The analyst or intern does much of the assembly: pulling data, building the deck, coordinating the specialists' sections. This is the "Solutions Team" work the posting describes.
4
Onboard
Know-Your-Customer and source-of-wealth documentation: how the fortune was made (business sale agreements, audited accounts, inheritance papers) and where each deposit comes from. Suitability assessments under UK and EU rules. Account opening in the right legal entity (J.P. Morgan SE in Europe, J.P. Morgan (Suisse) in Switzerland, the UK bank in London). Slow, intrusive and non-negotiable. Analysts spend real hours here.
5
Implement
Assets transfer in, portfolios are built, loans are drawn, trusts are settled, cash and FX arranged. The Investor executes through the firm's trading desks; the Capital Advisor's loan goes through credit underwriting; the Wealth Advisor works with the family's own lawyers.
6
Review
Quarterly reviews, market calls, rebalancing, and responding to life events (a sale, a divorce, a move to Dubai, a child joining the business). The house view arrives through Eye on the Market, the Outlook, Top Market Takeaways and the Global Investment Strategy team. J.P. Morgan's AI tool, Connect Coach, now flags for each advisor which clients are under-exposed to a theme after the morning meeting and drafts the talking points.
7
Deepen
Winning more of the wallet (most UHNW families use two to four banks), onboarding the next generation, and being referred to the client's friends. J.P. Morgan says that when a US Private Bank client also actively banks with it, assets and revenues are on average 65% higher, and only 2% of its clients are credit-only. This is the economic point of the whole model.

2.3 How we work with the internal teams

Read the diagram top to bottom. Clients arrive through the "one firm" referral engine at the top. The Advisor diagnoses the need and pulls in the right internal team. What goes back to the client is always owned by the Advisor, even though most of the technical content was produced by someone else. Three teams deserve special mention because they are what the interview will expect you to know:

  • Global Investment Strategy: the house view. Co-headed by Grace Peters (London) and Stephen Parker, with Michael Cembalest as Chairman of Market and Investment Strategy writing Eye on the Market. Their Outlook and weekly Top Market Takeaways are what advisors relay to clients.
  • Alternatives: run globally by Kristin Kallergis Rowland. The Private Bank places about $2 billion a month into alternatives, has over $200 billion of alternatives assets, and runs 850+ external managers on its platform alongside its own private equity and credit funds. About half of the assets of its 200 largest clients are in alternatives.
  • Credit underwriting: the reason the balance sheet is a differentiator. Erdoes' rule: "97% of what we do is fully collateralized... we want two ways out." A loan is only made if the bank can be repaid from the collateral and from the client's cash flow.

The full-time programme you would be converting into

The 2027 Global Private Bank Advisor Analyst Training Program is advertised for London, Manchester and Edinburgh. It opens with regional orientation and then a five-week global training programme with analysts from every region (historically in New York), followed by "exposure to every facet of wealth management, including investments, lending, banking, and trust and estate planning". Forum accounts from London analysts describe roughly two years as Analyst, then Associate, with the formal split between the Banker (Advisor) and Investor tracks made at Associate level, and a working day of about 8am to 6.30pm. Erdoes says "half of our new advisors come from growing them from within" and that home-grown 25-year-olds are now "just as productive as our 30- to 35-year-old mid-career hires". Reported London base pay for first-year analysts is around £60,000 plus a relocation payment; treat that as indicative, since it comes from forum posts rather than the firm. UK advisers must hold an FCA-recognised Level 4 qualification (typically the CISI's) before giving regulated advice; the programme funds the exams.

Hours are long but predictable compared with investment banking: roughly 50 hours a week is the industry norm. Erdoes has been blunt that early-career mastery takes intensity: junior bankers, she told a Georgetown audience, work "12-hour days six days a week" to reach "mastery", and the two most valuable skills are "how to pitch and how to negotiate a trade". Whether or not you find that appealing is itself a useful test of fit.
