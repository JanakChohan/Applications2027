Executive read: what the filings say in one page

Read in sequence, JPMorgan Chase's five annual reports from 2021 to 2025, its four Investor Days and its February 2026 Company Update tell one story in three phases.

1
2021 to 2022: invest through the cycle, and take the criticism
Coming out of the pandemic with record profits, the firm announced it would raise investment spending to $15bn a year and warned it might miss its 17% return target. The shares fell and analysts called it undisciplined. Management held a special Investor Day to defend the plan: "good expenses" (branches, bankers, technology) versus "bad expenses" (bureaucracy). Chase UK launched; a string of small technology acquisitions followed. Then rates rose, Dimon warned of a "hurricane", and the firm's fortress balance sheet became the point.
2
2023 to 2024: the crisis buyer, record profits, and a fight with regulators
When First Republic failed in May 2023 the firm was the only buyer with the capital and the systems to take it. That deal created "effectively a new business" in affluent wealth. Revenue jumped 23% in 2023 and set records again in 2024. The firm reorganised its wholesale businesses into one Commercial & Investment Bank. Dimon spent his letters attacking the Basel III capital proposal, refusing to buy back stock at high prices ("excess capital is earnings in store") and warning about deficits, geopolitics and inflation.
3
2025 to 2026: turning outward, AI at scale, and a succession clock
With about $40bn of surplus capital and a new US administration easing regulation, the firm launched a $1.5tn "Security and Resiliency" initiative with up to $10bn (or $20bn) of direct equity, bought Apple's card portfolio, launched Chase in Germany, put a deposit token on a public blockchain, and told investors that AI had already cut headcount in "discrete areas" by 30 to 40% while front-office hiring continued. In June 2026 two co-presidents were named and Dimon said he would stay "a few years" more. AWM, the division you are applying to, is the cleanest expression of the whole strategy: targets set in 2020 and beaten every year, advisor headcount up 64%, and a stated "path to $10 trillion" of client assets.

1. How to read a 10-K, for someone who never has

A Form 10-K is the annual report a US-listed company must file with the Securities and Exchange Commission. JPMorgan's runs to several hundred pages. For a job applicant, five parts matter:

PartWhat it containsWhat to look for
Business overview (Item 1)How the firm describes its segments, what each does, who its clients are.The exact definitions. When the definition of a segment changes, the strategy changed.
Risk factors (Item 1A)Everything that could hurt the firm, from geopolitics to a cyber attack.New risks added year on year (AI, stablecoins, private credit appeared recently).
Management's Discussion and AnalysisThe narrative of the year's results, segment by segment, with the key metrics (revenue lines, AUM, client assets, advisors, loans, deposits, margins, ROE).The segment tables. AWM's table has revenue split between Asset Management and Global Private Bank, the number of client advisors, and pretax margin by half.
Financial statements and notesAudited numbers; notes on acquisitions (First Republic is Note 34 in the FY2023 filing), litigation, capital.Deal terms and contingent liabilities.
The CEO letter (in the annual report, not the 10-K)Jamie Dimon's essay, typically 50 to 60 pages, plus letters from each line-of-business CEO including Mary Erdoes for AWM.The letters are where strategy and values are stated in plain language. They are the primary source for Sections 2, 3 and 5 of this guide.

JPMorgan reports revenue on a "managed" basis (grossing up tax-exempt income so segments are comparable). Every figure in this guide uses the managed basis unless stated. "ROTCE" is return on tangible common equity, the firm's headline profitability measure; its stated through-the-cycle target is 17%, which management calls "not aspirational" but "a helpful backstop".

What happened. A record year, then a shock. At the January 2022 earnings call the firm disclosed that 2022 expenses would be about $77bn, including roughly $15bn of "investments", and that it might miss its 17% return target. The stock fell and the firm held its first Investor Day since 2020 to explain itself. The 2021 letter is the rationale: "expenses related to investments would increase from $11.5 billion to $15 billion"; "over the last 18 months, we spent nearly $5 billion on acquisitions"; $2.2bn on new cloud data centres. Chase UK had launched in September 2021 and had half a million customers and $10bn of deposits within eight months, burning about $450m a year with break-even promised in "five to six years". Nutmeg, OpenInvest, 55ip, Campbell Global, Frank and The Infatuation were bought; a 40% stake in Brazil's C6 and a 49% stake in Greece's Viva Wallet were taken.

What the language signalled. Three things that have not changed since. First, the moat is not assumed: "the moats that protect this company are not particularly deep — and we face extraordinary competition" (Apple, Walmart, fintech, shadow banks). Second, the capital hierarchy: "Pay your dividend consistently, invest in the company, organic growth... acquisitions... and then stock buyback... that's the last thing." Third, "good expenses versus bad expenses": "Bad expenses are things which lead to bureaucracy, waste, corporate statism... Good expenses are new branches, new bankers... technology." The macro warning was already there: "Persistent inflation will require rising interest rates and a massive but necessary shift from quantitative easing to quantitative tightening." Nine days after Investor Day Dimon upgraded "storm clouds" to "a hurricane".

AWM in 2021. Revenue $17.0bn, net income $4.7bn, ROE 33%, record flows of $389bn, roughly 300 net new client advisors. Erdoes' letter set out the "four ingredients for growth" (investment performance, talent, new clients, flows) and named the alternatives build-out and personalisation (55ip, OpenInvest) as the investment priorities.

What happened. The fastest rate rise in forty years. Net interest income surged, markets fell, and both stocks and bonds had negative returns for the first time in fifty years (Erdoes' opening line that year). Profit fell only because the firm rebuilt loan-loss reserves. Investment continued: over 300 AI use cases in production, 900 data scientists, 38% of applications migrated to the cloud, 57,000 technologists.

What the language signalled. The fortress as the defining fact: the firm had "enough total loss-absorbing capacity to bear out peak CCAR losses... more than eight times over". The first sustained attack on the coming Basel III capital rules: "Banks will play a smaller role in the global financial system"; "regulatory arbitrage is already forcing many activities... outside the banking system." And the widening list of risks: deficits, quantitative tightening, uninsured deposits, and "we have not had a major land war in Europe since 1945". Read it now and it is the intellectual preparation for what the firm did when the regional banks failed four months later.

AWM in 2022. Revenue $17.7bn (+5%, a record despite falling markets), pretax margin 33%, ROE 25%, the 19th consecutive year of net inflows, and the Private Bank passed 3,000 advisors for the first time. Active ETFs went from two funds to 78, and J.P. Morgan became the number two active ETF manager.

What happened. Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank failed in March; First Republic, a San Francisco bank known for white-glove service to wealthy clients, failed on 1 May and the FDIC sold it to JPMorgan. The terms: $10.6bn paid, about $92bn of deposits, $173bn of loans and $30bn of securities assumed, a $50bn five-year loan from the FDIC, and loss-sharing on 80% of credit losses. The firm booked a $2.7bn day-one gain and later said the deal would add closer to $2bn a year of earnings rather than the $500m first announced. Daniel Pinto's framing at Investor Day: "Jamie always says that excess capital is just future earnings and I think this is a nice example of that." It was also the only kind of bank acquisition the firm is legally allowed to make: it holds more than 10% of US deposits and may only buy a failed bank.

Why it mattered for wealth. Dimon: "First Republic had done an outstanding job serving high-net-worth clients and venture capitalists, and we are developing what is effectively a new business for us following First Republic's servicing model." The roughly 200 brokerage advisors and $200bn went into Chase's J.P. Morgan Advisors; the private banking relationships went to the Private Bank; the service model became "J.P. Morgan Private Client" in 2024. Erdoes said First Republic's "insurance and annuities, trusts and estates, and fund finance" capabilities "will drastically help how we do those businesses." Retention was mixed: the firm claimed over 90% of clients stayed, an executive later put broker retention at about 75%, and at least 86 First Republic brokers with around $50bn left. The March 2023 turmoil also sent wealthy clients' cash to the largest banks; Erdoes reported the Private Bank onboarding 40,000 accounts between 10 March and Investor Day, and new $100m+ clients arriving at "four a day" versus one a day the year before.

What the language signalled. AI moved to the centre: "more than 2,000 AI/ML experts and data scientists", "over 400 use cases", and the claim that AI could be "as transformational as the printing press, the steam engine, electricity, computing and the Internet". The Basel fight sharpened ("many of the rules are flawed and poorly calibrated"; a 25% increase in required capital). Growth spending continued: 500 more branches and 3,500 hires over three years. And the firm prepared for a wide range of outcomes: interest rates "2% to 8% or even more".

AWM in 2023. Revenue $19.8bn, net income $5.2bn, ROE 31%, net flows $490bn ("nearly half a trillion dollars, $490 billion to be precise", Erdoes' opening), client assets $5tn, advisors 3,515. The Private Bank was bringing in "$1 billion in net new money" every day.

What happened. On 25 January 2024 the firm merged Commercial Banking into the Corporate & Investment Bank to form the Commercial & Investment Bank, with Jennifer Piepszak and Troy Rohrbaugh as co-CEOs and Marianne Lake as sole CEO of Chase. Profits set another record. Excess capital reached roughly $54bn and Dimon refused to spend it on buybacks "at these prices": "we look at excess capital as earnings in store or reserve — waiting to be used." The May 2024 Investor Day announced 1,300 more Chase wealth advisors, confirmed 500 new branches over five years, and put a value of $1 to 1.5bn on the AI programme. The firm's generative-AI portal, LLM Suite, reached 200,000 employees within eight months of launch. In February 2025 the direct-lending commitment was raised to $50bn.

What the language signalled. Confidence, and impatience with process. Dimon's succession timetable "isn't five years anymore". On 10 January 2025 hybrid working ended: five days in the office from March, defended bluntly at a February town hall. The 2024 letter, published in April 2025 as tariffs hit, was the darkest yet on the world ("the most perilous and complicated geopolitical and economic environment since World War II"; "the recent tariffs will likely increase inflation") and the sharpest on culture: a section titled "Why complacency, arrogance, bureaucracy and BS kill companies", and rules "written by academics with no pragmatic experience".

AWM in 2024. Revenue $21.6bn, net income $5.4bn, ROE 34% "versus a peer range of 12 to 28%", flows $486bn, client assets $5.9tn, advisors 3,775, alternatives $504bn, and the firm became number one in active ETF flows. Erdoes: "We will never stop investing in new advisors on the front lines until we run out of great people to hire."

What happened. A year of deploying capital outward. On 13 October 2025 the firm announced the Security and Resiliency Initiative: "$1.5 trillion, 10-year plan to facilitate, finance and invest in industries critical to national economic security", including "direct equity and venture capital investments of up to $10 billion" in supply chains, defence, energy and frontier technology; Todd Combs left the board in December to run the new Strategic Investment Group. The firm agreed to take over Apple's card portfolio from Goldman Sachs (about $20bn of receivables, closing around late 2027) and booked a $2.2bn reserve for it. It moved into its new $3 to 4bn headquarters at 270 Park Avenue. JPMD, a deposit token, went live on the Base blockchain in November 2025 as "an alternative to stablecoins"; Kinexys, the firm's blockchain unit, passed $3tn of cumulative volume. Chase Germany launched on 20 May 2026. Basel III was rescinded and recalibrated in March 2026. Dimon said he would remain CEO for "a few years" and in June 2026 the board named Doug Petno and Troy Rohrbaugh co-presidents; Marianne Lake retired.

The 2026 numbers so far. Second-quarter 2026 revenue of $58.0bn was up 27% and net income of $21.2bn included a $4.6bn Visa gain; excluding one-offs ROTCE was 23%. Investment banking fees rose 30% and equities trading 86%. AWM revenue rose 19% to $6.9bn and net income 33% to $2.0bn, with client assets reaching $7.7tn. Full-year 2026 guidance: net interest income around $105.5bn and adjusted expense around $107.5bn, the first driver of the expense increase being "bankers, advisors and branches".

What the language signalled. Four shifts. First, deregulation is now a tailwind rather than a fight ("the benefits of deregulation"; the GSIB surcharge is still "absurd" but the endgame is over). Second, AI has moved from promise to headcount: the CCB operations workforce "will trend down by about 10% over the next five years... even as the business grows by another more than 25%"; "we have had discrete areas where we did reduce jobs by 30% or 40%, and most of those people offered jobs elsewhere"; but also the honest caveat "you don't uniquely benefit from AI", because competitors get it too. Third, the firm now has more capital than ideas and is willing to say so: "approximately $40 billion... effectively earning a 4% after-tax return"; buying back stock at three times tangible book is "simply value destructive"; "we can deploy all that $40 billion, $50 billion organically over the next five years"; and, for the first time, Dimon openly musing about a $10 to 20bn acquisition abroad. Fourth, competitors are now named individually: "Block, Citadel Securities, Revolut and Stripe... we study and track over a hundred of them", plus "a whole new set of competitors... based on blockchain". The risk list continued: the Iran conflict, "stickier inflation", private credit ("when you see one cockroach, there are probably more"), and in July 2026 "several risks are shifting below the surface like tectonic plates".

AWM in 2025. Revenue $24.1bn, pretax income $8.6bn, net income $6.5bn, pretax margin 36%, ROE 40%, flows $553bn, client assets $7.1tn, AUM $4.8tn, advisors 4,101, alternatives $560bn, number one in active ETFs. Section 5 goes deep.

4. What the filings say about where the firm is going

Put the ten themes together and the guidance for 2026 to 2030 is explicit enough to list.

Capital: $40 to 50bn deployed organically

Branches (500 more; tripling Alabama by 2030), bankers and advisors, technology (~$20bn a year), the Apple Card portfolio, the $10 to 20bn Security and Resiliency equity programme, and a possible $10 to 20bn acquisition abroad. Buybacks only when the stock is cheaper.

Returns: 17% ROTCE as a floor, not a target

The firm has earned 20% or more for three years. Management calls 17% "a helpful backstop" and refuses to raise it because a higher target would constrain investment. AWM's own targets (25% margin, 25% ROE) are treated the same way.

AI: fewer operations staff, more front office

Operations headcount down about 10% over five years while business volumes grow 25%. Front-office headcount continues to rise. Every analyst is trained in Python and prompt engineering. In AWM: SpectrumIQ automates trading; Connect Coach is expected to let advisors "expand client rosters by approximately 50%".

AWM: the "path to $10 trillion"

From $7.1tn of client assets in 2025 to $10tn, via five "2030+" ambitions: equities $2tn, fixed income $1.5tn, active ETFs $1tn, Global Private Bank $5tn of client assets (from about $3tn), alternatives $1tn. Investment spend in AWM: $1.0bn (2019), $2.3bn (2024), $2.7bn (2025, "largest ever").

Europe: consumer and private bank both growing

Chase into Germany in 2026, full product suite by 2028, then more countries. The International Private Bank aims to nearly double relationship managers (1,000 to about 1,900) with Europe named as "where the biggest opportunity is to grow market share".

Leadership: a handover within about three years

Dimon to become Executive Chairman; the CEO will be Doug Petno or Troy Rohrbaugh. Mary Erdoes stays as CEO of AWM. Expect continuity: the board has run the same process since 2000 and the two candidates have each run multiple businesses.

The implication for an Advisor applicant
The firm is telling you, in its own filings, that it is spending record sums to hire and train client advisors, that it expects new advisors to become productive faster than ever, that international (and specifically European) private banking is where it wants share, and that the hiring surge will "normalize later this decade". The 2027 intern class is being recruited at the peak of that build. Say so, with the numbers, and you will sound like someone who has read what the firm wrote rather than what its careers page says.

5. Drill-down: Asset & Wealth Management through Mary Erdoes' letters, 2021 to 2025

Mary Callahan Erdoes has run AWM since September 2009 (she joined J.P. Morgan in 1996 aged 29 to run fixed income, and ran the Private Bank from 2005 to 2009). Her annual letter is short, numerical and repetitive in a useful way: the same handful of measures every year, so the trend is legible. The job posting told you to read the 2021 letter. This section reads all five.

5.1 The 2021 letter: the four ingredients

The 2021 letter set out "four ingredients for future growth": (1) investment performance, (2) talent ("we retained nearly 95% of top senior talent and more than doubled the number of front office joiners to over 2,500... adding nearly 300 net new client advisors"), (3) new clients ("over the past five years, the number of net new GPB clients tripled"), (4) flows ("clients vote with their feet"; a record $389bn). It closed on "first-class business in a first-class way" and "our fiduciary duty to our clients above everything else".

Figure 2. Mary Erdoes' four 'ingredients for growth' from the 2021 letter, drawn as the loop they form. Each feeds the next; the Advisor sits in ingredients 2 and 3 and is measured on ingredient 4.

The four ingredients are not a list; they are a loop. Performance attracts talent and clients; talent wins clients; clients bring flows; flows fund investment in performance and talent. The Advisor sits in ingredients 2 and 3 and is measured on ingredient 4. Every subsequent letter can be read as a report on the loop.

5.4 The business model, past the marketing

Two halves, one engine

The 10-K defines them. Asset Management "offers multi-asset investment management solutions across equities, fixed income, alternatives and money market funds to institutional and retail investors." The Global Private Bank "provides retirement products and services, brokerage, custody, estate planning, lending, deposits and investment management to high net worth clients." Client assets are split roughly equally between them. Asset Management is a manufacturer: it employs about 1,300 investment professionals and 500 career research analysts (Erdoes: "treasured athletes, not want-to-be portfolio managers"), spends over $500m a year on research, covers 5,000 companies, and sells the output to pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurers, fund platforms and, crucially, the Private Bank. The Private Bank is a distributor and adviser: it has the client relationships and the balance sheet.

The tension in every bank-owned wealth manager is between selling the house product and advising impartially. J.P. Morgan handles it by making the fiduciary standard the stated rule ("we do not believe that any fiduciary manager should dictate choice or preferences") and by running a large third-party platform (850+ external alternatives managers; Morgan Money as an "open architecture" cash platform), while candidly noting on its EMEA site that it "prefers J.P. Morgan managed strategies" and earns more when they are used. Whether the balance is right is a legitimate question to ask in an interview; the firm's answer is that performance is the arbiter: 83% of its ten-year active fund assets beat the peer median.

Three mechanics worth understanding

  • Net interest income and rates. NII was "about 25% of our business" in 2022, rose to 40% in 2024 as rates went up, and fell back to 28% in 2025 as loan spreads narrowed. A private bank is partly a bank: when rates rise, its deposit franchise earns more.
  • Cash sorting. In 2022 and 2023 clients moved deposits into higher-yielding money-market funds; Private Bank deposits fell from $282bn to $233bn while AWM's money-market assets rose to nearly $1tn. Because J.P. Morgan owns both the bank and the funds, "nearly all net deposit migration stays within the firm". A pure asset manager or a pure bank would have lost that money.
  • Lending as strategy. Only 2% of Private Bank clients are credit-only. When a US Private Bank client also actively banks with the firm, assets and revenue are on average 65% higher. The firm is the number one mortgage lender to US households with over $10m of net worth and the largest sports-team lender. Losses are tiny (one basis point over ten years; a single client charge-off pushed 2025 to four basis points) because "we want two ways out". Lending is how a client becomes hard to lose.

5.6 The economics of an advisor, and why the firm keeps hiring

Figure 5. The advisor J-curve as Erdoes describes it. A new advisor costs money for two to three years, then compounds. The firm says AI-assisted training has made the curve steeper and shorter.

Erdoes has disclosed more about advisor economics than any peer. In 2022: "This isn't the brokerage business model... This is really an apprenticeship model business. This is where we don't buy books of business. We seed books of business... nearly half of our new advisors are homegrown... their productivity doubles after they've been here for a year... our breakevens are still in year three or earlier and our paybacks are in year five." In 2023: revenue per producer around $3m, and "47,000 resumes for those 400 slots for the analysts and associates... It's harder than college." In 2024: revenue per US client advisor up 28% and average client size up 23% since 2019; training hours per new hire up 5.6 times; 493,000 applications for 4,000 summer places firmwide. In 2025: "these 25-year-olds that are coming online... are just as productive as our 30- to 35-year-old mid-career hires"; productivity "up 3.4 times"; the home-grown share of new advisors rose from 33% to 48%. In the 2025 letter: advisors 2,500 to 4,100 in five years, revenue per banker up 15%, training cost per advisor down 27%, and the signal that hiring "normalizes later this decade".

The reason this matters to an intern: the firm has decided that growing its own advisors is cheaper and more loyal than poaching them, that AI shortens the J-curve, and that the years in which it will hire most are the next two or three. Every analyst is trained in Python (from 2023) and prompt engineering (from 2024); front-line advisors teach the classes; the aim is to get hires "JPMorganized... that's our secret sauce".

5.7 The AI thread inside AWM

  • SpectrumIQ has "automated nearly 75% of equity trading (saving clients about $4 billion in trading costs since inception) and almost 85% of foreign exchange trading"; from April 2026 it also runs proxy voting, making J.P. Morgan "the first major asset manager to fully disengage from external proxy advisors".
  • Connect Coach, the advisor "next best action" tool: 12,000 users across the Private Bank and US wealth management, "25 specialized AI agents", "1 million personalized AI-driven insights"; advisers find information "up to 95% faster"; the firm expects it to let advisors "expand client rosters by approximately 50%". Erdoes' framing: "a practical step toward having every advisor operate like our very best and every client feel like our only one."
  • Her view on advisors versus AI (February 2026): "The more you invest, the more the ecosystem creates a moat... if a client says, 'I don't like fixed income'... that's not the right answer, but that's where AI will go. And so, you need the combination of really smart AI and then really smart advisors." The advisor's edge is judgment and the willingness to disagree with the client.

7. What all of this means for the Advisor role and your application

Strategy documents are written for shareholders, but they describe the job you are applying for with unusual precision. Five conclusions.

1
The Advisor is the firm's chosen growth lever in AWM
Erdoes calls advisors "our biggest investment across Asset & Wealth Management". Headcount went from 2,500 to 4,101 in five years; the 2026 expense budget's first driver is "bankers, advisors and branches"; investment spend in AWM hit a record $2.7bn. The internship is the top of that funnel: 493,000 applications for 4,000 summer places firmwide, "harder than college".
2
The firm grows its own, and expects you to be productive early
"We seed books of business"; half of new advisors are home-grown; 25-year-olds now match mid-career hires; break-even in year three. The programme is an apprenticeship with a five-week global training school, front-line advisors as teachers, and exams funded. What they are selecting for is trainability plus drive, not finished knowledge.
3
Europe and the Middle East are where the share gains are expected
"Where the biggest opportunity is to grow market share [is] very much within the European franchise"; international relationship managers to nearly double; new UK offices; a Saudi regional headquarters. A London Advisor intern in 2027 is joining a business that has publicly committed to growing faster than its US parent.
4
The model is team, balance sheet and fiduciary, and the firm will test whether you understand it
"We don't sell products. We provide solutions." "Banking is not a commodity." "Two ways out." "No side accounting groups." "The more you know about me, how could I ever not be with you." These are operating rules, and the questions reported from the interview process ("how do banks make money?", "how would you assess a client's goals?", "tell us about a market article you read") test them directly.
5
AI changes the job but the firm has bet that it does not replace it
Connect Coach is expected to let advisors handle 50% more clients; SpectrumIQ automates the execution. The advisor's remaining edge, in Erdoes' own words, is judgment: the willingness to tell a client that what they want is "not the right answer". Curiosity about markets and comfort with technology are therefore both on the checklist.
