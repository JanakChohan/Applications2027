Part 3

Why the firm is built this way

Structure, collaboration, what customers actually buy, and the non-core side. Four questions answered from the firm's own words and numbers, with the costs stated as well as the benefits.

3.1 Why this structure: teams around clients, and three businesses under one roof

The team model. Every UHNW client gets a pod: an Advisor who owns the relationship, an Investor, a Capital Advisor, a Wealth Advisor and a Client Service Associate [S28][S44]. The theory has four parts. First, coverage of the whole balance sheet: one person cannot be expert in markets, credit and trusts, so the firm assigns specialists and makes one of them accountable. Second, replaceable seats: the firm, not the banker, owns the client, so when a banker leaves the client usually stays ("over 95% don't leave" [S318]). Third, attributable but team-based pay: salary plus a revenue-related bonus, not a commission grid, so specialists have no incentive to over-sell their own product and bankers have no incentive to hoard [S82][S118]. Fourth, seeded books: "This isn't the brokerage business model... This is really an apprenticeship model business. This is where we don't buy books of business. We seed books of business" [S317]. The costs, stated plainly: five people per client is expensive (compensation is 56% of AWM's costs [S82]); coordination takes time; and a banker who is not the "owner" of a client in the legal sense is paid less than a wirehouse adviser with a large book would be.

Three businesses under one roof. Why does the firm keep a consumer bank, a wholesale bank and a wealth manager together when each could be a large company alone? Dimon's answer, in every letter: "we strive to build enduring businesses, which rely on and benefit from one another... we are not a conglomerate. This structure helps generate our superior returns" [S311]. Barnum's in February 2026: "Our completeness, global presence, diversification and scale are not just attributes. They're our competitive advantages... The composition and connectivity of our business lines creates durability" [S322]. The specific mechanisms for the Private Bank are in 3.2. The cost of the structure, which the firm also admits: "Size can often be a tremendous business disadvantage because it frequently comes with the baggage of complexity, bureaucracy and complacency" [S311]; a controls burden that a boutique does not carry; and a compensation model that can lose top bankers to firms paying a grid.

Why the Private Bank sits with Asset Management, not with Chase. Both halves of AWM are fiduciary and fee-based; the Private Bank is the asset manager's most reliable distribution channel and the asset manager's research and products are the Private Bank's raw material. Erdoes: "asset management... can give you a 4 to 6 times multiple lift... Because it's a recurring revenue business that's very capital light" and "73% of our revenues come from recurring revenues" [S320]. Chase's own wealth business stays in Chase because its clients come through branches: "90% of new advised clients were referred by bankers" in the branches (Lake, 2025) [S321]. A shared platform, Wealth Management Solutions under Martin Marron, supplies both so that "no matter where you come into this institution, you get the best advice" [S320].

3.2 How the teams work together, in the firm's own wording

Read the firm's words precisely. Erdoes at Investor Day 2025 on where Private Bank clients come from: "We are every day investing in the companies that are taken public by the investment bank. We're helping the CFOs of those companies to invest their liquidity in [our] money market fund business. We're helping the CEOs... on their personal balance sheets, taking their 10b5-1 programs, sending them through Troy's trading desks... or we're taking all the great things in the commercial bank and the leads that they have... There are 2,000 private banking clients that walk into those [Chase] branches every day" [S320]. On the innovation economy built after Silicon Valley Bank failed: "when you look at all the cap tables in the world, 42% of them are in the innovation economy" [S320]. On the rule that makes it work: "that whole flywheel of people, they don't sit there and count the beans of, like, who gets credit for what... We do not tolerate that here at J.P. Morgan. We do not have side accounting groups that think about where those revenues get counted for. We are trying to win for the client" [S320]. The disclosed results: "over 95% of our top 50 AWM clients also work with our other lines of business" [S316]; about 3,000 Private Bank referrals to Chase Wealth Management in 2023; 60% of major US sports-team owners are Private Bank clients and the firm financed "10 of the last 15 major sports transactions" by combining Private Bank lending with the investment bank's municipal-finance and the commercial bank's real-estate teams [S320]; a private-credit joint venture between the CIB and the Private Bank [S329].

Where collaboration does not happen, and why: the information barrier. The investment bank knows things about companies that the Private Bank must not know until they are public. Deal information, trading positions and one client's confidential affairs never cross. The firm's public documents do not describe the wall procedures in detail (no bank's do), but the 10-K's conduct-risk framework and the industry's standard restricted-list and wall-crossing practice apply [S82] [Inferred]. The practical consequence for an Advisor: you can be told "the investment bank is running a sale for company X, meet the founder afterwards"; you cannot be told the price, and you must not trade or advise on the company's shares in the meantime. 3.3 What customers actually buy, and the counter-case

Who they are. Individuals and families, family offices, "institutional families" (the 23 Wall segment, 800+ families with an average net worth around $5bn), founders and entrepreneurs, financial-institution executives and partners, athletes and entertainers [S2][S38]. Thresholds are not published; third-party guides put the UK floor around £10m of investable assets and the internal UHNW segment around $25m and up; the firm's family-office page says family offices usually hold "at least a few hundred million" [S76][S28][S39]. The 2026 Global Family Office Report (333 offices, average net worth $1.6bn) shows what the top of the book looks like: about 40% public equities, 35% alternatives, under 15% bonds, 8% cash; 86% with no formal succession plan; geopolitics the top risk for 64%; 72% with no gold and 57% with no venture exposure [S240][S34].

What they are buying. Four things, in the firm's disclosed measures.

{{FIG:customer_view}}
  1. Durability rather than the best year. Twenty-two consecutive years of positive net client flows; over $1tn of net new client assets in 2024 and 2025 combined; 83% of ten-year active fund assets above the peer median [S316].
  2. The balance sheet. $266bn of Private Bank loans with charge-offs of one to four basis points; the largest sports-team and jumbo-mortgage lender in private banking; "the largest lending business on the private banking space" [S82][S91]. A boutique or a Swiss partnership cannot lend at this scale in multiple currencies.
  3. Access. The number one investment bank by fees for 15+ years [S304]; over $200bn of alternatives with 850+ external managers and the firm's own private funds; research from 500 career analysts and $500m a year of research spend [S25][S316][S320].
  4. Institutional safety. A 14.5% CET1 ratio and $1.5tn of liquidity [S83]; in the March 2023 bank crisis the firm opened 40,000 Private Bank accounts in ten weeks and new $100m+ clients arrived at "four a day" [S318].

Why they accept the terms. Fees at the top end are not the deciding factor: research on the wealth industry shows blended fees falling from around 1% below $1m of assets to 0.5% to 0.8% above $5m, with the UHNW segment resilient because the value comes from lending, structuring and access rather than a model portfolio [S204][S216]. Most UHNW families use two to four banks; exclusive single-firm relationships have halved from 39% in 2019 to 19% in 2025 [S200]. What the firm sells is the case for being the primary bank.

Your opinion goes here: the counter-case, with numbers from both sides

Against: (1) Fees. A £50m client at 60 to 100 basis points pays £300,000 to £500,000 a year; an index fund and a mortgage broker cost a fraction. (2) House product. The EMEA site states the firm "prefers J.P. Morgan managed strategies" and earns more when they are used [S195]. (3) Size. Onboarding is slow (source-of-wealth checks take weeks), and the firm itself says size brings "complexity, bureaucracy and complacency" [S311]. (4) Conduct history. The Epstein record shows a profitable client overriding compliance for years [S108]. (5) Scale in Europe. International client assets of about $750bn against UBS's $4.8tn; share "below 1%" in several markets [S316][S320].

For: (1) Performance: 83% of ten-year active assets beat the peer median, and flows of $553bn in one year are the market's verdict [S316]. (2) Open shelf: 850+ external alternatives managers, a fiduciary stance stated as "no fiduciary manager should dictate choice" [S320][S315]. (3) Lending and safety: losses of one to four basis points on $266bn; deposits and loans clients cannot get from an adviser without a balance sheet [S82]. (4) The referral engine nobody else has at this scale [S320]. (5) Growth: the fastest advisor build among majors (+64% in five years), funded by a 36% margin while Barclays' private bank profit fell and Julius Baer's fell 25% [S316][S163][S159].

Decide where you land and be able to say it in two sentences. A defensible position: "For a family above £10m with cross-border needs and a company or concentrated stock, the balance sheet and the investment bank are worth the fee; below that, or for a purely domestic family, Coutts or an independent adviser may serve better."

3.4 The non-core side: how big, how organised, how wired to the front line

How big. Of 318,512 employees at the end of 2025, about 50,000 sit in Corporate (technology, risk, legal, HR, audit, finance) and management has said about 80,000 work in operations and call centres across the lines of business, with a further 60,000 technologists [S300][S319]. Technology spend is about $18bn a year on the firm's framing, rising to about $19.8bn in 2026 [S82][S322]. In AWM, 29,722 employees serve $7.1tn of client assets; the marketing organisation alone is "approximately 500 team members" [S82][S48]. Per unit of assets, that is a leaner centre than most wealth managers: UBS Global Wealth runs at a 76% cost-to-income ratio against AWM's 64% overhead ratio [S155][S82].

How organised. The AWM centre as the firm presents it: Finance and Strategy (Ben Hesse), a Chief Information Officer and Chief Data and Analytics Officer (Mike Urciuoli), Global Operations (Julie Harris), Human Resources (Nelli Childs), Risk (Gregg Gunselman, reporting into the firmwide Chief Risk Officer), and Wealth Management Solutions (Martin Marron) as the advice-and-product platform shared with Chase [S320][S322][S6]. In the UK the postings show the connective seats: Market Managers and Business Managers alongside each market team; a Channel Marketing Lead for the UK, Nordics, Benelux, Middle East and emerging markets; a regulatory controls lead in Glasgow who builds "partnerships across the first and second lines of defense, Legal Entity management, Legal, Regulatory Management, and Control Management" [S49][S51].

How wired to the front line: the postings that prove it. The Advisor's job in this wiring is to know what the centre can do and to ask for it early.
