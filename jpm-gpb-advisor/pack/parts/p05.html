Part 5

The industry from zero

What private banking is, who the customers are, how firms differ, the vocabulary, and the forces reshaping the sector with three competing views on where it goes.

5.1 What the product is

Private banking is wealth management plus a bank. Wealth management is advice on how a person's money should be invested and organised around their goals. A private bank adds a banking licence and a balance sheet, so it can also take deposits, lend, and hold assets in custody. That combination is the product: one relationship that can invest £50m, lend £20m against it, hold cash in five currencies and set up the trust that passes it on [S217][S3].

Key terms: the family of firms
Retail banking: accounts, cards and small loans for everyone; product-led, high volume. Wealth management: advice and portfolios for people with money to invest; in the US often by registered investment advisers with no balance sheet. Private banking: wealth management for the wealthy, delivered by a bank that can also lend and hold deposits. Asset management: running pooled funds (mutual funds, ETFs, private-equity funds) for institutions and individuals; J.P. Morgan Asset Management is the Private Bank's sister business. Investment banking: advising companies on mergers and raising capital; it serves companies, the private bank serves the people who own or run them. Private equity: funds that buy whole companies; a client of the private bank as an investor, a counterparty when the client sells a business.

5.2 Who the customers are

Industry convention (Capgemini's definitions): mass affluent $100,000 to $1m of investable assets; "millionaires next door" $1m to $5m; mid-tier $5m to $30m; ultra-high-net-worth over $30m, about 1% of millionaires holding roughly a third of their wealth [S216]. Altrata counted 556,850 UHNW individuals in 2025, up 14%, holding $63.8tn, with about $26tn investable; 80% of North American UHNW individuals are self-made [S192]. J.P. Morgan's ladder runs from Chase Private Client at $150,000, through J.P. Morgan Private Client at $750,000, to the Private Bank at around $10m and the Institutional Wealth segment for the largest families [S219][S38]. A private bank client is therefore usually a founder, an executive, a financial professional, an inheritor or a family office, and increasingly a self-made one.

5.4 How a private bank makes money and how a banker is measured

A rule of thumb from listed pure-plays: a private bank earns roughly 70 to 100 basis points of revenue a year on client assets (Julius Baer's gross margin was 82 to 87 basis points in 2025 and early 2026), spends 60 to 70% of that on costs, and keeps the rest [S218]. AWM's numbers are richer because of lending and asset management: $24.1bn of revenue on $7.1tn of client assets is about 34 basis points at the segment level, at a 64% overhead ratio [S82]. A banker's book is measured on client assets, net new money (inflows minus outflows, excluding market moves), revenue on the book, return on assets in basis points, share of wallet, retention and referrals [S201]. Exclusive single-firm relationships have halved to 19% of UHNW households since 2019, and 35 to 50% of private-banking relationships are loss-making, which is why "primary bank" status is the whole game [S200].

5.5 The forces reshaping the sector

{{FIG:shift_map}}
  • The great wealth transfer. Cerulli estimates $124tn will pass between generations in the US through 2048, half of it from the 2% of households that are HNW or UHNW, and about $40tn first to widowed women [S190]. In the UK, £5.5tn between 2017 and 2047, over 70% of it property, with annual transfers rising from £69bn to £355bn [S191]. Heirs often switch advisers, so banks court the "rising generation" early; J.P. Morgan's family-office report finds over 75% of families now involve the next generation [S34].
  • Alternatives into private wealth. UHNW individuals hold about 22% in alternatives, family offices 35 to 46%, institutions about 55% [S203][S240]; semi-liquid and evergreen funds reached $644bn in 2025 [S202]. J.P. Morgan places about $2bn a month [S320]. The 2026 counter-current: private-credit redemption gates (Blue Owl halted quarterly redemptions in one fund in February; Cliffwater received requests for 14% against a 7% cap) and Dimon's warning that private credit "does not tend to have great transparency or rigorous valuation marks" [S257][S223].
  • AI. Connect Coach lets advisers find information "up to 95% faster" and is expected to let them "expand client rosters by approximately 50%"; the firm has cut headcount 30 to 40% in some operations units [S213][S336][S232]. Erdoes' position: "you need the combination of really smart AI and then really smart advisors" [S322].
  • Fee compression below, resilience above. 83% of US advisers expected to charge under 1% for $5m+ clients by 2026; robo fees of 0.25%; the UHNW end holds up because value comes from lending, structuring and access [S204]. Adviser supply is a constraint: 37% of US advisers plan to retire within ten years; the UK has about 31,000 advisers for 4.1m clients [S205].
  • Regulation. The Consumer Duty, MiFID II suitability, client-categorisation reform, and financial-crime enforcement: Part 6.
  • Tax migration. The UK's abolition of the non-dom regime from April 2025 and Gulf and Italian incentives pull mobile families; Henley's 2026 index scores the UK 68.3 against the UAE's 85.3, and British citizens are now about half of its applicants [S243][S244].
  • Talent movement. Bankers move with books; 2026 saw a Citi financial-institutions head join J.P. Morgan in London, UBS's UK head of private clients leave after 26 years, and Deutsche hire a Goldman managing director [S214][S377].

5.6 Three competing views on the next three to five years

View 1: the big universal banks win

Scale in technology, balance sheet and alternatives access compounds; AI raises adviser productivity 50%; the referral engine of a universal bank is unmatchable. Evidence: J.P. Morgan's $553bn of flows and 40% ROE against Julius Baer's falling profit; UBS's capital squeeze [S316][S159][S253]. Held by: J.P. Morgan's own management; Morgan Stanley's.

View 2: the independents and multi-family offices take the top

UHNW families distrust house product and want open architecture, discretion and lower fees; family offices grow their own investment teams; boutiques and Swiss partnerships win on intimacy. Evidence: exclusive relationships halving to 19%; Pictet and Lombard Odier's steady growth; the "prefers J.P. Morgan managed strategies" disclosure [S200][S160][S161][S195]. Held by: the boutique and independent-adviser industry.

View 3: technology and regulation flatten the middle

AI and targeted support push advice down-market; fee compression hollows out the £1m to £10m segment; the survivors are giant platforms at the top and digital services below; the human adviser's remaining edge is judgment and complexity. Evidence: FCA targeted support live from April 2026; 83% of advisers expecting sub-1% fees; Chase UK's 2.8m customers [S120][S204][S74]. Held by: fintech founders and some regulators.
