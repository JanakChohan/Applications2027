Part 2

The firm as a business, from the inside

The founding insight, how the money flows, the contracts the business is made of, the risk and control architecture, the strategy and mix, what being public means for research, the leadership's view of the future, recent moves, and the skeletons told straight.

2.1 The founding insight

The firm is the product of two lineages and one merger culture. J.P. Morgan descends from the New York merchant bank Drexel, Morgan & Co. (1871), which financed railways, US Steel and General Electric and acted as the de facto central bank in the panic of 1907; the firm's history page traces more than 1,200 predecessor institutions back to the Manhattan Company of 1799 [S78]. Chase descends from Chase National Bank and Chemical Bank, retail and commercial lenders. The two combined in 2000 (Chase Manhattan bought J.P. Morgan & Co.) and the modern firm was shaped by the 2004 merger with Bank One, which brought Jamie Dimon and his management method. Dimon's own background matters for the design: American Express, then chief financial officer and president at Commercial Credit and Travelers under Sandy Weill, president of Citigroup in 1998, then chairman and CEO of Bank One from 2000; he became president of JPMorgan Chase on the Bank One merger on 1 July 2004, CEO on 1 January 2006 and chairman a year later [S80]. Bank One's Chicago retail and card franchise is why Chase looks as it does [Inferred].

The phrase the firm still uses for its mission is J.P. "Jack" Morgan Jr.'s, from his 1933 Senate testimony: "the idea of doing only first class business, and that in a first class way, has been before our minds" [S78]. The firm's own mission statement, from the 2014 report How We Do Business: "We are devoted to building and maintaining the best and most respected financial services company in the world, serving our clients and growing our communities globally... A bank can't be a fair weather friend. We must be unquestionably safe and sound" [S81]. AWM's version, from Mary Erdoes: "Our North Star has never changed: to be the best in the industry for our clients, not the biggest" [S90].

Key term: fortress balance sheet
Holding more capital and liquidity than regulators require so that the bank is the safe place in a crisis and can lend or buy when others cannot. It is Business Principle 5 ("we will always maintain a fortress balance sheet") [S79]. At the end of 2025: CET1 capital $288bn (14.5% of risk-weighted assets), $1.5tn of cash and marketable securities [S83].

2.2 How the money flows

{{FIG:money_flow}}

Trace it from the customer. Consumers and small businesses give Chase deposits and card spending; companies and institutions pay the CIB for advice, capital raising, trading, payments and custody; wealthy families and institutions pay AWM fees on assets and hold deposits and loans with the Private Bank. In 2025 that produced $185.6bn of managed revenue, of which $95.4bn was net interest income (the spread between what the bank earns on loans and securities and pays on deposits) and $90.1bn was fee and trading revenue [S82][S83]. Expenses were $95.6bn, of which compensation was $54.5bn (57%) and technology, communications and equipment $11.0bn; management's broader "technology spend" framing is about $18bn [S82]. Credit costs were $14.2bn, tax $15.5bn, and net income $57.0bn [S82]. Return on tangible common equity was 20%.

By segment: Chase $76.0bn of revenue and $18.2bn of profit (32% ROE); the Commercial & Investment Bank $78.5bn and $27.8bn (18% ROE); AWM $24.1bn and $6.5bn (40% ROE); Corporate the rest [S82][S300]. For AWM the lines are: asset management fees $15.5bn; commissions and other fees $1.2bn; other income $0.6bn; net interest income $6.8bn; expense $15.3bn of which compensation $8.6bn (56%); pretax income $8.6bn, a 36% margin; net income $6.5bn. Asset Management earned $11.7bn (35% margin) and the Global Private Bank $12.4bn (37% margin) [S82]. The 10-K's own words on the Private Bank's growth: "higher management fees due to strong net inflows and higher average market levels, as well as higher brokerage commissions" and "higher net interest income, driven by higher average loans and deposits, largely offset by narrower spreads on loans"; on expense: "higher compensation, primarily higher revenue-related compensation and continued growth in private banking advisor teams" [S82].

Guidance for 2026, given in January and reaffirmed in February: net interest income about $103bn (later nudged to about $104.5bn), adjusted expense about $105bn, up about $9bn, with the first driver named as bankers, advisors and branches and "continued growth in JPMorgan Wealth Management and Private Bank client advisors" [S82][S86]. By July the expense guide was about $107.5bn [S340].

2.4 The risk and control architecture

The 10-K describes three lines of defence in its own words. The first line is "each LOB, Treasury and CIO... including their aligned Operations, Technology and Control Management", which "owns the risks, and identification of risks, associated with their respective activities and the design and execution of controls to manage those risks". The second is "the IRM function [Independent Risk Management], which is separate from the first line of defense and is responsible for independently measuring risk, as well as assessing and challenging the risk management activities of the first line of defense". The third is "Internal Audit... led by the General Auditor, who reports to the Audit Committee and administratively to the CEO" [S82]. The Chief Risk Officer, Ashley Bacon (in the role since 2013, joined in 1993), "reports to Chairman and CEO Jamie Dimon and the Risk Committee of the Board" [S96]. Conduct risk is defined as "the risk that any action or misconduct by an employee could lead to unfair client or customer outcomes", with annual Code affirmation and an anonymous hotline [S82].

What this produces in the Private Bank is a characteristic result: a loan book that is "highly collateralized" (Erdoes) with charge-offs of one to four basis points a year, and a business whose revenue is 73% recurring [S90][S320]. The rules an Advisor lives inside: "two ways out" on every loan (repayment from cash flow and from collateral), never opening an account before Know-Your-Customer is complete, suitability documented for every recommendation, and an information barrier between the Private Bank and the investment bank. The firm's rewrite of its controls after 2012 to 2014 is documented in How We Do Business, which was published to describe "actions taken to address recent legal and regulatory issues" [S81].

What has grown fastest is the client-facing front line and the fee businesses. Employees went from about 271,000 (2021) to 318,512 (2025) and 320,560 by June 2026; branches from about 4,800 to 5,083; Private Bank advisors from roughly 2,700 to 4,101 [S300][S302][S305][S316]. Dimon's letters describe the design as "enduring businesses, which rely on and benefit from one another... we are not a conglomerate" [S311]. Part 3 explains the mechanism.

2.6 Public company: what that means for research

JPMorgan Chase is listed on the New York Stock Exchange, so almost everything in this pack comes from documents the firm is legally required to publish: the annual 10-K, quarterly earnings releases, proxy statements, and the shareholder letters it chooses to write. Segment-level numbers for AWM are audited and comparable year to year. What is not published: the Private Bank's own P&L below the segment line, UK headcount, fee schedules, and compensation. For those the pack uses postings, regulator registers, trade press and forums, and says so. In the UK the relevant entities are on the FCA register: JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. London Branch (FRN 124491), J.P. Morgan Securities plc (FRN 155240), J.P. Morgan SE London Branch (FRN 755066; Companies House FC035501), J.P. Morgan Europe Limited (FRN 124579; the entity behind Chase UK) and JPMorgan Asset Management (UK) Limited (FRN 119337); all registered at 25 Bank Street, Canary Wharf, with the Private Bank's client address at 60 Victoria Embankment [S70][S71][S73][S74]. The EU hub is J.P. Morgan SE in Frankfurt (ECB and BaFin supervised); the Swiss hub is J.P. Morgan (Suisse) SA in Geneva (FINMA) [S72][S70].

2.7 The leadership's view of the future, and what the hiring says

In the firm's own words: deploy "$40 billion, $50 billion organically over the next five years" (Dimon, February 2026) [S322]; a possible "$10 [billion] or $20 billion" acquisition "in the next couple years" (May 2026) [S230]; Chase into Germany (May 2026) and at least three more European countries by 2030 (FT report) [S228][S229]; AI that has already removed "30% to 40%" of headcount in some units while "you don't uniquely benefit from AI" [S232]; and in AWM a "path to $10 trillion" of client assets with Global Private Bank client assets going from about $3tn to $5tn, advisor hiring continuing and then "normalizing later this decade" [S320][S316]. The hiring pattern confirms it: the first driver of 2026 expense growth is "bankers, advisors and branches"; UK Private Bank postings in September 2026 include five banker seats, two lending advisors and two analyst programmes; the International Private Bank wants to go from about 1,000 relationship managers to about 1,900 [S86][S40]-[S47][S65]. The author's inference: the firm is spending the surplus capital of a record year on people and platforms, and the International Private Bank in London is one of the named destinations. [Inferred]
