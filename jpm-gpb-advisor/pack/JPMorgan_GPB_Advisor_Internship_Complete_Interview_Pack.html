The firm in one line
JPMorgan Chase is the largest bank in the world by market value: $185.6bn of revenue and $57.0bn of profit in 2025, about 320,000 people, 66 countries, run since 2005 by Jamie Dimon [S300]. Its three businesses are Chase (US consumers), the Commercial & Investment Bank (companies and institutions) and Asset & Wealth Management (AWM), where the Private Bank sits. AWM earned $24.1bn of revenue and a 40% return on equity in 2025; the Private Bank is half of it [S300].
3
The mechanism that makes it different
Four things, each with a number. The referral engine: the investment bank sells a founder's company, the founder becomes a Private Bank client; "2,000 private banking clients walk into the Chase branches every day"; over 95% of AWM's top 50 clients use other parts of the firm [S320][S316]. The balance sheet: $266bn of Private Bank loans, 97% collateralised, losses of one basis point a year [S300][S318][S319]. The apprenticeship: "we seed books of business, we don't buy them"; half of advisors are home-grown; advisors went from 2,500 to 4,101 in five years [S317][S320][S316]. ⟦p1a⟧1.1 The posting, line by line

The posting is short and non-technical, which is itself a signal: the firm is hiring for trainability and disposition, not for finished skills. Each line below is quoted, then decoded [S1].

"Our advisors help clients achieve their financial goals by delivering the right solutions and services across our industry leading investments, credit, banking and Trust & Estates practices."

What it involves. Four product areas, one relationship. "Investments" means portfolios run by the Investor; "credit" means lending against portfolios, property and other assets run by the Capital Advisor; "banking" means deposits, payments and foreign exchange; "Trust & Estates" means the structures through which wealth passes between generations, run by the Wealth Advisor [S3][S4][S5]. Who. The client family and the four specialist seats. Good looks like. A client who uses all four, because the firm's own disclosure is that when a US Private Bank client also actively banks with it, assets and revenue are on average 65% higher, and that only 2% of clients are credit-only [S319]. Structural fact. The Private Bank is deliberately a bank, not just an adviser: the balance sheet is part of the product.

Key term: Trust & Estates
A trust is a legal arrangement in which one party (the trustee) holds assets for others (the beneficiaries) under rules set by the person who created it. An estate is everything a person owns at death. "Trust and estate planning" is the work of arranging who gets what, when, and with how much tax, before the question becomes urgent.

"This individualized attention supports our clients' unique goals and helps build, preserve and manage their wealth over time."

What it involves. The firm's planning framework has four "lenses": Liquidity (cash for the near term), Lifestyle (spending), Legacy (heirs and philanthropy) and Growth (long-term appreciation) [S32]. "Individualized" means there is no model portfolio pushed at everyone; the Investor builds from the family's goals. Good looks like. A written plan the family recognises as theirs. Structural fact. Advice is the product; the fee is charged on assets, not per transaction, so the firm earns when the relationship lasts.

"As a future Advisor, you'll work with our team of advisors and specialists to bring in new clients and to serve existing ones."

What it involves. This is the sentence that defines the seat. Two channels: bringing in new clients (prospecting, referrals, pitching) and serving existing ones (reviews, problems, life events). The UK Client Advisor posting at Executive Director level lists the same two duties in adult form: "generate business results and acquire new assets" and lead "new client acquisition and retention" [S13]. Who. Bankers on your team, the shared specialists, and the internal referral sources: the investment bank, the commercial bank and the Chase branches [S320]. Good looks like. Net new assets and new clients on the team's scorecard. Structural fact. "Team of advisors and specialists" confirms the integrated team model rather than a lone-banker model (Figure on team versus alternative in Part 3).

Figure 2. The role's value chain from internal demand to outcome. The intern touches every step but owns none of them yet.

"We deliver highly customized and comprehensive solutions to help protect, manage and grow wealth."

What it involves. "Comprehensive" is code for the whole balance sheet: assets, liabilities, entities, residences, family. "Protect" includes diversification away from the single stock a founder holds, currency, and, increasingly, cyber advice, which the EMEA wealth planning page lists explicitly [S5]. Structural fact. The firm competes on breadth. A boutique cannot lend, custody, execute, structure and advise in ten currencies from one relationship.

"No client has identical goals, so understanding their unique needs and tailoring our approach to exceed expectations requires work across teams and the firm to provide first-class service."

What it involves. Discovery conversations, then coordination. "Across teams and the firm" points at the referral engine and at the specialists. "First-class" is a quotation from J.P. Morgan Jr.'s 1933 Senate testimony ("first-class business in a first-class way") that the firm still uses [S324]. Good looks like. The Advisor knows enough of every discipline to ask the right specialist the right question, and owns the answer that goes back.

"Our nine-week program kicks off with a week of induction, where you'll have training and orientation to our businesses and gain the technical and practical knowledge you'll need to start contributing to our teams."

What it involves. One week of classroom: the firm's businesses, compliance and conduct, systems, the team model, products. Then eight weeks placed with a team. The Hong Kong version of the same programme uses identical wording, which suggests a global template [S15]. Structural fact. The programme is a global pipeline; the full-time Advisor Analyst programme that follows opens with a five-week global training school [S17].

"Top performers may receive a full-time offer at the end of the summer."

What it involves. The internship is a nine-week interview. Conversion rates are not published; Part 9 gives what is reported. Structural fact. Erdoes says half of new advisors are grown from within and that "25-year-olds" now match mid-career hires in productivity, so the intern pipeline is the firm's preferred source of advisors, not an afterthought [S320].

Responsibility: "Work with our teams to understand client needs and examine market activities to develop and execute strategies that help clients meet their financial goals."

What it involves. Reading the house view (Eye on the Market, the Outlook, weekly Top Market Takeaways [S35][S36]), translating it for a specific family, and helping the Investor turn it into portfolio changes. Good looks like. You can explain why a rise in UK gilt yields matters to a client with a £20m interest-only mortgage. Structural fact. The house view is centralised (the Global Investment Strategy team, co-led from London by Grace Peters [S37]); the Advisor personalises it.

Responsibility: "Work with a team of bankers, investment specialists, wealth advisors, and lending specialists. Throughout the summer, you'll learn about our business and our clients while building your own expertise."

What it involves. The four seats named in the firm's own words: bankers (Advisors), investment specialists (Investors), wealth advisors, lending specialists (Capital Advisors) [S1][S28]. Structural fact. The seat you are applying for is "banker". The Investment Solutions internship, advertised separately in London, is the route into the specialist seats [S16].

Responsibility: "Collaborate with various teams, including but not limited to market strategy, equity, and alternative investment teams to conduct research and analysis, develop products and respond to client needs as part of a Solutions Team."

What it involves. The project. In practice a research or proposal deliverable built with the strategy, equities and alternatives specialists and presented to senior bankers. "Alternative investment teams" is a pointer to the firm's largest differentiator: over $200bn of alternatives in the Private Bank, about $2bn a month placed, 850+ external managers on the platform [S25][S320]. Good looks like. A deliverable a banker could use in front of a client without rewriting it.

Key term: alternatives
Investments that are not listed shares, bonds or cash: private equity (buying whole companies), private credit (lending to companies outside the bond market), hedge funds, real estate, infrastructure. Illiquid (your money is locked up for years), higher fee, and sold on returns and diversification.

Responsibility: "Continuously learn through one-on-one mentorship, learning sessions and ongoing events to grow your professional network."

What it involves. A mentor, a speaker series, and events. Erdoes' 2025 letter says the Private Bank runs "850+ events" for advisor engagement in a year [S316]. Good looks like. Senior people remember you, because you asked one good question and followed up.

⟦p1f⟧1.6 The compliance and control dimension

A private banker is a regulated person doing a regulated activity for clients who are, in law, mostly "retail clients" with the highest protection. The bright lines an intern must know unprompted:

  • Confidentiality. Client information is never discussed outside the team, including with friends, and never used for personal benefit. The posting makes "discretion" a hiring criterion [S1].
  • Know Your Customer and source of wealth. Before a client is onboarded the bank must document who they are and how they made their money; before a deposit is accepted, where that money came from. Politically exposed persons and complex structures trigger enhanced checks [S199]. No account is opened "now, documents later".
  • Suitability. Under MiFID II (in force 3 January 2018) and the FCA rules, an investment recommendation must fit the client's knowledge, financial situation and objectives, and the firm must record why [S215]. An intern never gives investment advice to a client; the Advisor and Investor do, and they are qualified and certified to.
  • Consumer Duty. Since 31 July 2023 UK firms must deliver "good outcomes" for retail clients across products, price and value, understanding and support, and avoid "foreseeable harm" [S206]. This is why proposals are written in plain language and fees are shown.
  • Information barriers and market abuse. The Private Bank sits next to an investment bank. Non-public information about a deal or an issuer must not cross to the Private Bank, and if it does by accident, the rule is stop, tell compliance, do not act or repeat. Part 11 has the decision tree.
  • Credit discipline. Never promise a loan; the credit committee decides. Erdoes: "we want two ways out" [S318].
  • Conduct rules. Under the Senior Managers and Certification Regime every employee is bound by conduct rules (act with integrity; act with due skill, care and diligence; be open and cooperative with regulators; pay due regard to customers' interests; observe proper standards of market conduct). Part 6 gives the dates.
Say this in the interview, unprompted if the topic is near
"I'd assume everything I see is confidential, that I never open anything without the KYC being complete, and that if I hear something about a listed company that isn't public I stop and tell compliance before I do anything else."

⟦p2a⟧2.1 The founding insight

The firm is the product of two lineages and one merger culture. J.P. Morgan descends from the New York merchant bank Drexel, Morgan & Co. Chase descends from Chase National Bank and Chemical Bank, retail and commercial lenders. The two combined in 2000 (Chase Manhattan bought J.P. Morgan & Co.) and the modern firm was shaped by the 2004 merger with Bank One, which brought Jamie Dimon and his management method. Dimon's own background matters for the design: American Express, then chief financial officer and president at Commercial Credit and Travelers under Sandy Weill, president of Citigroup in 1998, then chairman and CEO of Bank One from 2000; he became president of JPMorgan Chase on the Bank One merger on 1 July 2004, CEO on 1 January 2006 and chairman a year later [S80]. ⟦p2d⟧2.4 The risk and control architecture

The 10-K describes three lines of defence in its own words. Why the firm is built this way

Structure, collaboration, what customers actually buy, and the non-core side. Four questions answered from the firm's own words and numbers, with the costs stated as well as the benefits.

⟦p3a⟧3.1 Why this structure: teams around clients, and three businesses under one roof

The team model. Every UHNW client gets a pod: an Advisor who owns the relationship, an Investor, a Capital Advisor, a Wealth Advisor and a Client Service Associate [S28][S44]. The theory has four parts. First, coverage of the whole balance sheet: one person cannot be expert in markets, credit and trusts, so the firm assigns specialists and makes one of them accountable. Second, replaceable seats: the firm, not the banker, owns the client, so when a banker leaves the client usually stays ("over 95% don't leave" [S318]). Third, attributable but team-based pay: salary plus a revenue-related bonus, not a commission grid, so specialists have no incentive to over-sell their own product and bankers have no incentive to hoard [S82][S118]. Fourth, seeded books: "This isn't the brokerage business model... This is really an apprenticeship model business. This is where we don't buy books of business. We seed books of business" [S317]. The costs, stated plainly: five people per client is expensive (compensation is 56% of AWM's costs [S82]); coordination takes time; and a banker who is not the "owner" of a client in the legal sense is paid less than a wirehouse adviser with a large book would be.

Three businesses under one roof. Why does the firm keep a consumer bank, a wholesale bank and a wealth manager together when each could be a large company alone? Dimon's answer, in every letter: "we strive to build enduring businesses, which rely on and benefit from one another... we are not a conglomerate. This structure helps generate our superior returns" [S311]. Barnum's in February 2026: "Our completeness, global presence, diversification and scale are not just attributes. They're our competitive advantages... The composition and connectivity of our business lines creates durability" [S322]. The specific mechanisms for the Private Bank are in 3.2. The cost of the structure, which the firm also admits: "Size can often be a tremendous business disadvantage because it frequently comes with the baggage of complexity, bureaucracy and complacency" [S311]; a controls burden that a boutique does not carry; and a compensation model that can lose top bankers to firms paying a grid.

Why the Private Bank sits with Asset Management, not with Chase. ⟦p5a⟧5.1 What the product is

Private banking is wealth management plus a bank. Wealth management is advice on how a person's money should be invested and organised around their goals. A private bank adds a banking licence and a balance sheet, so it can also take deposits, lend, and hold assets in custody. That combination is the product: one relationship that can invest £50m, lend £20m against it, hold cash in five currencies and set up the trust that passes it on [S217][S3].

Key terms: the family of firms
Retail banking: accounts, cards and small loans for everyone; product-led, high volume. Wealth management: advice and portfolios for people with money to invest; in the US often by registered investment advisers with no balance sheet. Private banking: wealth management for the wealthy, delivered by a bank that can also lend and hold deposits. Asset management: running pooled funds (mutual funds, ETFs, private-equity funds) for institutions and individuals; J.P. Morgan Asset Management is the Private Bank's sister business. Investment banking: advising companies on mergers and raising capital; it serves companies, the private bank serves the people who own or run them. Private equity: funds that buy whole companies; a client of the private bank as an investor, a counterparty when the client sells a business.

⟦p5b⟧5.2 Who the customers are

Industry convention (Capgemini's definitions): mass affluent $100,000 to $1m of investable assets; "millionaires next door" $1m to $5m; mid-tier $5m to $30m; ultra-high-net-worth over $30m, about 1% of millionaires holding roughly a third of their wealth [S216]. Altrata counted 556,850 UHNW individuals in 2025, up 14%, holding $63.8tn, with about $26tn investable; 80% of North American UHNW individuals are self-made [S192]. J.P. Morgan's ladder runs from Chase Private Client at $150,000, through J.P. Morgan Private Client at $750,000, to the Private Bank at around $10m and the Institutional Wealth segment for the largest families [S219][S38]. A private bank client is therefore usually a founder, an executive, a financial professional, an inheritor or a family office, and increasingly a self-made one.

⟦p5d⟧5.4 How a private bank makes money and how a banker is measured

A rule of thumb from listed pure-plays: a private bank earns roughly 70 to 100 basis points of revenue a year on client assets (Julius Baer's gross margin was 82 to 87 basis points in 2025 and early 2026), spends 60 to 70% of that on costs, and keeps the rest [S218]. AWM's numbers are richer because of lending and asset management: $24.1bn of revenue on $7.1tn of client assets is about 34 basis points at the segment level, at a 64% overhead ratio [S82]. A banker's book is measured on client assets, net new money (inflows minus outflows, excluding market moves), revenue on the book, return on assets in basis points, share of wallet, retention and referrals [S201]. Exclusive single-firm relationships have halved to 19% of UHNW households since 2019, and 35 to 50% of private-banking relationships are loss-making, which is why "primary bank" status is the whole game [S200].

⟦p6a⟧6.1 The arc in one paragraph

Until 1986 the City largely regulated itself. The Financial Services Act 1986 created a statutory framework. The Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA) swept it into one regulator, the FSA, from 1 December 2001, using the concept of "regulated activities": you need permission to advise on investments, manage them, deal, arrange or take deposits. After the 2008 crisis the Financial Services Act 2012 split the FSA into the FCA (conduct) and the PRA (prudential, inside the Bank of England) on 1 April 2013. Since then the layers a private banker lives under are: the Retail Distribution Review (2012: how you are paid and qualified), MiFID II (2018: how you assess, disclose, record and govern), the Senior Managers and Certification Regime (2016 and 2019: personal accountability), the Consumer Duty (2023 and 2024: outcomes for retail clients), and a 2025 to 2027 "growth" wave: targeted support, client re-categorisation, a new disclosure regime, a slimmed-down accountability regime and Basel 3.1 [S141][S144][S146][S120][S121][S126].

⟦p6e⟧6.5 Qualifications

To advise on retail investment products a UK adviser needs a qualification on the FCA's Training and Competence tables that "fully meets requirement from 31 December 2012": the CISI Level 4 Investment Advice Diploma is the standard route; the CFA UK Investment Management Certificate covers securities advice and managing investments but not, on its own, retail investment products [S141][S142]. The CISI Level 7 Diploma in Wealth Management is the Chartered Wealth Manager qualification [S143]. Retail investment advisers also need an annual Statement of Professional Standing and 35 hours of continuing professional development. J.P. Morgan's UK banker postings prefer a "certificate in Investment Management level 4" and treat the CFA as "a plus"; whether the analyst programme funds the CISI exams could not be confirmed from a primary source [S44][S40].

⟦p6f⟧6.6 Cross-border: why a London banker cannot freely advise an EU client

The UK's MiFID passport ended on 31 December 2020. A UK firm is now a "third-country firm" and may serve an EU-resident client only under a member state's national regime or "reverse solicitation", where the client initiated the service "at its own exclusive initiative". ESMA warned on 13 January 2021 that "where a third-country firm solicits clients or promotes investment services in the Union, it should not be deemed a service provided at the client's exclusive initiative" [S148]. In practice, EU-resident clients are booked and advised through J.P. Morgan SE (Frankfurt, created 24 January 2022, with branches in Dublin, Luxembourg, Paris, Madrid and Milan and a London branch "to service certain aspects of the firm's private banking business"), and Swiss clients through J.P. Morgan (Suisse) SA under FINMA [S147][S70]. This is why the EMEA structure has three regions and why the firm stresses its "uniform global model".

⟦p11f⟧11.6 The CV grill

How a three-levels-deep attack works. Level one: "Tell me about X on your CV." Level two: "What specifically did you do?" Level three: "What would have happened if you hadn't?" or "What number did that change?" Most candidates survive level one and collapse at level three because the bullet was a group's work or a description of activity rather than of a result.

AttackFrame-holding answer
1. "This bullet says 'led'. How many people, and what did you decide?"Give the number and one decision you made alone, with its consequence. If you did not lead, say "coordinated" and own that.
2. "What was the result, in a number?"One number, its baseline, and the period. If there is no number, say what changed that someone else could see.
3. "What would you do differently?"One specific thing, and what it would have improved. Never "nothing".
4. "Why did you leave / stop / not continue?"The true reason in one sentence, then what you took from it. No criticism of the previous organisation.
5. "This looks like a group project. What was yours?"Name your piece, its interface with others' pieces, and how you resolved one conflict in it.
6. "Your degree isn't finance. Why should we trust you with markets?""You shouldn't yet; that's what the training is for. What I bring is [curiosity, evidence]. And here is what I've read in the last month and what I think about it."
7. "You've applied to other banks.""Yes, to [n]. Here is what is specific about this seat that the others don't have" (one mechanism from Part 12).
8. "Tell me something not on your CV."Prepared: one thing that shows persistence or trust-building, with a number.

The four-line drill for every CV bullet. For each bullet write: (1) what I personally did (verbs); (2) the number that changed and its baseline; (3) the hardest moment and what I decided; (4) what I would do differently. If you cannot fill all four lines, rewrite the bullet or remove it.

Glossary

Sixty-plus terms, each defined the way you would say it to a client.
Advisor (J.P. Morgan)
The client-facing relationship banker; elsewhere "private banker" or "relationship manager". The seat this internship leads to.
Advisory mandate
The bank recommends; the client approves each decision.
Alternatives
Investments outside listed shares, bonds and cash: private equity, private credit, hedge funds, real estate, infrastructure. Illiquid, higher fee.
AML
Anti-money-laundering: the rules and checks that stop criminal money entering the bank.
Appropriateness test
Under MiFID II, checking that a client understands a complex product bought without advice.
Asset management fees
Fees charged as a percentage of assets managed or advised; AWM's largest revenue line ($15.5bn in 2025).
AUM / client assets
Assets under management are money the firm actively invests; client assets add money it holds, advises on or custodies. Client assets is the broader, headline number.
AWM
Asset & Wealth Management, the JPMorgan Chase segment containing Asset Management and the Global Private Bank.
Basel 3.1
The final set of international bank capital rules; live in the UK from 1 January 2027.
Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of one percent. Fees, spreads and returns are quoted in it.
Brokerage / execution-only
The client instructs, the bank executes, no advice is given.
Capital Advisor
The Private Bank's lending specialist: mortgages, securities-based loans, specialty lending.
CCB
Consumer & Community Banking: Chase, the US retail bank, which also houses Chase's own wealth business and Chase UK.
CET1 ratio
Common Equity Tier 1 capital divided by risk-weighted assets; the core measure of a bank's safety. JPMorgan: 14.5% at end-2025.
CIB
Commercial & Investment Bank: the wholesale bank serving companies and institutions, including Commercial Banking since 2024.
CISI
Chartered Institute for Securities & Investment; awards the Level 4 Investment Advice Diploma and the Level 7 Chartered Wealth Manager qualification.
Client Service Associate
The team member who runs onboarding, documents, payments and meeting materials.
Connect Coach
J.P. Morgan's AI assistant for advisors: next-best-action prompts, meeting prep, talking points; 12,000 users.
Consumer Duty
FCA rules (in force 31 July 2023) requiring firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers and avoid foreseeable harm.
Corporate trustee
A company (here a J.P. Morgan trust company) acting as trustee of a family's trust.
Discretionary mandate
The bank makes and executes investment decisions within parameters the client sets.
Drawdown fund
A private fund that calls committed capital over several years and returns it over about ten.
Elective professional client
A client who opts out of retail protections; the FCA proposes a £10m investable-assets route (CP25/36).
Evergreen fund
A private-markets fund invested up front with limited periodic redemptions; "semi-liquid".
Eye on the Market
Michael Cembalest's research note for the Private Bank, published since 2005.
Family office
A private company a very wealthy family sets up to manage its affairs; single- or multi-family.
FCA / PRA
The UK conduct regulator and the prudential regulator (part of the Bank of England), created 1 April 2013.
Fiduciary
A duty to act in the client's interest ahead of your own; AWM's "north star".
FIG regime
The four-year foreign income and gains exemption that replaced the non-dom regime from 6 April 2025.
Fortress balance sheet
Holding more capital and liquidity than required; Business Principle 5.
FSCS
The UK deposit guarantee scheme; J.P. Morgan SE London Branch participates.
Global Private Bank (GPB)
J.P. Morgan's business serving wealthy families worldwide; half of AWM's revenue.
HNW / UHNW
High-net-worth (over $1m investable by convention) and ultra-high-net-worth (over $30m).
HireVue
The recorded video interview platform; three to five questions, two-minute answers, one retry.
Information barrier
The controls that stop non-public information crossing between the investment bank and the private bank.
Inheritance tax (IHT)
UK tax on estates; now residence-based for worldwide assets after ten of twenty years' residence.
International Private Bank (IPB)
The non-US Private Bank, run from London; about 1,000 relationship managers.
Investor (J.P. Morgan)
The investment specialist who builds and runs client portfolios and leads the market conversation.
KYC
Know Your Customer: verifying identity and source of wealth before and during a relationship.
Liquidity event
The moment paper wealth becomes cash: a sale, an IPO, an inheritance.
Lombard loan
A loan secured on a portfolio of securities; also "securities-based lending".
Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions (LTCMA)
J.P. Morgan Asset Management's annual 10-to-15-year return forecasts used for strategic allocation.
Loan-to-value (LTV)
The loan as a share of the collateral's value; the lower, the safer.
Managed revenue
JPMorgan's non-GAAP revenue basis that grosses up tax-exempt income; used for all segment figures.
Margin call
A demand for more collateral or repayment when the value of pledged securities falls.
Market abuse
Insider dealing, unlawful disclosure or manipulation; a civil and criminal offence.
Market Manager
The commercial lead of a Private Bank market team; the Lending Advisor's counterpart.
MiFID II
EU and UK investment-services rules in force 3 January 2018: suitability, costs disclosure, product governance, call recording.
Net interest income (NII)
Interest earned on loans and securities minus interest paid on deposits; 28% of AWM revenue in 2025.
Net new assets / flows
Money in minus money out, excluding market moves; AWM's headline growth measure ($553bn in 2025).
Non-dom
The former UK status for residents domiciled abroad; abolished from 6 April 2025.
Open architecture
Offering external managers' products, not only the firm's own.
PEP
Politically exposed person; triggers enhanced due diligence.

Pretax income divided by revenue; AWM 36% and the Private Bank 37% in 2025.
Private credit
Loans made by funds rather than banks; a $1.8tn market under stress in 2026.
RDR
The Retail Distribution Review (31 December 2012): commission ban, adviser charging, Level 4 qualification.
ROTCE / ROE
Return on tangible common equity (the firm's headline measure, 20% in 2025) and return on equity (AWM 40%).
Share of wallet
The fraction of a family's total wealth held with the firm; the aim is to be the primary bank.

The MiFID II requirement that a recommendation fits the client's knowledge, finances and objectives, with the reasoning recorded.

The FCA's new regime (6 April 2026) for ready-made suggestions to groups of consumers without a personal recommendation.
