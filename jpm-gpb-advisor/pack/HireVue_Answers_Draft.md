## Answer 1: "What attracts you to J.P. Morgan, and specifically, an opportunity in Global Private Bank?"

*Preview (15 seconds)*

Thank you for taking the time to watch this. I'll give three reasons. First, a mechanism: the way clients arrive at J.P. Morgan's Private Bank through the investment bank and the rule that nobody counts the referral credit. Second, the way advisors are made here, seeded rather than bought, which is how I built my own first client base from zero. Third, why the London seat specifically, after a summer on the other side of the industry at Pictet.

*Reason one: the mechanism*

Most private banks compete for the same families with the same products. J.P. Morgan's is wired differently. The investment bank that sells a founder's company is the bank that meets the founder first, and Mary Erdoes has told shareholders that the firm has "no side accounting groups" deciding who gets credit. *Reason two: how advisors are made*

The firm says it seeds books of business rather than buying them, that half its advisors are grown internally, and that it has gone from 2,500 to 4,100 advisors in five years. I know what seeding a book feels like at a small scale: I built an online coaching business from nothing to more than fifty clients by posting every day for two years, and on a shop floor I lifted average transaction value by 22% by learning to hear the objection before answering it. The Advisor seat is that work with far more at stake.

*Reason three: London, now*

This summer at Pictet I worked on onboarding for institutional clients: due diligence questionnaires routed across twenty teams, prime brokerage documentation for a UCITS ETF. I saw a first-class asset manager, and I saw what it cannot do: it has no investment bank and does not lend like a bank. J.P. *Factor one: energy, inflation and rates*

The disruption to the Strait of Hormuz this year produced what the World Bank called the largest oil market shock in history, and it has kept inflation sticky. In July the Bank of England held at 3.75% but three members voted to hike, and ten-year gilts are above 5.3%, the highest since 2007. For a client that matters twice. *Factor two: AI concentration*

I ran a simulated twenty-million-dollar US equity book at Goldman's spring week and learned the hard way to cut a position when the thesis broke. For a founder or executive whose wealth is mostly one technology stock, the question is not whether AI is a bubble but how much of their family's plan survives a 40% drawdown, and how to diversify without triggering tax.

*Factor three: private credit liquidity*

This year several semi-liquid private credit funds had to gate redemptions: requests of 14% against a 7% cap at one manager. At Pictet I saw how much documentation sits behind a fund's liquidity terms. Clients who bought these funds for yield now need someone to explain that the illiquidity premium is only earned by money that can genuinely be locked up.

## Answer 3: "Please share an example of a time when you had to work as part of a team to accomplish an important goal, describing the situation, your actions, and the outcome."

*Preview (15 seconds)*

Thank you for watching. My example is from this summer at Pictet Asset Management, where a team of relationship managers and twenty specialist teams needed to answer institutional clients' due diligence questionnaires faster, and I built the tool that made it a team process rather than a chase.

*Situation*

Institutional clients send due diligence questionnaires before they invest: hundreds of questions on risk, operations, compliance, ESG and more. Each question belongs to a different specialist team, and the relationship managers were tracking who owed what in a spreadsheet of about four hundred lines. Answers were late, and the people answering them were not the people the client would blame.

*My actions*

I did three things. First, I listened before I built: I sat with the relationship managers and with several of the specialist teams to understand where the delays actually were, which turned out to be the hand-offs, not the answers. Second, I built an internal portal that ingests a questionnaire and automatically assigns each question to the right team of the twenty, with a single-click reconfirmation so a team could push a question back if it was misrouted rather than leaving it. Third, I made the routing rules something the teams owned: I ran short sessions with each of them so the assignment logic was theirs, not mine.

*Outcome*

The four-hundred-line tracker was retired, response latency for the relationship managers came down, and, more to the point, the argument about whose question it was disappeared because the system decided. It was adopted while I was still there.

*What I learned*

The goal was a team goal, but the blocker was that nobody owned the join between teams. Owning the join is most of the job. I noticed later that J.P. Morgan's Private Bank describes its own culture the same way: no side accounting for who gets credit, one owner for the client.

*Close*

That is the habit I would bring to an advisor team: own the hand-off, not just the task.

(About 320 words; the "what I learned" paragraph can be shortened to one sentence if needed.)
